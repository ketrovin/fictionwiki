# Chapter 4: The Arithmetic of Hardwood

---

### Part I: The Cold Math of the 45th Parallel

A man who does not understand the arithmetic of winter in eastern Canada will spend his summer bragging about his rifles and his autumn freezing to death in his own kitchen.

On the morning of Day Nineteen, the temperature in the hemlock hollow dropped to seven degrees Celsius at dawn. 

A thick, silver skin of ground frost was glittering across the broad leaves of the wild ostrich ferns along the brook, and the wind blowing down through the granite notch from the north had the sharp, metallic, flinty smell of an impending blizzard. 

It was only the third week of June, but the maritime forest has never cared about the Gregorian calendar. In the Siknikt district, summer is not a season; it is merely an eight-week green truce between two seven-month winters.

By the middle of December, the temperature across the Petitcodiac valley would plunge to twenty-five degrees below zero. 

The Nor'easters rolling up the eastern seaboard from Cape Hatteras would collide with the arctic high-pressure domes sweeping down from the Ungava Peninsula, dumping four feet of heavy, wet salt snow across the spruce forests in thirty-six hours. The gales roaring up the Bay of Fundy would drive freezing sleet through every unchinked gap in a timber wall, and a man who let his hearth fire die at three o'clock in the morning would wake up at sunrise to find his water wash-bucket frozen solid to the bottom planks.

I sat on the three-legged chopping block in the center of the yard, pulled my waterproof field notebook onto my knee, and did the cold, merciless math of survival.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ THE 120-DAY WINTER THERMAL EQUATION                                     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ • 1 Full Day of Continuous Heating (Dec 1 – Mar 31) = 1/16th Cord       │
│ • Total Winter Requirement = 7.5 to 8.0 Full Cords                      │
│ • Volume per Cord = 128 Cubic Feet (4 ft x 4 ft x 8 ft tightly stacked)│
│ • Total Wood Volume Required = 1,024 Cubic Feet of Dense Hardwood       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ • Current Stack Seasoning in Shed = 3.0 Cords                           │
│ • The Deficit = 5.0 Full Cords (640 Cubic Feet) to Felled & Split       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

If you do not possess a gasoline-powered chainsaw with a twenty-inch bar and a two-stroke engine, five cords of dense Canadian hardwood is roughly **two hundred and fifty thousand physical impacts of steel against wood.**

It is three hundred hours of blistered palms, aching lumbar vertebrae, sawdust ground deep into the pores of your neck, and forearms swollen tight as hamstrings. 

And far more dangerous than the physical exhaustion: every single sharp, ringing *crack* of an axe bit striking frozen maple echoes through a silent summer spruce forest for three-quarters of a mile, acting as a rhythmic acoustic beacon that can draw every wandering corpse and every desperate scavenger in the county directly toward your clearing.

"Two hundred swings every morning," I said aloud to Nipn, who was lying in the warm sun on the porch steps, watching me through half-closed amber eyes. "Two hundred swings before the sun clears the east bluff, while the river mist is still heavy enough to muffle the iron."

### Part II: The Singing Saw and the Felling Axe

I took my two-man crosscut saw—which I had converted for solo operation by bolting a vertical auxiliary handle carved from rock maple to the far end of the five-foot blade—my 2.5-pound Hudson Bay felling axe, two forged-steel timber wedges, and an eight-pound wooden maul carved from a solid root-knot of sugar maple.

I did not cut live green timber.

Green hardwood is nearly sixty percent water by weight. If you fell a living green sugar maple in July, the wood is so heavy it will sink like a stone in a creek, and if you burn it in a cast-iron stove in January, seventy percent of the fire's thermal energy will be wasted simply boiling the sap out of the cellular pores. 

It will hiss, it will sputter, it will coat the inside of your stone chimney flue with thick, flammable creosote tar that will ignite into a chimney fire by mid-winter, and it will produce half the heat of dry fuel.

I walked the high northern ridge behind the camp, scanning the canopy for **standing dead timber**:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ THE THREE ROYAL HARDWOODS OF SIKNIKT                                    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Standing-Dead Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum)                           │
│    • Density: 44 lbs/cu ft | Heat Output: 24.0 Million BTUs / Cord      │
│    • Burns with long, steady, glowing coals; zero sparks; clean ash.    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Yellow Birch (Betula alleghaniensis)                                 │
│    • Density: 42 lbs/cu ft | Heat Output: 21.8 Million BTUs / Cord      │
│    • High natural birch-oil content; ignites easily; hot blue flame.    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. White Ash (Fraxinus americana)                                       │
│    • Density: 41 lbs/cu ft | Heat Output: 23.6 Million BTUs / Cord      │
│    • Straight-splitting radial grain; naturally low moisture (25%).     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

The saw work was a slow, hypnotic, rhythmic meditation.

I selected a standing dead sugar maple forty feet tall and fourteen inches through the butt, killed by a lightning strike two summers ago. The bark had already sloughed off the upper trunk, leaving the gray, bone-dry heartwood exposed to the sun and wind, seasoned to perfection where it stood.

I dropped onto both knees in the soft moss, set the five-foot crosscut saw against the southern face of the trunk two feet above the root flare, and began to draw the blade.

*Zhhht-zhhht. Zhhht-zhhht.*

The razor-sharp champion lance teeth, which I had dressed with an eight-inch mill bastard file yesterday afternoon, sliced through the dry maple grain with a clean, singing hiss. 

With every draw stroke of my right arm, the saw threw long, curling ribbons of fragrant, snow-white wood shavings across my moccasins. 

When the kerf sank four inches into the trunk, I tapped a forged steel wedge into the cut behind the blade with the flat of the axe, keeping the thousands of pounds of tree weight from pinching the steel plate.

Twenty minutes of steady, unbroken pulling. 

The trunk gave a sudden, sharp, internal *tock*—the sound of the last structural fibers of the heartwood yielding under tension.

I slid the saw plate out of the cut in a single smooth motion, stepped four paces backward along my pre-cleared escape path behind a massive granite boulder, and watched.

The forty-foot maple leaned forward into the morning breeze, its bare gray crown sweeping through the sky, and crashed down through the hazel understory with a massive, rushing, earth-shaking *whump* that sent a cloud of dry lichen and green leaves billowing into the sunlit air.

Before the dust had even settled on the moss, I was over the trunk with the limbing axe.

I lopped off the side branches, stacking every limb thicker than two inches into neat bundles to serve as wattle fence rails and weir stakes, then used the crosscut saw to buck the fourteen-inch trunk into sixteen-inch stove lengths with mathematical precision.

### Part III: The Physics of the Split and the Windrow Stack

Splitting dense Canadian hardwood with a heavy maul is not an act of brute muscle; it is an act of **applied crystallographic fracture.**

Every tree trunk is a bundle of millions of vertical cellulose tubes bound together by natural lignin. As the wood dries, microscopic radial checks open up from the central heartwood out toward the cambium layer. 

If you drop a heavy splitting maul blindly across those radial lines, the steel blade bounces off the tough end-grain with a jarring, bone-rattling shock that vibrates straight up into your collarbone.

If you read the end-grain, locate the natural hairline check radiating from the center, and drop an eight-pound maul with the full acceleration of gravity behind your shoulders, the steel wedge enters the check and the wood flies open in two clean, symmetrical halves with a soft, hollow, musical *pop*.

*Pop. Pop. Pop.*

By noon every morning, I had split half a cord of sixteen-inch billets.

I hauled the split firewood down from the ridge to the camp clearing using a hand-drawn wooden travois constructed from two flexible, twelve-foot white birch poles, lashing thirty billets at a time onto the cross-slats with rawhide cords.

I did not toss the firewood into an untidy heap on the ground, where the bottom logs would absorb groundwater and rot into sponge in six weeks.

I stacked the wood along the southern exterior wall of the cabin using the traditional **Acadian / L'nu Windrow Method**:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ THE 64-FOOT ACADIAN WINDROW FIREWOOD FORTIFICATION                      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ [ Birchbark Capstone Layer ] ──▶ Overlapping sheets shed autumn rain     │
│ [ Four-Foot-Deep Hardwood ]  ──▶ Split billets stacked bark-side up     │
│ [ Criss-Cross Corner Towers] ──▶ 4x4 ft interlocking structural pillars │
│ [ Split-Cedar Bed Rails ]    ──▶ Keeps bottom row 4 inches off the moss │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

1. **The Bed Rails:** I laid four parallel lines of split cedar poles along the ground to hold the bottom row of firewood four inches above the damp forest duff, allowing the air to circulate freely beneath the stack.
2. **The Interlocking Corner Towers:** At each end of the sixty-four-foot run, I framed a four-foot-by-four-foot square pillar of criss-crossed billets, interlocking the layers at right angles like timber cribbing. These end pillars carried the lateral thrust of the entire woodpile, preventing the stack from tumbling over under high winds.
3. **Bark-Side Up Orientation:** Every split billet was laid with its rounded bark face pointing upward. If rain blew against the stack, the natural waterproof bark acted as a miniature shingle, shedding the water out toward the yard rather than letting moisture soak into the porous split grain.
4. **The Birchbark Capstone:** When the stack reached six feet in height, I covered the entire top surface with overlapping sheets of heavy paper-birch bark peeled from deadfalls, weighting the bark down with fifty smooth granite river cobblestones.

By Day Twenty-Six, the southern wall of my cabin was fortified by a solid, sixty-four-foot-long, six-foot-high, four-foot-deep wall of **eight full cords of split, air-drying sugar maple, yellow birch, and white ash.**

It was not merely fuel. 

It was a four-foot-thick thermal insulating barrier that blocked the freezing north gales from touching the cabin planks, and it represented **eight hundred days of continuous, smokeless, life-saving heat.**

I ran my calloused palm over the rough, fragrant bark of the top row, inhaling the sweet, sharp scent of split maple in the summer heat.

"The woodpile is full," I whispered, wiping the sweat from my eyes with the back of my leather work glove. "Now we build the eyes and the voice."

### Part IV: The Clacks in the Sky

On the morning of Day Twenty-Seven, I climbed the scarp trail to the Old Stone Light to construct the heart of my long-range intelligence system: **The Optical Semaphore Telegraph (The Clacks).**

Terry Pratchett’s *Discworld* novels had chronicled the rise of the Grand Trunk Semaphore Company—a continent-spanning network of wooden towers using mechanical black shutters and oil lamps to transmit messages across thousands of miles at the speed of light.

Long before the invention of copper telegraph wires, telephone lines, or radio satellites, the French engineer Claude Chappe had constructed a national optical semaphore network across France in 1792 that could transmit a thirty-word military dispatch from Paris to Lille in less than ten minutes using nothing more than wooden louvers, counterweights, and telescopes.

In a post-collapse world where cell towers were dead, where copper wires had been torn down for scrap, and where any radio transmission would broadcast your location to every scanner within fifty miles, **an optical semaphore telegraph was completely silent, invisible to the ear, and 100% untraceable.**

---

I spent three days inside the eight-sided glass lantern room of the lighthouse, constructing the **Six-Shutter Clacks Box** inside the central south-facing window frame:

```
┌─────────────┬─────────────┬─────────────┐
│  [ BOX 1 ]  │  [ BOX 2 ]  │  [ BOX 3 ]  │  ──▶ 6 Pivoting Matte-Black Louver Panels
├─────────────┼─────────────┼─────────────┤
│  [ BOX 4 ]  │  [ BOX 5 ]  │  [ BOX 6 ]  │  ──▶ Controlled by 6 Weighted Spectra Lines
└─────────────┴─────────────┴─────────────┘
```

1. **The Louver Frames:**
   * Six rectangular frames, each measuring twenty-four inches wide by eighteen inches tall, crafted from ultra-lightweight northern white cedar laths.
   * I covered each frame in tight, non-reflective, matte-black ripstop nylon salvaged from the throat of the Salisbury hot air balloon.
   * Each panel was mounted on a center-axis white ash pivot rod, seated in greased hardwood trunnion blocks lubricated with dry powdered graphite.
2. **The Control Mechanism:**
   * In their resting state, the six louvers hung edge-on to the window (completely invisible and transparent from the southern horizon).
   * Inside the lantern room, six wooden pull-levers carved from rock maple were linked to the louver rods via braided Spectra lines running through polished brass eyelets.
   * Pulling a lever rotated the louver ninety degrees, presenting a solid, flat, twenty-four-inch black rectangle against the bright, sky-lit glass of the tower cupola.
3. **The 6-Bit Binary Shutter Cipher:**
   * With six independent shutters (each capable of being Open [□] or Closed [■]), the array produced $2^6 = 64$ unique geometric combinations—more than enough for the entire English alphabet, the numerals 0 through 9, and twenty-eight tactical shorthand codes:
     * `[ ■ □ □ □ □ □ ]` = **Letter A**
     * `[ ■ ■ □ □ □ □ ]` = **Letter B**
     * `[ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ ]` = **CODE RED: Horde Migration in Valley**
     * `[ □ □ ■ ■ □ □ ]` = **Tide Safe / Shallow Weirs Open**
     * `[ ■ □ ■ □ ■ □ ]` = **GNU Terry Pratchett** (The broadcast keep-alive signal)
4. **The Heliograph (Daytime Solar Signaling):**
   * Beside the shutter array, I mounted an eight-inch polished stainless steel mirror on a two-axis brass gimbal bracket salvaged from the balloon burner frame.
   * Using a notched sighting tube aligned with crosshairs, I could direct a concentrated beam of reflected sunlight across twenty miles of open water with rifle-like accuracy.

---

At four o'clock in the afternoon on Day Twenty-Nine, with the summer sun sinking behind my right shoulder, I brought the sighting tube to bear on the high, wooded headland of **Amlamgog (Fort Folly)**, twelve miles to the south across Shepody Bay.

Through the 25x brass field spyglass, I could see the ancient granite cliffs where the Mi'kmaw gathering grounds overlooked the mouth of the Memramcook River.

There was no smoke down there. No burning buildings. Just the deep, primeval green of the spruce woods meeting the red tidal silt.

I sat at the wooden control console, took the six pull-levers in my hands, and cycled the array through the standard Discworld broadcast sequence:

*Click-shutter-clack. Click-shutter-clack.*

`[ G ] [ N ] [ U ]`

I locked the shutters open, leaned my elbows on the console, brought the spyglass to my eye, and watched the southern horizon for twenty minutes.

There was no answering flash. 

No mirror gleam from the distant trees; no lantern shutter blinking through the sea haze.

Just the vast, silent blue of the Atlantic sky and the eternal roar of the Fundy tide rushing past the rocks four hundred feet below the cliff.

I did not feel a single pang of disappointment.

You build the lighthouse before the schooner sets sail; you string the telegraph before the telegrapher sits down at the key. The tower was armed, the shutters were true, and the signal was ready the moment a pair of binoculars on the southern shore swept the northern sky.

### Part V: The Arrowmaker's Ledger and Quiver Segregation

On the morning of Day Thirty, I sat at the pine workbench in the cabin to address my long-range ammunition reserves.

A modern hunter who relies on brass centerfire cartridges is living on a countdown timer. 

Once your two boxes of .30-30 Winchester or twelve-gauge buckshot are fired, your precision steel rifle is nothing more than an expensive, four-foot iron club that you must clean with chemical solvents you cannot manufacture in the bush.

An ash flatbow and a quiver of wooden arrows is an **infinite loop of silent, reusable ammunition.**

I laid my materials out across the bench in clean, disciplined rows:
* Twelve seasoned Port Orford cedar shaft blanks, 5/16-inch diameter, spine-tested for my 48-pound bow.
* A bundle of wild Canada goose secondary wing feathers, harvested from the tidal mudflats during the spring molt.
* Two dried shin bones from last November's white-tailed doe.
* A bottle of boiled white pine pitch mixed with charcoal dust.
* A spool of fine silk thread from an old fly-tying kit.

---

I split the dense deer shin bone lengthwise with a cold chisel and a wooden mallet, using the six-inch mill file to shape eight **triangular, two-edged bone broadheads.**

Bone is an extraordinary, underrated material for arrow points.

It is lighter than steel, hard enough to punch through two layers of heavy leather, hide, and rib cartilage without chipping, and slightly porous on its tang, which allows it to absorb hot pine pitch for an unbreakable, permanent bond with the cedar wood.

I cut deep, self-cut nocks into the tail of the shafts, reinforcing the wood against the bowstring shock with tight wraps of silk thread soaked in pitch.

Then came the **Strict Biological Segregation Protocol**:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ THE CLEAN QUIVER (White / Gray Goose Fletching)                         │
│ • 6 Bone-Tipped Arrows / Oiled Port Orford Cedar                        │
│ • Strictly Dedicated for Live Game (Deer, Geese, Hares)                 │
│ • Sealed in grease-treated leather tube (Zero Pathogen Exposure)        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ THE "HOT" QUIVER (Charred Black-Banded Fletching)                       │
│ • 6 Scrap-Steel Triangular Broadheads                                   │
│ • Marked with black charcoal dye along the entire shaft length          │
│ • Dedicated Strictly for Infected Defense (Never Touches Food Animals)  │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

I dipped the fletchings of the six defense arrows into a black slurry of powdered charcoal and fish glue, staining the shafts with three broad black bands behind the nock.

If an arrow pierced the gray, rotting neck of an infected corpse, it became a biohazard weapon forever.

Even if I retrieved the arrow from the dead man's jaw, wiped the blade in the sand, and boiled the steel point in a pot of water, that black-banded arrow remained in the **Hot Quiver.** 

It would never be shot into a live deer; it would never hang in the smokehouse; and it would never come within ten feet of the kitchen table.

"Discipline," I muttered, sliding the six clean white-fletched hunting arrows into their sealed leather tube. "The moment you get lazy about biological hygiene, you are merely committing suicide on a delayed fuse."

### Part VI: The Deep Solitude of July

By Day Thirty-Two—the first week of July—the Creek Camp was a masterclass in solitary post-apocalyptic engineering.

* **Water:** Fourteen thousand gallons a day pouring through the split-cedar gravity flumes, ice-cold, crystal clean, and eternal.
* **Firewood:** Eight full cords of seasoned sugar maple, yellow birch, and white ash stacked four feet deep along the southern cabin wall.
* **Food:** Seventy pounds of cold-smoked flounder and bass in the cellar crocks, eighty-five pounds of coarse marsh salt, twenty pounds of dried wild greens, and three shallow pit weirs swimming with live fish.
* **Security:** Gravity trunnion gates dropping silent on bear-grease pivots, the Four Secure Living Corridors cleared and baffled, and the Old Stone Light retrofitted with Montgolfier thermal traps, the 35-foot cliffside elevator hoist, and the 6-shutter Clacks array.

The camp was safe. The food was secure. The roof was tight.

And I was completely, utterly alone.

---

At eight o'clock in the evening, I sat on the cedar porch step with a tin mug of hot sweetgrass tea, watching the first yellow fireflies begin their slow, silent dance across the dark green ferns.

Beside my moccasins, Nipn let out a long, heavy, shuddering sigh, resting his gray muzzle across my knee.

I scratched the thick fur behind his ears, feeling the steady, rhythmic rise and fall of his ribs.

"You're a good dog, Nipn," I said into the quiet woods. "You don't talk back, and you don't borrow tools without putting them back in the chest."

The dog thumped his tail once against the cedar deck planks, closed his eyes, and went to sleep.

I looked down at my hands.

My palms were thick with yellow calluses from four hundred hours of timber framing, crosscut sawing, and weir digging. My skin was dark brown from the Fundy sun, smelling permanently of woodsmoke, cedar oil, and salt ocean brine.

I had constructed an empire of wood and stone where a man could live for twenty years without ever setting foot in a grocery store, touching an electrical switch, or paying a dollar of municipal taxes.

I opened my field notebook, dipped my drafting pencil into the inkwell, and looked at the clean, white grid page.

I did not calculate cords of wood. I did not draw a map of the river.

I wrote four words in the center of the page:

> *Still here. Still building.*

I closed the notebook, blew out the candle, and sat in the dark with my arm around the dog, listening to the vast, empty silence of Mi'kma'ki, waiting for the trail to speak.
