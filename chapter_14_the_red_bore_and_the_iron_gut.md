# Chapter 14: The Red Bore and the Iron Gut

### I. The Threshing Floor and the Granite Anvil

The smell that rose off the headland at sunrise was the deep, sour perfume of awakening clay.

Three days after the great southeasterly gale had broken the winter ice pack, the snow was retreating in long, ragged gray tongues into the deep hollows beneath the red spruce. Water was everywhere—trickling down the sandstone fractures in clear, iron-cold threads, pooling in the sheep pastures, and dripping from the eaves of the high stone barn with a heavy, metronomic cadence that measured out the end of the long dark.

Inside the barn, the air was cold enough to show a man’s breath, but the draft was still. The threshing floor—thirty feet of three-inch white pine planks pegged with seasoned oak trunnels into red spruce sleepers—had been swept clean of chaff and dried sheep dung.

Mac stood near the central post, having stripped off his heavy mackinaw and moose-hide vest. He wore only a salt-and-pepper woolen henley with the sleeves pushed up over his forearms and his heavy homespun trousers tucked into his moose-hide winter moccasins. His broad hands hung loose at his sides, fingers curved, knuckles scarred and white from forty years of cold chisels and green timber.

"Put the staff in your hands, Maya," Mac said. His voice was low, level, and possessed that calm, unhurried cadence that belonged to the red sandstone beneath the floorboards. "Not the broom. Not the iron shovel. The ash."

Maya stood ten paces away, near the open hay doors that looked out over the churned, chocolate-red waters of the river.

Her white ash staff rested upright in her right fist, the forged iron ferrule planted firmly between two floorboards. She wore her faded blue denim work shirt over a dark wool undershirt, her hair pulled back into a tight, practical braid that exposed the sharp line of her jaw and the hard, healing hollow of her collarbone. The bruise over her left fourth rib had faded to a mottled greenish-yellow, the thick bone callus under her skin feeling solid now—not like a cracked eggshell, but like an iron rivet holding two plates of boiler iron together.

Tucked into the deep fleece pocket sewn into the inside lining of her shirt, directly between the warm swell of her breasts, Scar was a motionless ball of dark russet fur. Only his black-rimmed ears twitched at the hollow drip of meltwater outside. The squirrel was fat on parched corn and cracked pumpkin seeds; he knew that Maya’s chest was ninety-eight point six degrees, and as long as the vibrations through the wood did not carry the high-frequency *click* of a steel rifle sear, he had no intention of leaving his nest.

Maya looked at Mac, her dark eyes narrowing with sudden, suspicious caution.

"Sparring?" she asked. "Mac, you don't even have a padded gambeson on. That staff has forty ounces of seasoned white ash behind a four-sided carbon-steel point. If I catch you under the ribs with a full-extension lunge, I’ll split your cartilage."

The corner of Mac’s mouth twitched—the faintest ghost of a smile creasing the red-brown leather of his cheek.

"You won't split my cartilage, Maya," he said quietly.

"I’m not playing," she said, her voice tightening, a sudden prickle of heat rising into her cheeks. "You spent three weeks teaching me how to drop my weight through the pelvis and drive the iron through a man’s breastbone. I know how to hit now. I’m not some kid swinging a broom handle in an orchard."

"I know you aren't," Mac said. He stepped forward two paces, coming to a halt six feet from the reach of her staff. He widened his stance slightly, his knees flexing by half an inch, his shoulders dropping into an easy, settled posture. "That’s why we’re here. You’ve been hitting empty flour sacks and cedar posts. A sack doesn't have weight; a post doesn't look back at you. Hit me."

"No."

"Hit me, Maya."

"Mac, don't be stupid—"

"I’m not asking you to tap me," Mac said. His tone didn't rise, but it took on the deep, resonant gravity of an anvil being struck underwater. He drew a slow, measured breath through his nose—the quiet, deliberate inhalation of a man reaching down through thirty feet of red mud into the unbroken granite shelf of the province. 

In the shadows beneath his heavy grey brows, his irises shifted. 

The pale, weathered grey of his everyday gaze dissolved, replaced by a cold, luminous ring of pale azure—not a theatrical flare of fire, but an ancient, freezing incandescence, like winter sunlight trapped thirty fathoms deep inside an Atlantic iceberg.

Maya saw it. The hair on her forearms rose beneath her flannel sleeves. The skin at the back of her neck prickled with an instinctive, primal dread that made every muscle in her calves lock tight.

Beneath her shirt, Scar let out a tiny, stifled *chirr* and burrowed three inches deeper into her fleece, curling his tail tight over his snout.

"Give me Sussex," Mac said softly, his eyes locked on hers. "Give me the cold gravel of the railway bed. Give me the men who put their hands on your collar and told you that you were livestock. Give it to the wood, Maya. Put it in my chest."

Something cracked inside her—a frozen dam of humiliation and terror that had been holding back an ocean of poison for six long months.

She didn't think. Her body moved on raw, ignited instinct.

She stepped forward with her left foot, locked her right heel into the pine seam, and drove the white ash staff forward with a savage, two-handed piston thrust from her hip. The forged iron shoe aimed straight for the center of his chest—the exact strike Mac had drilled into her, backed by every ounce of her seventy-pound forward drive and the furious torque of her shoulders.

*THWACK.*

The iron ferrule struck the center of Mac’s woolen henley directly over his sternum.

The sound was not the wet, sickening crunch of yielding flesh; it was the sharp, metallic *CLANG* of a five-pound sledge striking a Mary’s Point granite foundation block. 

A violent, numbing vibration shot back up the length of the ash staff, stinging Maya’s palms, rattling her elbow joints, and sending a dull jolt through her healing ribs.

Mac did not move. He did not rock backward on his heels; he did not grunt; he did not blink. The wool of his shirt wasn't even indented. He stood there like a stone mooring bollard on the Dorchester wharf, looking down at her with those pale, azure-rimmed eyes.

"Weak," Mac said, his voice quiet, calm, entirely without mockery. "You hesitated on the push. You pulled three inches of wind out of the stroke because you were afraid you’d hurt me. They didn't hesitate when they threw you into the mud, did they?"

A hot, choking sob tore out of Maya’s throat—half fury, half shame.

"Damn you," she snarled.

She ripped the staff back, spun three feet to her left to clear his centerline, and brought the heavy ash sweeping down in a vicious, two-handed diagonal strike aimed at the side of his neck. It was a stroke meant to shatter a collarbone and drop a grown man like a felled spruce.

*CLANG-THUMP.*

Mac brought his left forearm up. He didn't swat the staff away; he simply let the cured white ash crash into his bare forearm at sixty miles an hour.

The wood rebounded with an ear-splitting crack. The white pine floorboards groaned beneath Maya’s boots from the sheer kinetic recoil, but Mac’s arm didn't drop an inch. The skin of his wrist wasn't even red.

"Better leverage," Mac observed calmly, his breath steady, his eyes tracking the path of her shoulders. "Good torque through the oblique. But you’re clenching your back teeth, Maya. You’re holding your breath in your throat. When you hold your breath, your forearms run out of oxygen in twenty seconds. Exhale on the impact. Again."

"Shut up!" she screamed, and the rage finally took her completely.

She lost all sense of the barn, all sense of the spring thaw, all sense of the man standing before her. 

She was back on the rail cut in Sussex. She was back in the reeking, greasy machine shop with the smell of stale moonshine and unwashed men. She was back on her hands and knees in the freezing cinder ballast, listening to the laughter of the raiders, feeling the cold steel of a shotgun barrel shoved into the small of her back while her hands were wired with barbed wire.

She attacked with a blind, terrifying ferocity.

*Crack. Thwack. Clang-thump.*

The ash staff became a blur of white wood and dark iron. She thrust at his belly; she swept at his shins; she hooked the ferrule around his knee to trip his pivot; she lunged for his throat. Every blow was delivered with maximum human force, the seasoned wood whistling through the cold barn air, striking him again and again—across the shoulders, into the ribs, against his forearms, straight into the broad wall of his chest.

The noise inside the barn was deafening. It sounded like a shipyard blacksmith hammering cold boiler plate.

And through it all, Mac stood in the storm.

He didn't strike back. He moved his feet only to maintain his balance, parrying with an elbow, taking a direct blow to the shoulder, absorbing a thrust that would have caved in the chest of an ox. He was the rock; he was the riverbed; he was the ancient red sandstone of the Petkotkweyak that had watched ten thousand spring floods roar past without losing a single grain of shale.

Between each thunderous impact, his voice drifted through the storm, quiet, steady, and relentlessly instructive:

"Watch your left elbow. You’re leaving your liver open when you recover from the hook."

*THWACK.*

"Breathe. Through the nose. Don't pant like a dog."

*CLANG.*

"Good footwork on the sidestep. Put the hate in the heel. Not the teeth. The heel."

*THUD.*

"Again."

Maya drove forward with one final, desperate, lunging strike—a two-handed overhead smash that brought the full mass of the ash staff whistling down toward his skull. 

Mac lifted his right hand. He didn't duck; he didn't dodge. He caught the seasoned white ash four inches below the iron ferrule in the open palm of his hand.

The wood stopped dead with a sharp, concussive *CRACK* that echoed into the high cedar rafters. 

Maya froze. Her hands were still clamped to the middle taper of the staff, her arms trembling violently from exhaustion, her chest heaving as she sucked in great, ragged lungfuls of the cold barn air. Sweat was dripping from the tip of her nose, stinging her eyes, plastering loose strands of dark hair to her temples.

She tried to wrench the staff free. 

It was like trying to pull an embedded anchor out of solid bedrock. Mac’s fingers were simply closed around the wood; he wasn't straining, his knuckles weren't white, but twenty horses couldn't have pulled that staff from his grip.

"Now," Mac whispered, his azure gaze looking straight down into her wild, dilated pupils. "Your weapon is trapped. You have no leverage. You have no breath left. What do you do?"

Maya stared at him, her lips parted, her chest burning like she had swallowed hot coals.

The fury that had carried her through the last five minutes suddenly collapsed, leaving behind nothing but an immense, bottomless, hollow exhaustion. Her fingers went limp. The white ash staff slipped from her calloused palms and dropped to the threshing floor with a hollow, wooden clatter.

### II. The Muffled Breath and the Wool

The silence that followed the clatter of the wood was absolute.

The only sound in the barn was the heavy, ragged sound of Maya’s breathing and the slow, rhythmic dripping of the meltwater from the eaves outside. High up on a cross-beam, Scar sat on his haunches, his little russet ears twitched forward, watching the two humans with bright, unblinking black eyes.

Maya stood in front of Mac, her hands hanging empty at her sides. Her palms were raw, red, and blistered from the vibrating wood; her shoulders were sagging, and the muscles in her thighs were trembling so hard she could barely keep her moccasins flat on the pine boards.

She didn't look down at the staff. She looked at Mac’s chest.

His woolen henley had two light smears of white wood-ash where the staff had struck him, but his skin was unbruised, his breathing was slow and even, and the cold azure glow in his irises was already softening, fading back into the deep, warm, weathered grey of an early spring sea.

The anger was gone. The poison that had been eating her alive since Sussex had been hammered out on the anvil of his body, leaving her completely defenseless. For the first time in six months, she had no staff to lean on, no knife in her fist, no wall behind her back.

She looked up at his face—at the deep, sun-etched lines around his eyes, the broad, crooked nose that had been broken by green timber twenty years ago, the quiet, immoveable gentleness that lived behind his silence.

Her chin trembled. A single hot tear cut a clean path through the sweat and wood-dust on her cheek.

She took one half-step forward.

Her boots dragged on the pine planks. She didn't hesitate; she closed the two feet of empty air between them and buried her face directly into the heavy, lanolin-scented wool of his shirt, right over his breastbone. Her hands came up, her blistered fingers twisting into the thick fabric of his sides, gripping the wool with a desperate, white-knuckled intensity as if she were hanging over the edge of a sixty-foot drop.

Mac didn't move. He stood still as a weathered red spruce pillar, letting her anchor herself to his frame.

Her voice came out muffled against his chest, stripped of every shred of sarcasm, every wall of pride, raw and broken and small:

"Just hold me, ok? ... Just for a minute."

Mac didn't speak. He didn't offer a platitude; he didn't tell her it was over; he didn't lecture her on surviving.

He simply brought his arms around her.

His hands were massive, scarred, and warm as heated soapstone. He laid one broad palm flat against the trembling muscles between her shoulder blades and wrapped his other arm around her ribs, gathering her against him with infinite, deliberate care. He pulled her flush against his chest, lifting her weight just enough to take the strain off her shaking knees, tucking the crown of her dark head beneath the curve of his jaw where the cold wind blowing through the open hay doors couldn't touch her.

Maya let out a long, shuddering sob that seemed to tear itself out of the very bottom of her lungs.

She didn't weep like a child; she wept like a soldier who had held the perimeter for a hundred days without sleep and had finally found a wall that couldn't be breached. Her forehead pressed hard against the dense muscle of his sternum. Beneath her cheek, she could feel the deep, slow, thunderous cadence of his heart—a steady, unhurried sixty beats a minute, completely untroubled by the violence that had just filled the barn.

The heat radiating from him was astonishing. It was the same subtle, ancient warmth he had coaxed into the stone floor of the lighthouse—the quiet, living vitality of the red earth that pushed back the frost and made the winter sap run in the sugar bush. It seeped through her damp denim shirt, soaking into her exhausted shoulders, loosening the agonizing knot of tension in her neck, wrapping around her bruised ribs like a heated woolen blanket.

Inside her pocket, Scar shifted. The squirrel poked his black nose out through the top button of her shirt, found himself pressed comfortably between Maya’s warm breast and the solid expanse of Mac’s woolen chest, let out a tiny, soft whistle of absolute approval, and settled back down into the dark.

They stood there in the center of the threshing floor for a long time.

Minutes drifted past, measured only by the meltwater dropping from the roof and the steady, calming rise and fall of Mac’s ribs beneath Maya’s hands. The air between them was thick with the scent of wood-smoke, sweat, wool, and the clean, mineral smell of the river. 

It was not the embrace of an elder comforting a stray. It was the fierce, electric, unspoken covenant of two adults who had seen the old world die in fire, who had bled into the same red dirt, and who had chosen to stand together against whatever darkness was coming up the bay.

Maya’s trembling gradually slowed. 

Her grip on his shirt softened, her fingers uncurling from the wool to rest flat against his ribs. She didn't pull away; she stayed tucked against him, her face tilted slightly into the warm hollow of his neck, breathing in the smell of him—pine pitch, cedar smoke, salt water, and cold iron.

"You're an idiot, Mac," she whispered into his skin, her voice still rough, but carrying the first faint spark of her old, defiant self.

"Aye," Mac rumbled, his chest vibrating against her cheek. "I’ve been told that by better scholars than you."

"You let me hit you with an iron-shod staff for five minutes."

"You needed to hit something that wouldn't break," Mac said gently. "If you kept that Sussex mud locked up in your ribs, it would have turned into an abscess. You can't shoot a rifle with poison in your blood."

Maya took a slow, deep breath, feeling her lungs expand fully for the first time without pain. 

She lifted her head slowly, stepping back three inches, but leaving her hands resting lightly on the broad planes of his chest. Her dark eyes, rimmed with red, searched his weathered face. 

"Did it hurt?" she asked, a faint, crooked smile touching her lips. "Even a little bit?"

Mac looked down at her, his grey eyes warm and clear, the last trace of the cold azure fire completely gone. He reached out with one scarred thumb and gently wiped away the smudge of wood-ash and salt from beneath her left eye.

"A mosquito bit me on the Miramichi in eighty-four," Mac said solemnly. "That hurt more."

Maya let out a short, wet laugh, shaking her head. She leaned her forehead against his chest one last time, a quick, affectionate touch, before stepping back and picking up her white ash staff from the floorboards.

The wood felt different in her hands now. It was no longer a crutch to keep her from falling, nor was it an instrument of desperate, terrified rage. It was just seasoned ash and forged steel—a tool of the commonwealth, balanced and ready for the work ahead.

"Come on," Maya said, rolling her shoulders and feeling the clean, elastic strength flowing through her back. "Let’s go up to the light. The sun is on the glass, and I want to see if the flint corn has popped."

### III. The Green Shoots of the High Cupola

Sixty feet above the red mud of the headland, the lantern room was an island of midsummer trapped in the throat of early April.

When Mac and Maya pushed open the heavy copper-sheathed trapdoor at the head of the spiral stairs, the warmth washed down over them like the breath of a greenhouse. The air was thick with the rich, fermenting aroma of wet riverbank silt, the sweet mineral dust of sugar-maple ash, and the faint, unmistakable green scent of waking vegetation.

Maya leaned her staff against the brass-fitted frame of the southern reflector and walked along the curving perimeter bench.

"Look at them," she said, her voice dropping into a tone of pure, quiet wonder. "Mac, look at the third flat."

In the shallow cedar boxes they had seeded three days before the ice breakup, the earth had begun to crack.

Dozens of tiny, pale-green arches—thick as baling twine and tipped with hard, amber seed-coats—had pushed through the dark loam, lifting the crust of dried silt toward the sunlight. In the center flat, where the Fort Folly flint corn had been sown, sixty sharp green spears had already unfolded, standing two inches high in neat, straight ranks, drinking in the seventy-degree heat trapped by the massive glass panes.

Maya knelt by the staging bench, her fingertips hovering a fraction of an inch above the green shoots.

"Auntie Elizabeth was right about the eelgrass compost," she said, looking back over her shoulder at Mac. "The salt keeps the mold down, but the marine nitrogen acts like an accelerator under this glass. Look at the vigor on those stems. They aren't spindly; they're thick as goose quills."

Mac walked over with a copper sprinkling can filled with rainwater from the cistern on the second tier. He handed the can to Maya.

"They have the blood of the valley in them," Mac said, leaning his hip against the copper sill, gazing out through the glass at the vast, shining expanse of the Cumberland Basin. "That corn has been grown on the Fort Folly ridge since the French built their first dyke at Port Royal. It knows how to survive three weeks of frost and an east wind that smells of salt. If you give it five inches of silt and two days of sun, it will grow through a floorboard."

Maya took the copper can. The rose on the spout was pierced with hundreds of needle-fine holes Mac had drilled with a watchmaker’s punch. 

She moved along the bench with a slow, deliberate grace, tilting the can to lay down a gentle, misty shower of lukewarm rainwater over the cedar flats. The droplets caught the morning sun, glistening like seed pearls on the dark earth and the pale-green shoots.

Inside her shirt, Scar finally poked his head out through her collar. He rested his front paws on the copper rim of the staging bench, sniffed the damp earth with deep, twitching curiosity, and let out a soft, contented *churr*.

"Don't you even think about digging up those kernels," Maya warned, tapping the squirrel’s wet black nose with the tip of her wet finger. "You eat the corn from the jar, not the seed flats. If you dig up Noel’s flint corn, I’m putting you on watch duty on the outer gallery without your fleece."

Scar blinked his large black eyes, entirely unimpressed by the threat, and hopped down onto the bench to inspect a curled squash seedling that was wrestling its way out of a cracked shell.

Maya stood up, set the copper can on the floor, and joined Mac at the southern curve of the glass.

From this height, the full scale of the spring transformation was laid out beneath them.

The great white ice bridge that had choked the river for three months was completely gone. In its place, the Petitcodiac was a raging, chocolate-red highway of liquid mud and foam, sweeping down from the Moncton bends toward Shepody Bay with a violent, terrifying momentum. Along the mud shelves eighty feet below the cliff, the stranded slabs of drift ice were melting rapidly under the warm sun, leaving behind deep, greasy gouges in the red silt where the floes had grounded on the ebb.

To the south, twelve miles across the grey-blue water of the Cumberland Basin, the low, forested spine of the Nova Scotia shore lay sharp against the horizon. 

Through the brass nautical telescope mounted on the center pedestal, the mouth of the Missaguash River was visible as a dark, muddy notch in the yellow salt-marsh grass. 

"Ross is moving," Mac said, his voice dropping into that cool, analytical cadence that measured distance and tide like a surveyor’s chain. "Look at the high water at the border railway cut."

Maya stepped up to the telescope, bent her knee, and pressed her right eye against the cold brass cup.

The thirty-power precision lenses brought the distant shoreline into razor-sharp clarity. 

At the mouth of the Missaguash, where the old timber railway bridge crossed the tidal gut, three wooden double-ended whaleboats lay moored to the pilings. On the mud bank above the high-water line, groups of men in dark green winter coats and grey wool watch caps were rolling heavy fifty-five-gallon drums down the embankment on skids. 

"Whaleboats," Maya whispered, her knuckles tightening around the brass focusing barrel. "They’re twenty-six-footers, Mac. Just like Noel said. Double-ended fisheries cutters with oak keelsons and clinker-built cedar strakes. I see six men on the middle boat... they’re rigging an iron tripod over the stem post."

"The Brownings," Mac said.

"Aye. The fifty-calibers. They’re bolting a heavy pintle mount to the apron behind the stem. If they mount a heavy machine gun there, they have a hundred and eighty degrees of traverse over the bow."

She adjusted the fine-focus ring. 

Further up the embankment, standing on the ballast ties near a khaki-painted military command tent, stood a tall, lean man in a long dark wool greatcoat with a sheepskin collar. He carried no rifle; in his right hand was a pair of heavy field glasses, which he held to his eyes, staring directly northwest across twelve miles of open water toward the high red cliff of their lighthouse.

Even through thirty-power optics, there was an unmistakable chill in the man’s posture. He did not pace; he did not shout at his men; he stood motionless on the railway iron, cold, patient, and methodical as a spider waiting on a web.

"Colonel Ross," Maya murmured, her throat going dry. "He’s watching the tower."

"He knows we have the glass," Mac said, not looking through the telescope, keeping his naked eyes fixed on the distant notch of the river mouth. "He knows Noel traded us the rifles. A soldier like Ross doesn't hurry. He’ll wait for the full-moon spring tide on Tuesday. That’s when the flood runs fastest—forty-eight feet of water piling into the bay at seven knots. He’ll launch on the last of the ebb, ride the current down to the mouth of the basin, and let the bore carry his eight boats straight into our gut before we can work the ballista."

Maya pulled back from the eyepiece. She looked at Mac, her jaw setting with that hard, cold determination that had carried her through the snow from Sussex.

"We have four days," she said.

"Four days," Mac agreed. "The forge is banked. The spikes are quenched. Tomorrow at low slack, we drop the sixty-foot spruce stringers into the channel and chain the deadmen to the cliff."

Maya picked up her white ash staff, feeling the solid, living weight of the wood in her hands. She looked back at the twenty-four cedar seed flats, where the green spears of the flint corn were reaching up toward the glass, drinking in the sun.

"Let him bring his whaleboats," Maya said, her voice dropping into a quiet, lethal promise. "We’re not giving up an inch of this dirt."

### IV. The Mooring of the Spruce Teeth

The low-water slack on Sunday morning left the throat of the Petitcodiac looking like a canyon cut through raw liver.

Forty-eight feet of tide had drained out into the Cumberland Basin in six relentless hours, exposing nearly a half-mile of glistening, red-ochre silt on either side of the central channel. The mud was not solid ground; it was an emulsion of pulverized red shale, salt water, and marine clay that had the consistency of grease on the surface and the suction of wet cement six inches down. A man stepping carelessly into an unmapped gutter could sink to his knees before his weight transferred, and if the tide caught him with his boots glued to the clay, the river swallowed him without a ripple.

Maya stood on the narrow shale terrace twenty feet above the silt line, her boots planted firmly on the fractured bedrock, her ash staff driven four inches into a gravel seam to anchor her balance. 

She wore her heavy oilskin trousers over her wool trousers, her sleeves rolled to the elbows, and a thick leather rigger’s apron cinched over her chest. Tucked into her flannel cleavage, Scar was awake but completely motionless, his head flattened against her sternum, his little black claws hooked into the dense weave of her brassiere. The squirrel hated the sound of the mud; whenever a pocket of trapped marsh gas *blooped* out of the red gutters, his ears flattened back against his skull, but the steady warmth of Maya’s skin kept him from panicking.

Down below her, in the middle of the narrow gut where the channel contracted to eighty feet between the shale bluff and a submerged sandstone reef, Mac was working in the mud.

He wore no snowshoes or mudboards. He was bare-legged to the thighs, wearing only an old pair of laced seal-skin wading moccasins and his cut-off wool trousers, his massive thighs and calves caked in dried red clay.

"Give me the turnbuckle, Maya," Mac called up from the gut. His voice was completely steady, showing none of the ragged strain that usually accompanied two hours of labor in freezing salt silt.

Maya picked up the forged iron rigging screw—a thirty-pound double-ended turnbuckle Mac had salvaged from the boom-guy of an old gypsum derrick in Hillsborough. She balanced it across her forearms, scrambled down the notched cedar ladder pinned to the sandstone face, and stepped out onto the narrow ledge of hard gravel that Mac had banked against the cliff base with dry quartz cobbles.

She handed the heavy screw to him. 

Mac was standing in waist-deep red water at the edge of the channel, holding the crown of the first sixty-foot red spruce stringer against the current with one hand.

The log was two feet in diameter at the butt, weighing nearly two tons, its surface completely stripped of bark and spiked every eighteen inches with Mac’s nine-inch forged triangular iron teeth. In the water, the log was a lethal, buoyant battering ram, bucking against the eddies like a hooked sturgeon. Yet Mac held it pinned against the bedrock shoulder as easily as a man holding a birchbark canoe by the gunwale.

"You're not sinking," Maya said, her eyes dropping to his feet.

Around Mac’s ankles, the red silt didn't behave like mud. It packed tight, forming a hard, dense pedestal of compacted sand beneath his moccasins, as if the riverbed itself recognized the covenant of the *Kinap* and refused to let him slip.

"The mud knows who belongs to it," Mac said, taking the turnbuckle from her. 

He slipped the heavy forged clevis pin through the end link of the 7/8-inch wrought-iron anchor chain that was bolted through the spruce butt. The other end of the chain disappeared into a four-foot horizontal bore Mac had blasted into the face of the sandstone cliff two weeks before the freeze.

Inside that bore, wedged behind three tons of hydraulic cement and quartz boulders, lay a six-foot length of eighty-pound railway iron—the deadman anchor.

Mac threaded the turnbuckle’s square-headed screw into the jaw with his bare fingers, spinning the three-quarter-inch thread until the slack came out of the chain. He took a two-foot forged iron spud wrench from his belt, slipped it through the turnbuckle’s central barrel, and leaned into the rotation.

*Screee-chank.*

The massive wrought-iron threads groaned, biting together under immense torque. With every half-turn, the sixty-foot red spruce stringer was drawn tighter against the channel throat, until the timber settled into the water at a forty-five-degree angle pointing diagonally down-stream toward the oncoming flood.

The nine-inch barbed iron spikes lay submerged six inches below the brown surface, completely invisible to anyone looking across the water, their sharp, triangular teeth aimed straight at the soft cedar bottoms of any boat attempting to run the gut.

"That's the port stringer anchored," Mac said, releasing the wrench and wiping a streak of red silt from his temple with the back of his arm. "Now we link the center hinge."

"What about the telegraph wire?" Maya asked, pointing with her ash staff toward the two wooden spools sitting on the gravel shelf.

"We string it three feet off the mud," Mac said. "Noel’s boys brought four hundred yards of galvanized shortwave line from Sackville. It’s high-carbon steel—it doesn't stretch, and it doesn't rot in salt water. We run two strands across the shallows between the reef and the mud flat, stake them with four-foot ash pickets driven flush with the silt, and leave eighteen inches of slack."

Maya’s mechanical mind instantly processed the trap. A fierce, predatory grin spread across her face.

"The oars," she said.

"The oars," Mac nodded. "Ross’s whaleboats have twenty-six-foot cedar hulls pulling eight-foot ash sweeps. A whaleboat can't steer with a rudder alone in a seven-knot tidal bore; the steersman has to rely on the stroke oar and the bow oar to keep the head from broaching into the mud banks. When those wooden sweeps hit that galvanized wire three inches under the surface, the wire won't snap—it’ll whip around the blades, bind the loom against the thole pins, and tear the oars right out of the rowers' hands."

"And while they’re drifting sideways without oars," Maya added, her eyes flashing, "the current sweeps them straight onto the spruce spikes."

"Aye," Mac said, wading out of the red water onto the gravel shelf, the clay dripping from his dark, muscular legs. "The river does all the work. We just provide the iron."

They worked in relentless, synchronized rhythm for the next three hours, racing against the turning tide.

While Mac hauled the second sixty-foot spruce stringer across the channel and anchored it to the southern cliff deadman, Maya unspooled the stiff, springy telegraph wire across the outer mud shelf. 

She used her white ash staff as a lever to tension the steel line, driving three-foot white cedar pickets into the gravel seams with a heavy wooden maul, wrapping the galvanized wire around the picket crowns with three half-hitches, and pinning each turn with two-inch forged staples. Her hands moved with the quick, automatic precision of the engine mechanic she was, her fingers ignoring the freezing salt mud that caked her knuckles.

Inside her shirt, Scar’s nose twitched as the tide turned.

A low, distant whisper began to hum through the red silt beneath their boots—a sound like dry peas being shaken in an iron kettle, miles to the south.

Mac paused on the gravel shelf, his head coming up, his nostrils flaring to catch the scent of the incoming air.

"Slack is done," he said. "The Cumberland Basin is filling. In forty minutes, the bore will hit the bends."

"Boom is locked," Maya said, driving the final staple into the southern picket and giving the telegraph wire a solid kick with her boot. 

The wire sang with a high, tight *ping*—taut as a banjo string, hidden beneath the first swirling tongues of the brown flood water that were already beginning to creep across the red mud shelves.

Mac reached down, took Maya by the elbow with one massive, clay-streaked hand, and lifted her three feet up onto the lowest rung of the cliff ladder as easily as if she were a cedar shingle. 

"Up," Mac said. "Get the grease off your boots. Tonight we keep the glass clean."

---

### V. The Red Pulse from Fort Folly

Night fell over the Petitcodiac without stars.

A heavy, low-hanging maritime ceiling had rolled in off the Bay of Fundy just after dusk—a thick, wet blanket of salt-mist that smelled of herring brine, cold mud, and the distant, rotting seaweed of the outer islands. It choked the river canyon in a gray, spectral gloom, hiding the opposite shoreline and deadening the roar of the rushing water to a low, hollow rumble that vibrated through the foundation stones of the lighthouse.

Sixty feet up in the lantern room, the air was warm, quiet, and smelled of steeping mint and clean copper.

Maya sat on the staging bench beneath the south-facing glass, her mackinaw unbuttoned, her wool trousers dry and clean. Her white ash staff rested across her knees, its iron ferrule polished smooth with fine emery paper. 

Beside her, sitting in the warm glow of a small whale-oil candle, Scar was occupied with a dried apple ring Noel had sent from the Fort Folly stores. The squirrel held the fruit in both leathery front paws, nibbling in quick, circular rotations with his sharp yellow teeth, entirely oblivious to the eight wooden whaleboats moving across the salt water miles away.

Mac stood at the Clacks frame on the outer catwalk, his silhouette framed against the dark sweep of the bay. He had cleaned the river silt from his skin; he wore his heavy boiled wool sweater beneath his moose-hide vest, his hair tied back with a rawhide thong.

Maya looked out through the glass, her eyes adjusting to the thick, shifting gloom.

"Can Noel even see our shutter through this soup?" she asked, setting her mug of mint tea on the cedar bench.

"Noel isn't looking with his eyes," Mac replied without turning around. "He’s using the brass transit with the cross-hairs aligned to the high chimney of this light. A kerosene mantle cuts two miles of sea-fog if you know which compass degree to stare at. Watch the high shoulder of the Fort Folly bluff."

Maya lifted the twenty-four-power brass telescope from its bracket and leveled it southwest, training the cross-hairs on the black, invisible hump where the Maringouin Peninsula met the mouth of the bay.

For ten minutes, there was nothing. Only the dark, swirling mist and the rhythmic, greasy slap of the high tide forty feet below the parapet.

Then, five miles away in the black heart of the spruce timber, a needle of red fire blinked out of the fog.

*One. Two. Three.*

Maya’s pulse gave a sharp, electric kick against her ribs. 

"Red light," she said, her voice dropping into a hard, tactical whisper. "Auntie Elizabeth put the copper slide on her lantern. She’s flashing the battle cipher."

Mac stepped into the lantern room, pulled the louver operating rod, and stepped onto the Clacks treadle. 

*Clack. Clack-clack.* 

The twenty-inch silvered locomotive reflector flared once—a blinding white lance of light that stabbed through the fog, acknowledging the signal, before the cedar shutters snapped shut with a dull wooden report.

Across the bay, the distant red needle began to chatter with frantic, rhythmic precision:

*R-O-S-S. L-A-U-N-C-H-E-D.*

A five-second pause. Then:

*E-I-G-H-T. B-O-A-T-S. F-O-R-T-Y. R-O-W-E-R-S. T-W-E-N-T-Y. H-I-G-H-L-A-N-D. R-I-F-L-E-S.*

Maya read the letters aloud as they burned in her mind, her jaw locking tighter with every pulse:

*T-H-R-E-E. F-I-F-T-Y. C-A-L. B-R-O-W-N-I-N-G-S. B-O-W. M-O-U-N-T-S.*

The distant red light paused again, then flashed three quick, final staccato bursts:

*R-I-D-I-N-G. F-L-O-O-D. T-I-D-E. A-R-R-I-V-A-L. O-N-E. H-O-U-R.*

The red light vanished into the black fog and did not return.

"One hour," Maya whispered.

She set the telescope down on the bench. Her hands were completely steady now; the tremors of the morning sparring match were gone, replaced by a cold, crystalline stillness that settled over her thoughts like thin ice on a rain barrel.

"Twenty Highland riflemen," she said, looking up at Mac. "Not the sixteen Miller claimed. Ross brought the entire veteran garrison from the Acadia Street barracks. He left the cooks and the sick conscripts behind to hold the armory gate and brought every single man who knows how to shoot."

"Ross knows this is his only throw," Mac said. He walked over to the heavy zinc-lined arms chest bolted beneath the chart table, lifted the lid, and pulled out the two captured PGW Timberwolf .308 bolt-action rifles.

He laid them side by side on the staging bench beside the cedar seed flats. 

The heavy fluted stainless steel barrels gleamed with a faint coat of clear bear-grease; their massive twenty-four-power Schmidt & Bender scopes sat locked in six-screw steel rings, their objective lenses wiped clean of salt film. Beside them, he laid out four ten-round box magazines packed with 175-grain match Lapua ammunition—copper-jacketed, boat-tail hollow-points designed to punch through three-eighths-inch mild steel plate at five hundred yards.

"You take the south embrasure on the gallery catwalk," Mac said, his tone matter-of-fact, as if outlining the morning chores. "The granite parapet is four feet thick. Even a fifty-caliber ball won't penetrate four feet of Mary’s Point sandstone; it’ll shatter on the face. You keep the glass on the trailing boats. When the lead boat hits the spruce boom, Ross’s steersmen will try to back their oars to keep from piling into the wreckage. That’s your window."

"Aim for the rowers?" Maya asked, picking up the first rifle and checking the bolt raceway with a flick of her thumb.

"Aim for the sweeps," Mac corrected. "A twenty-six-foot whaleboat with eight men aboard weighs four tons in a seven-knot tide. If you splinter two ash oars on the port side, the boat will spin broadside to the current in four seconds. The bore will roll them onto the mud shelf before they can clear the tholes."

Maya nodded once. She slipped a ten-round magazine into the rifle’s belly, hearing the heavy steel latch catch with a solid, definitive *snick*. 

She leaned the rifle against the embrasure frame, then reached into her shirt, gently lifted Scar out of her fleece pocket, and set him down on the staging bench beside the sprouting flats of flint corn.

The squirrel looked up at her, his whiskers twitching, sensing the sudden change in the atmosphere. He chirred once, softly, and nudged her knuckles with his warm, dry nose.

"Stay here, Scar," Maya whispered, her voice softening for a split second as she stroked the soft russet fur behind his ears. "Stay in the warm room. Don't go near the stairs."

Scar seemed to understand. He didn't scramble for her sleeve; he hopped into the narrow, sheltered space between the two cedar seed boxes, curled his bushy tail over his nose, and settled down into the soft earth, keeping his dark eyes fixed on the copper door.

Maya picked up her white ash staff in her left hand, swung the heavy Timberwolf rifle over her right shoulder by its padded sling, and walked to the iron door that opened onto the sixty-foot catwalk.

Mac was already standing at the threshold, looking down into the black abyss of the river canyon.

The wind had shifted. It was blowing straight up the estuary from the southwest now—warm, wet, and carrying the deep, unmistakable roar of eighteen million tons of Atlantic salt water charging into the mouth of the red river.

"Maya," Mac said quietly, not looking back.

She paused on the iron sill, the cold salt draft whipping loose strands of her hair across her cheek. "Aye?"

Mac turned his head slightly. In the pale glow of the lantern candle, his dark, weathered face was completely relaxed, his eyes clear and steady.

"Keep your head behind the stone," he said. "Don't rush the shot. Let the river bring them to us."

A slow, fierce smile touched the corner of Maya’s mouth. 

"I won't rush, Mac," she said, her fingers tightening around the cold wood of her staff. "I’ve been waiting six months for these bastards."

Together, they stepped through the iron storm door out onto the wet granite gallery, vanishing into the roaring darkness of the spring flood.

### VI. The Roar of the Cumberland Flood

The sound came out of the southern gloom five minutes before the water appeared.

It began as a subterranean tremor that set the iron gallery deck humming beneath Maya’s boots—a deep, resonant vibration that traveled through forty feet of Mary’s Point sandstone, rattling the copper flashing and stirring the cold salt mist into dancing eddies. 

Then came the roar.

It was not the sound of common river rapids; it was the deafening, concussive thunder of forty-eight feet of Atlantic tide being jammed into the funnel of the Petitcodiac estuary by an ocean that weighed sixty-two pounds per cubic foot. 

Around the lower bend, two miles south, the tidal bore tore through the darkness—a tumbling, eight-foot wall of chocolate-brown water crested with jagged yellow foam, charging up the canyon at fourteen knots. 

Behind the wall of the bore, riding the immense hydraulic surge like hunting gannets on a storm wave, came the eight wooden whaleboats.

Maya lay prone on the damp granite floor of the south gallery, her heavy mackinaw buttoned tight against the salt spray, her white ash staff resting within reach against the parapet wall. 

She had mounted the PGW Timberwolf rifle on its folding Harris bipod in the four-foot notch of the stone embrasure. Through the twenty-four-power Schmidt & Bender optic, the distant flotilla jumped into razor-sharp, illuminated relief against the pale, phosphorescent froth of the churned river.

The boats were twenty-six-foot fisheries cutters, double-ended, their clinker-built cedar strakes pitched black against the red silt. On each boat, six conscript rowers pulled eight-foot ash sweeps in frantic, rhythmic desperation, trying to keep their bows pointed straight ahead into the teeth of the following sea. 

In the bow of each of the first three boats, bolted to a reinforced iron apron over the stem post, sat a heavy fifty-caliber Browning machine gun, its long perforated cooling jacket pointing forward like a black spear. Standing behind the gun on the lead boat was an officer in a khaki greatcoat—Captain Fraser, Ross’s veteran assault commander—holding a short-barreled pump twelve-gauge in his left hand, his face set in a grim, snarling mask of anticipation.

Further back, riding in the fourth whaleboat in the center of the line, Colonel Ross stood erect in the sternsheets, one gloved hand resting lightly on the long oak steering sweep. 

Ross did not shout. He wore his Highland service cap pulled low over his brow, his wool greatcoat buttoned to the chin, watching the towering red bluffs of the lighthouse approach with the cold, calculating detachment of an engineer inspecting a bridge abutment.

Maya adjusted her parallax knob with a gloved finger.

"Lead boat is two hundred yards from the narrows," she murmured into the salt wind. Her voice was flat, ice-cold, stripped of all emotion. "Six knots over the ground. Fraser has the helm. Browning is loaded—I can see the brass belt hanging from the steel feed tray."

Beside her, leaning his forearms on the raw granite parapet, Mac looked down into the dark water. He did not hold a weapon. His broad shoulders were loose, his hands hanging relaxed over the drop, his dark eyes tracking the white froth curling around the submerged reef.

"Let the boom speak first," Mac said.

The lead whaleboat hit the narrow gut.

Captain Fraser was leaning forward over the Browning, his eyes scanning the black timberline above the cliff for muzzle flashes, entirely unaware that eighty feet of red spruce was waiting six inches below his keel.

*CRUNCH-SCREEECH.*

The impact was catastrophic.

At six knots, with four tons of boat, crew, and ammunition pushed by the unstoppable momentum of the spring flood, the lead whaleboat ramped directly onto the port spruce stringer. 

The nine-inch four-sided barbed iron spikes punched through the two-inch cedar strakes beneath the forward thwart with the sound of a bowling ball smashing through a crate of dry shingles. The log held fast, anchored by its 7/8-inch anchor chain into the solid sandstone deadman. 

As the boat tried to ride over the timber, the triangular barbs bit deep into the oak keelson, tearing the bottom out of the hull from stem to sternpost in three terrifying seconds.

The whaleboat did not simply stop; it accordioned. 

The forward thwarts shattered into jagged splinters; the heavy fifty-caliber Browning tore free from its iron apron and plunged into the river like a stone anvil, dragging the gunner down into the churning silt by his canvas ammo harness. Six rowers were hurled head-first into the freezing torrent, their eight-foot sweeps snapping like dry kindling as the forty-eight-foot tide rolled the shattered wreckage over and slammed it down onto the spiked spruce teeth.

"First boat neutralized!" Maya said, her finger resting against the flat shoe of the Timberwolf’s trigger.

The second and third whaleboats were barely forty feet behind, caught in the narrow throat of the gut with four knots of tide pushing them from astern.

"Back water!" the second steersman shrieked, his voice carrying up the cliff face like the cry of a terrified gull. "Back water, you bastards! Back her down!"

The conscript rowers threw their weight onto the sweeps, digging the blades deep into the boiling red current to check their headway.

It was exactly what the river had been waiting for.

Three inches below the surface, running across the shallows between the reef and the mud shelf, lay Maya’s two strands of galvanized Sackville telegraph wire.

As the second boat backed down, the port sweeps struck the taut steel line. 

The high-carbon wire did not break; it bit into the ash blades, whipped around the looms, and bound the oars tight against the forged iron thole pins. The kinetic force of four tons of wooden boat fighting against an immovable steel wire tore the heavy sweeps out of the rowers' blistered hands, shattering their wrists and flipping two oarsmen backward across the center thwarts.

Without oars to balance the head, the second whaleboat broached instantly. 

The fierce, six-knot flood current caught the boat broadside, spinning it forty-five degrees and slamming its cedar flank directly into the jagged teeth of the southern spruce stringer.

*CRACK.*

The side strakes caved in like a wicker basket. The brown, silt-laden water poured over the gunwales in a foaming torrent, swamping the bilge, soaking the ammunition crates, and leaving eight men screaming in knee-deep mud water as the boat grounded hard on the shallow reef.

From the fourth boat, Colonel Ross’s voice cut through the darkness like a whip:

"Cast off the sweeps! Riflemen, clear the bluff! Mount the gun on the third cutter—FIRE ON THE TOWER!"

On the third whaleboat, which had managed to avoid the boom by shearing hard to starboard into the slack-water eddy beneath the western cliff, the machine gunner scrambled to his knees behind his bow-mounted Browning.

He hauled back the charging handle twice, aligned his iron sights on the dark sixty-foot silhouette of the lighthouse catwalk, and clamped his thumbs down on the butterfly trigger.

*THUD-THUD-THUD-THUD-THUD.*

The fifty-caliber machine gun detonated in the narrow canyon with a terrifying, ear-shattering roar.

The heavy, half-inch armor-piercing bullets tore through the salt mist at three thousand feet per second, slamming into the granite face of the lighthouse seventy feet above the water.

*SPALL-SPALL-CLANG.*

Chunks of Mary’s Point sandstone the size of dinner plates were blasted off the exterior wall, showering the lower rocks with red dust and stinging fragments. Three rounds punched through the heavy copper roof of the oil shed, tearing the galvanized iron to ribbons, while two more slammed directly into the four-foot parapet six inches below Maya’s embrasure.

The shock wave hit Maya’s chest like a physical blow, showering her hair and shoulders with powdered rock, but she didn't flinch. 

Her training on the threshing floor held her bone-still. She kept her cheek welded to the synthetic cheek-piece of the Timberwolf, her left hand locking the ash staff flat against the stones, her right eye staring through the illuminated cross-hairs of the German glass.

She centered the reticle not on the gunner, but on the cast-iron pintle socket holding the Browning to the bow apron.

*Crack.*

The Timberwolf barked with a sharp, whip-like report that was swallowed instantly by the roar of the tide.

The 175-grain Lapua match bullet struck the three-quarter-inch mounting bolt of the Browning pintle socket at two thousand six hundred feet per second. 

The tempered steel bolt sheared clean in half. 

The violent, fifty-caliber recoil tore the machine gun out of its ruined mount; the heavy weapon kicked violently upward, jumped over the gunwale, and splashed into eight feet of churning red mud, dragging two linked belts of fifty-caliber ball into the depths behind it.

The gunner screamed, clutching his broken fingers, as the third whaleboat drifted helplessly sideways into the telegraph wire entanglements.

"Gun disabled!" Maya called out, working the slick, mechanical bolt of the Timberwolf with a swift *snick-click*, chambering another match round. "Three boats down, Mac! The trailing four are backing their sweeps toward the mud shelf!"

Mac was no longer beside her on the catwalk.

### VII. The Inhale and the Azure Fire

When Mac stepped out onto the red shale ledge at the base of the sixty-foot bluff, he carried no iron.

He wore only his homespun trousers, his moose-hide moccasins, and his salt-and-pepper woolen henley, unbuttoned at the throat to catch the cold salt mist of the flood. His posture was loose, relaxed, and utterly unassuming—the easy, unhurried stance of an Acadian woodsman stepping out of his kitchen to check the morning wind.

He walked ten paces along the hard gravel seam and stopped twenty feet from where the fourth whaleboat—Colonel Ross’s command cutter—had beached on the high-water mud shelf.

Around the cutter, fifteen Highland riflemen from the Amherst armory had scrambled out into the knee-deep red water, their Lee-Enfield and PGW rifles leveled, their faces pale and wild with the sudden, shocking disaster that had consumed their vanguard. 

In the sternsheets of the beached boat stood Colonel Ross.

Ross’s long wool greatcoat was spattered with red clay, but his Highland service cap was still straight, his service pistol held low in his right hand. He stared at Mac through the swirling mist, his sharp, flint-grey eyes taking in the solitary, unarmed man standing on the stones.

"Stand your ground, old man!" a young militia sergeant screamed from the water, his hands trembling as he leveled a short-barreled pump twelve-gauge directly at Mac’s chest from fifteen paces. "Hands on your head! Hands on your head or I’ll cut you in two!"

Mac didn't raise his hands. 

He didn't reach for a blade; he didn't duck behind the sandstone boulders. He stood tall, his broad shoulders squared against the howling wind, his voice low, steady, and carrying across the roaring water with the calm, uncontested authority of the river itself:

"The river is closed, boys," Mac said. "Your boats are broken. Your guns are in the mud. Turn your sweeps around, climb onto the high ledge, and wait for the ebb. Nobody else needs to die on this shore today."

For two seconds, the only sound was the slosh of the red tide against the cedar planks.

Then Ross’s jaw set into iron. 

"Kill him," Ross commanded, his voice cold and devoid of doubt. "Clear the bank."

The militia sergeant didn't hesitate. He pulled the trigger of the twelve-gauge.

*BOOM.*

A three-inch cloud of orange flame and blue powder smoke erupted from the muzzle, hurling nine copper-plated buckshot pellets across the fifteen feet of open air straight into Mac’s chest.

At the exact instant the hammer dropped on the primer, Mac went dead silent.

He did not flinch; he did not raise an arm to guard his face. 

He drew a single, deep, measured breath through his nose—the quiet, terrifying inhalation of the *Kinap* reaching down through three hundred feet of red bedrock, pulling the ancient, unyielding marrow of *Mntu* straight out of the earth into his blood and sinew.

His chin lifted. 

His head came up, locking his gaze directly onto the sergeant’s face.

In the shadows beneath his heavy brows, his eyes ignited.

It was not a metaphor; it was not a trick of the lantern light. A deep, incandescent, freezing azure blue blazed in his irises—a cold, blinding electric arc burning out of the darkness like sunlight through fifty feet of glacial sea ice, ancient, sovereign, and terrifyingly alive.

*CLANG-PING.*

The nine copper-plated buckshot pellets struck Mac’s woolen henley directly over his heart.

They did not penetrate the wool; they did not break the skin. The lead pellets flattened against his chest like dry peas hitting a five-ton blacksmith’s anvil, mushrooming into useless discs of crushed metal, and dropped harmlessly into the red mud at his feet.

The wool of his shirt wasn't even torn.

The militia sergeant’s jaw dropped. The color drained from his face until he looked like a corpse dredged from the river bed. 

"Sweet mother of spruce..." the young man choked out, his knees buckling beneath him in the mud. "What... what are you?"

Mac didn't answer.

*Violence.*

The transition was instantaneous, terrifying, and completely beyond the physical mechanics of mortal men.

Mac did not charge; he launched.

With a single, explosive spring of his thighs that sent two hundred pounds of red shale flying fifty feet behind him, he covered forty feet of open mud in a single, low-trajectory soaring arc. 

He landed in the middle of the shallows directly in front of the sergeant with the soft, elastic absorption of a panther. Before the empty twelve-gauge hull had even finished tumbling into the water, Mac’s left hand shot out, caught the steel barrel of the shotgun, and closed his fingers.

*SCREEECH.*

The cold-rolled ordinance steel crumpled like a paper soda straw beneath his grip, the barrel flattening into a crushed ribbon of twisted metal. 

Mac didn't strike the boy. With a simple, effortless twist of his wrist, he wrenched the ruined weapon from the sergeant’s frozen hands, swung the stock like a bat, and shattered the gunwale of the nearest whaleboat into kindling.

"Rifles!" Ross roared from the sternsheets, his voice finally cracking with real, desperate panic. "VOLLEY! FIRE ON HIM!"

Ten Lee-Enfield bolt-actions and five semiautomatic rifles erupted in a frantic, uncoordinated fusillade from twenty paces.

*CRACK-CRACK-CRACK-BANG.*

Dozens of high-velocity copper-jacketed .303 and 7.62 rounds ripped through the spray, striking Mac across the shoulders, into the ribs, against his forearms, and across his chest.

Every single bullet failed.

The heavy military rounds flattened into lead coins against his skin, shearing their copper jackets, sparking violently as they ricocheted off his forearms, dropping into the churned foam like spent hail. 

Mac stood in the middle of the gunfire, his eyes blazing that terrifying, cold azure fire, his face completely calm, an immovable monument of living stone that simply refused to acknowledge the existence of lead.

He stepped forward. 

Another thirty-foot leap carried him straight over the heads of the waist-deep rowers directly onto the center thwart of the third whaleboat. 

The two-ton wooden boat groaned beneath his boots, settling four inches deeper into the mud. Mac reached down with both hands, caught the heavy white oak keelson—a four-inch beam of solid cured hardwood—and pulled upward with the slow, hydraulic inevitability of a tidal heave.

*CRAAAACK.*

The oak keelson snapped in half with the sound of a falling tree. The bottom boards buckled upward, the seams split from stem to stern, and the whaleboat collapsed into two halves of floating driftwood, spilling weapons, ammunition, and terrified soldiers into the red mud shelf.

From the high gallery sixty feet above, Maya stood at the parapet, her white ash staff planted firm on the stones, her Timberwolf rifle resting ready. 

She saw the azure fire burning down in the mist—a cold, beautiful star of ancient power moving through the wreckage. She didn't fire; there was no need to fire. The high ground was secure, the flanks were covered, and the river had already done its work.

Down on the mud shelf, Mac walked through the surf toward the command boat.

The remaining Amherst riflemen were throwing their weapons into the water, scrambling up the steep shale ledges on their hands and knees, clawing at the mud in mindless, hysterical terror to get away from the azure-eyed titan who broke steel rifles with his fingers.

Colonel Ross stood alone in the sternsheets of his beached cutter, his service pistol hanging limp at his side. 

His face was grey, his hands trembling as he stared up at Mac. For forty years, Ross had believed in the arithmetic of rifles, the geometry of artillery, and the authority of the uniform. 

In ten minutes on the red river, all of it had been reduced to kindling by a man in a wool shirt.

Mac stepped onto the beached gunwale of Ross’s boat. The timber didn't creak; the river seemed to hold the wood steady beneath his feet. 

The cold azure fire in Mac’s eyes slowly dimmed, the blinding electric light receding into the deep, quiet, weathered grey of his everyday gaze. He looked down at the Highland colonel with that calm, unhurried sadness that belonged to the red sandstone cliffs.

"Ross," Mac said, his voice quiet beneath the roar of the receding bore.

Ross swallowed hard, his Adam’s apple bobbing in his throat. "What... what kind of man are you?"

"I’m a man who lives on this river," Mac said simply. "And you're a soldier who forgot that the land doesn't belong to the army. The army belongs to the land."

He reached out, took the service pistol from Ross’s limp fingers, pulled the slide back to eject the live round into the mud, and tossed the empty frame onto the cedar seat.

"The tide is turning in twenty minutes," Mac continued, pointing south toward the gray mouth of the Cumberland Basin. "Take your surviving dory. Take your wounded. Put your four veteran NCOs on the sweeps and row back to Amherst. If another boat from your garrison crosses the mouth of the Missaguash before the green grass comes, I won't break the wood next time. I’ll break the armory."

Colonel Ross stared at him for a long, silent moment. 

He looked at the shattered whaleboats pinned to the red spruce spikes; he looked at his fifty-caliber machine guns buried in the silt; he looked at his forty conscripts huddled shivering on the shale ledges with their hands in the air.

Slowly, deliberately, the Highland colonel lowered his head in a single, stiff, formal bow of absolute surrender.

"We go," Ross whispered.

---

An hour after the fight, the first pale, golden light of the spring sunrise broke through the salt fog over Shepody Bay.

The river was falling rapidly now, twenty feet of red mud reappearing on the banks as the ebb tide drained the basin toward the Atlantic. Down in the narrow gut, the sixty-foot spruce boom lay unbroken, its nine-inch iron teeth spattered with red clay and splintered cedar, holding the sovereign channel against the world.

Maya walked down the cedar cliff ladder, her white ash staff clicking sure and steady against the wet sandstone rungs. 

She walked across the gravel shelf to where Mac was sitting on an overturned cedar thwart, washing the salt and wood-dust from his forearms in a clear pool of fresh meltwater that trickled from the cliff.

She didn't speak. She sat down on the timber beside him, propped her staff between her knees, and let her shoulder rest against the heavy, solid warmth of his arm.

Tucked into the deep fleece pocket of her shirt, Scar poked his head out into the morning air. He sniffed the clean salt breeze, saw the sunlight shining on the red water, let out a loud, triumphant *chitter*, and hopped down onto Mac’s knee, settling comfortably over his wrist to groom his whiskers in the light.

Mac looked down at the squirrel, a faint, wry smile touching his lips. He reached out with one scarred finger and gently scratched Scar behind his small, tufted ears.

"You held the high glass, Maya," Mac said softly.

Maya looked out across the vast, shining expanse of the red river—at the three captured fifty-caliber Brownings sitting on the gravel, at the crates of dry match ammunition, at the forty conscripts who were already hauling wood for the homestead fires in exchange for bowls of hot gaspereau broth.

She leaned her head against Mac’s shoulder, feeling the deep, slow, unbreakable beat of his heart through his woolen shirt.

"We held the river, Mac," Maya whispered, her fingers reaching down to close over his hand on the cedar timber. "We held the whole damn river."

The sun climbed over the red cliffs of Fort Folly, painting the mudflats in gold and crimson, and the commonwealth of the Petkotkweyak stood sovereign under the sky.
