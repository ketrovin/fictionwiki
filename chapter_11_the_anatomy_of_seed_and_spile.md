# Chapter 11: The Anatomy of Seed and Spile

### I. The Soil of the Lantern Room

The first thaw of late January did not bring rain; it brought light.

It arrived as a sharp, blinding brilliance that angled low through the sixty-foot southern casements of the Old Stone Light, turning the frost flowers on the glass into running rivers of clear crystal. Outside, the mercury crept up from the mid-twenties below zero until the spirit column hovered at minus six—still cold enough to freeze an unmittened finger in four minutes, but to two people who had weathered fifty-below nor’easters inside four-foot granite walls, minus six felt almost tropical.

In the uppermost chamber of the tower—the circular lantern room where three hundred square feet of heavy plate glass offered an unbroken view of Shepody Bay and the frozen loops of the Petkotkweyak—the sun did something remarkable.

It was pure passive solar physics.

The six-sided glass lantern was capped by a conical copper dome, insulated below by two layers of heavy wool felt and double-tapped cedar decking. When the mid-morning sun struck the south-facing glass panes, the trapped radiation could not escape through the triple-laminated storm sashes. By ten in the morning, while the snow outside on the granite gallery remained crusted with ice, the air inside the lantern room climbed to fifty-five degrees Fahrenheit. By noon, with the copper roof absorbing the high radiant spectrum and re-radiating long-wave infrared heat down into the stone core, the temperature hit seventy-one.

Along the perimeter of the curved outer wall, Mac had cut shallow condensation gutters into the zinc astragals with a cold chisel. As the frost melted off the high panes, the runoff did not pool on the floorboards; it trickled along the zinc channels into a pair of copper downspouts that fed a fifty-gallon oak cistern resting on the gallery landing below. Every ounce of water that fell as snow against the high lantern glass was captured, filtered through activated birch charcoal, and held in thermal reserve for the seedlings.

Maya had climbed the seventy-two winding steps of the granite spiral stair at dawn, her right hand gripping her white ash staff, feeling the sharp thermal graduation of the tower. At the base, where four feet of Mary’s Point dressed sandstone met the bedrock, the air was a cool, unmoving thirty-eight degrees—the ideal cellar temperature for keeping forty-two Hubbard squashes, twenty bushels of carrots, and sixteen hundred pounds of flint corn dormant and mold-free. At the thirty-foot level, where the living snug surrounded the cylinder stove, the cedar paneling radiated a steady seventy degrees. And up here, in the glass lantern, the transition was dramatic: thirty-two degrees before sunrise, jumping forty degrees the instant the red disk of the sun broke the horizon over the Tantramar ridges.

She had spent the first twenty minutes of the morning leaning against the south astragal with a brass compass and her field notebook, plotting the sun’s azimuth. At winter solstice, the solar elevation had crested at a meager twenty-two degrees above the southern tree line, throwing long, cold shadows across the river ice. But today, thirty-five days past the turn, the arc had climbed past twenty-seven degrees. The sun was lingering an extra hour and twelve minutes each afternoon, pouring eighty thousand British Thermal Units of pure, unpolluted solar energy into the glass dome before dipping behind the western red spruces.

"Thermal inversion complete," Maya said, resting her calloused hands against the cedar frame of a four-foot potting flat.

She wore a faded flannel work shirt over a sleeveless thermal undershirt, her sleeves rolled to the elbows, revealing the corded forearm muscles she had built over months of hauling split birch, working the treadle stone, and swinging a broadaxe. Her dark hair was wound into a functional knot at the back of her head, secured with a carved bone bodkin. In her right hand she gripped a garden trowel forged from an old leaf-spring tooth, its cutting edge honed on the oilstone until it sliced through fibrous loam like cold butter.

Her white ash staff stood propped securely in the corner between the brass naval telescope and the south astragal, its iron shoe resting flat on the zinc deck. She didn't need it to stand at the bench anymore, but she kept it within reach as naturally as a carpenter kept a pencil behind his ear.

Beneath the hem of her open flannel shirt, nestled snugly inside the woolen cup of her sports bra, Scar was having an identity crisis.

The little red squirrel poked his whiskered snout over the fabric, blinked at the blinding cascade of golden sunlight streaming through the lantern glass, and sniffed the air. The smell of cedar smoke and dry wolf fur was gone; in its place was the rich, intoxicating, humid scent of black earth, decaying leaf duff, and wet worm castings.

To Scar, this was an alarming contradiction. Under the unwritten Acadian Boreal Statutes, warm dirt belonged to May, not January. He scrambled out from the warm fleece of Maya’s shirt—where she carried him tucked against her ribs like an over-indulged tenant in a luxury boreal boarding house—and planted his tiny black paws squarely on the rim of her bra cup. He cocked his tufted ears, flicked his plume of a tail against her collarbone, and let out a sharp, questioning *chuff*.

"Don't look at me like that, you freeloader," Maya said, nudging the tip of his nose with a dirt-stained knuckle. "The calendar says February is coming. Mac says we plant. If you don't like the mud, go back down to the bunk and count your sixty-four stolen butternuts."

Scar leaned into her knuckle for a brief, vibrating scratch behind his ear, then scrambled down her ribs, hopped across the cedar workbench, and planted his front feet directly on the rim of the soil sieve. He reached into the dark loam with one tiny paw, pulled out a crumb of rotten spruce bark, chewed on it with a disgusted grimace, and immediately spat it onto the zinc floor with an indignant chatter.

"He's criticizing your sifting," Mac’s voice rumbled up the spiral stairs.

Mac’s broad shoulders appeared through the trapdoor, followed by the rest of his six-foot-two frame. He was carrying a seventy-pound wooden hod balanced on his right shoulder, filled to the brim with rich, dark forest mold he had hauled up from the bottom of the root cellar vestibule where three barrels of composted spruce needles, crushed mussel shells, and wood ash had spent the autumn curing at forty degrees.

He moved with the effortless, rolling grace of an ocean-going dory riding a Fundy swell. He didn't heave or pant; the weight rested against his trapezius as if it were a bag of feathers.

Mac set the hod down on the central bench with a dull, hollow *thump* that didn't rattle a single pane of glass.

"That's not just dirt," Mac said, gesturing to the contents of the hod with a chin nod. "That is the memory of three hundred years of spruce forest. If you put straight garden topsoil in these flats, the clay from the river silt would bake into red brick under this glass in four days. The roots would suffocate before the first true leaf unfurled."

Maya leaned over the hod, plunging both bare hands deep into the compost. She brought a handful to her nose, inhaling the deep, earthy bouquet of geosmin, fungal mycelium, and forest duff.

"The texture is phenomenal," she said, her agricultural training taking over. She rubbed a pinch between her thumb and forefinger, examining the particle size. "It’s roughly forty percent partially decomposed coniferous litter, twenty percent coarse river sand, twenty percent well-rotted seaweed and fish meal from the autumn weir traps, and twenty percent screened wood ash."

"Auntie Elizabeth’s blend," Mac said, picking up a wooden riddle—a square cedar frame with an eight-mesh galvanized wire screen tacked to the bottom with copper clouts. "The old Acadian grandmothers on the Tantramar dikes learned it from the L'nu three centuries ago. The sea gives you phosphorus, calcium, and potassium from the shells and kelp. The spruce duff provides the acid-loving mycorrhizal fungi that crack the minerals loose. The sand keeps the capillaries open so the water doesn't pool and drown the seed. And the wood ash sweetens the pH just enough so the corn doesn't curl its roots."

Beside the riddle, Maya had laid out three glazed ceramic saucers containing strips of heavy rag paper that had been soaked in boiled red cabbage juice and dried on the stove shelf in October. She took a small glass dropper filled with distilled condensation water, mixed a pinch of the sifted soil in a saucer, and touched a droplet of the clear extract to a strip of purple paper.

The paper turned a faint, pale violet-blue at the margins.

"Roughly six point five," Maya noted, her eyes brightening with clinical satisfaction. "Slightly on the acidic side of neutral. Perfect for maize and climbing beans. At Truro, we had ten-thousand-dollar digital benchtop probes connected to automated nutrient injectors, and half the time the probes drifted by three tenths of a pH point because the calibration buffer expired. A strip of dried cabbage paper and a handful of wood ash does the exact same calculation without consuming a single watt of electricity."

"That’s because the cabbage doesn't lie to impress a grant committee," Mac said with a dry, quiet grin. "Industrial science was always obsessed with speed, Maya. They wanted a tomato to grow in forty days, so they pumped it full of synthetic nitrates until the fruit was ninety-eight percent water and tasted like wet cardboard. Out here, speed gets you killed. We don't want fast plants. We want plants with roots like wire cables that can drink hematite from the shale."

He picked up a flat wooden block with a carved handle—a soil-blocking press he had shaped from a billet of sugar maple.

"Show me the blocking technique," Mac said, stepping back from the bench to give her the working room. "No peat pots. No rotten plastic cell packs scavenged from town garden centers. When the seedling leaves this flat in May, the root ball must be an unbroken cube of living soil that drops straight into the terrace mound without losing a single root hair."

Maya nodded. She had watched him make fifty blocks the previous winter from the drawings in his notebook, but doing it with her own hands was different.

She loaded the four-compartment press with the damp compost, pressing the wooden plunger down with the heel of her palm until moisture beaded along the drainage grooves. She set the press down on the bottom boards of the cedar flat, applied forty pounds of downward pressure with both hands, gripped the side levers, and lifted the mold cleanly with a smooth, vertical motion.

Four perfect two-inch cubes of black earth stood on the cedar wood, each with a neat, half-inch dimple pressed into its top face for the seed.

Mac examined the cubes with a practiced eye. He reached out with a blunt index finger and tapped the corner of the nearest block. It held its shape without crumbling, yet yielded softly to the pressure.

"Good," he said, his voice quiet with approval. "Proper compression. If you pack it too hard, the radicle cannot penetrate the matrix. If you pack it too loose, the block collapses when the watering can hits it. You have the feel for the dampness, Maya. That's eighty percent of the craft."

"I spent three years at Truro calibrating automated greenhouse misters and calculating bulk density on soil core samples," Maya said with a dry, self-deprecating snort. "Who knew all that expensive tuition was just training me to make mud pies in a nineteenth-century lighthouse?"

"The men who built the pyramids made mud pies," Mac countered, leaning against the astragal, his pale blue eyes sweeping the vast white wasteland of the frozen bay. "The men who built the dikes at Grand-Pré made mud pies. An empire that forgets how to handle wet dirt is an empire that starves the first time an oil tanker misses its tide."

He reached into the pocket of his wool trousers and pulled out a small, folded packet of heavy brown wrapping paper tied with twisted nettle cord. He laid it gently on the cedar bench between them.

Maya looked at the packet. "What's in there?"

"The heart of the valley," Mac said.

### II. The Unteachable Line and the Language of the Beast

Maya untied the nettle cord with careful fingers, unfolding the heavy paper.

Inside lay thirty seeds.

They were not the uniform, yellow-varnished dent corn kernels from a commercial seed catalog. These were broad, flat, pearlescent grains of eight-row Northern Flint—some a deep, translucent amber like solidified pine rosin, others stained with streaks of blood-red hematite, and four that were as black and shiny as obsidian chips. Beside them lay sixteen plump, kidney-shaped cranberry pole beans, their cream-colored skins marbled with intricate crimson whorls that looked like miniature contour maps of the Fundy coastline.

"Tuscarora White and Gaspe Flint," Mac said, his voice dropping into the quiet reverence he reserved for living things that had outlasted the centuries. "The Gaspe flint matures in sixty-five frost-free days. It was grown on the north shore of the Baie des Chaleurs when the Little Ice Age drove the growing season down to ten weeks in the seventeen-hundreds. If a late frost strikes in June and an early gale blows in September, this corn doesn't wither. It puts down four-foot taproots into the red mud, stays three feet high, and fills two ears of solid flint per stalk."

Maya picked up a single amber kernel, rolling it between her thumb and index finger. The seed was hard as a river pebble, its outer pericarp thick and vitreous.

"This is the seed we harvested from the twenty-four mounds in October," she murmured.

"The very best ears," Mac confirmed. "Selected from the center of the stalks that stood upright through the hurricane gale. Every seed in this paper knows what thirty-below winter feels like. It carries the chemical memory of the Petkotkweyak soil in its germ. When you plant a seed that was born in the dirt where you sleep, it grows with a different strength."

Maya looked at the seeds, then looked up at Mac.

The giant woodsman was standing in the full wash of the morning sunlight. Through the open collar of his flannel shirt, the heavy muscles of his neck and shoulders were visible—broad, dense, and knotted with a physical mass that didn't belong to the gym-built men of the modern cities. It was the structure of an old-growth red spruce that had spent fifty years anchoring its roots into granite bedrock against forty-knot winds.

She thought of the previous month on the red ice.

She thought of the raider Garret—a desperate, starving predator charging with a six-pound felling axe swinging at terminal velocity—and how Mac had stepped inside that lethal arc with the effortless, unhurried ease of a man closing a cabin door. He hadn't dodged. He hadn't leaped. He had simply raised his left forearm, met the steel momentum with iron bone, and delivered a short, five-inch palm strike that had shattered the man's breastbone and ended the fight in three-tenths of a second.

"Mac," she said, her voice dropping into a quiet, serious register that caused Scar to lift his head from the potting flat.

"Aye?"

"How did you do it?"

Mac picked up a wooden dibble, his thumb smoothing the grain of the ash wood. "Do what?"

"You stopped a six-pound Swedish steel axe with your bare arm," Maya said, her eyes fixed on his. "You didn't have a bruise. I checked your skin that night while you were dressing the venison. There wasn't even an erythema on the epidermis. No swelling. No capillary rupture. By every rule of biomechanics and kinetic energy transfer I ever studied, that strike should have compound-fractured your ulna and driven the radius through the skin of your elbow."

She stepped closer, tapping the bench with her fingers. "I calculated the numbers while I was turning the sumac taps yesterday. A six-pound axe head travelling at thirty feet per second carries roughly one hundred and sixty foot-pounds of kinetic energy. Focused onto a quarter-inch contact patch of bone, that is over two thousand pounds per square inch of instantaneous shearing force. Human cortical bone fractures under six hundred. You didn't just take that blow, Mac. You absorbed it like a concrete seawall absorbing a rowboat."

Mac was silent for a long moment. He set the dibble down on the bench, turned, and looked out through the glass toward the southern ridges where the black timber met the sky.

"Some things are physics, Maya," he said slowly, his voice carrying the low, resonant vibration of the river stones. "And some things are blood."

"Explain it to me," she pressed gently. "You've taught me how to lay up a drystone foundation that doesn't heave in the frost. You've taught me how to read the tension on a dory's ash rib. You taught me how to boil lye from wood ash to turn flint corn into hominy. Can a person learn that? Can a person train their bones to take a blow like that?"

Mac turned back to her. His pale blue eyes were warm, but beneath the warmth was an ancient, immovable boundary—a line as hard and unyielding as the Mary’s Point granite beneath their feet.

"No," he said simply.

The single syllable hung in the warm air of the lantern room, clear and final.

"Why not?"

"Because it is not a technique," Mac explained, leaning his large hands against the cedar potting bench. "A technique can be broken down into lever arms, fulcrums, and muscular contraction. You can teach a man to throw a punch, or swing an axe, or shoot a broadhead sixty paces through the ribs of a deer. That is craft. But what happened on the ice is not craft."

He looked down at his own thick, calloused hands—hands that had split ten thousand cords of firewood, hauled two-ton boulders from the creek bed, and set broken human bones with delicate, millimeter precision.

"My people—the L'nu—have a word for it," Mac said softly. "*Kinap*. It does not mean a warrior who is angry, or a man who has big biceps from lifting iron bars in a basement. A Kinap is born with the weight of the land settled inside his marrow. It is a physical covenant between the human frame and the living bedrock of this continent. When the ground is attacked, the ground answers through the man. The bone does not break because it is not acting as individual bone; it is acting as the granite ridge that has stood against the tides for five hundred million years."

He looked at Maya, his face etched with the gravity of an inheritance that carried no joy, only duty.

"When I was nine years old on the Miramichi," Mac continued in a low, measured cadence, "a pair of logging Clydesdales bolted on a frozen ice skid. The team weighed four thousand pounds, running blind down an eight-percent grade toward a timber crib where my cousins were sorting red spruce logs. My grandfather didn't shout. He didn't run. He just looked at me. I walked out into the middle of the ice, caught the trace chains in both hands, dug my heels into the frozen crust, and dragged those two horses to a dead halt in six feet. My shoulder didn't tear. The bones in my wrists didn't splinter. But when I looked at the horses, their eyes were rolling in white terror—not because of me, but because they felt the mountain standing in the harness."

He shook his head, a faint, melancholic smile touching his graying beard.

"My grandfather took me into the deep woods that night. We sat by a small alder fire until dawn. He told me: *Kwitn, the earth gave you iron bone so you can protect the living things that have no iron. But if you ever use that strength to bully a weaker man, or take what is not given, or play the tyrant over the river, the stone will turn to sand inside your joints, and your heart will freeze before the next moon.* You cannot teach that, Maya. You cannot pass it down in a book or drill it in an orchard. If a man tries to summon it who was not born with that covenant in his blood, his shoulder will dislocate, his heart will burst, and the earth will discard him like a dry leaf. That is my burden to carry alone until I go under the spruce duff."

Maya listened, her breath held tight in her chest. She had heard the old legends from Noel down at Fort Folly—whispers around the summer fires about the ancient giants who could carry a two-man dory over their shoulders through a swamp or uproot a cedar tree with their bare arms when the spring ice threatened a weir. She had thought they were metaphors—poetic exaggerations of a storytelling people.

Now, looking at the man across the cedar bench, she knew they were not metaphors at all.

"Then what *can* you teach me?" she asked softly.

Mac’s face relaxed, the somber weight lifting like mist off the bay. He reached out and tapped the side of her temple with a blunt finger.

"I can teach you how to see," he said.

At that moment, as if summoned by the word, the trapdoor at the top of the spiral stair creaked open.

Nipn stepped into the lantern room.

The gray wolf did not bound up the stairs like a domestic dog; he flowed into the space with the silent, predatory fluidity of liquid mercury. His slate-colored winter coat was thick and immaculate, silvered with frost from his morning patrol around the base of the tower. He stopped five paces from the bench, his golden eyes scanning the room, taking in Maya, the potting flats, the dirt, and the small red creature sitting on the edge of the hod.

Scar did not run.

The squirrel’s reaction to Nipn had undergone an extraordinary evolution over the winter. Back in August, the mere scent of the wolf had sent Scar into screaming, tail-shaking fits of territorial terror. But five months of shared hearth fire, shared venison scraps, and the quiet, sovereign peace of the lighthouse had forged an unwritten treaty between the apex predator of the boreal forest and the tiny tyrant of Maya’s cleavage.

Scar sat upright on his haunches, his round belly protruding over his hind paws. He gave Nipn a short, arrogant *tsik*, turned his back on the eighty-pound wolf with deliberate insolence, and picked up a scrap of dried corn to nibble.

Nipn gave a soft, shuddering huff through his black nostrils, stepped forward, and laid his heavy chin flat on the cedar potting bench three inches from the squirrel’s tail. He closed his eyes, inhaling the scent of the warm soil, completely indifferent to the rodent’s defiance.

"Look at them," Mac whispered.

Maya looked.

"You think they tolerate each other because they are domestic?" Mac asked, his voice low and rhythmic. "Because Nipn is a good boy and Scar is cute?"

"I figured Nipn knows Scar belongs to the house," Maya answered.

"Nipn knows nothing about houses," Mac said. "A wolf does not understand property. A wolf understands field strength. He understands the acoustic and chemical frequency of the mind."

Mac stepped around the bench, standing behind Maya. He did not touch her, but his presence was a massive wall of quiet, steady heat at her back.

"Close your eyes, Maya," he instructed softly.

She hesitated, then let her eyelids slide shut.

The world contracted. Without vision, the other senses flared into sharp, immediate relief: the seventy-degree heat radiating from the lantern glass against her cheeks; the faint, high whistle of the northwest wind shearing off the copper cowl sixty feet above; the humid, mushroom-rich smell of the potting soil; the faint, rhythmic ticking of the zinc floor plates expanding in the sun; and the tiny, rapid *scritch-scritch-scritch* of Scar gnawing his corn kernel on the cedar wood.

"Now," Mac’s voice murmured, hovering just behind her left ear. "Where is your pulse?"

"In my neck," she whispered. "Seventy-two beats a minute."

"Lower it," Mac said. "You are thinking like an engineer who is late for a meeting. You are calculating seed counts, frost dates, and wood consumption. Your brain is generating high-frequency electrical static. To an animal, that static sounds like a screeching iron wheel on a rusty rail. It smells like panic. It smells like a predator that is about to lunge."

Maya took a slow, deep breath through her nose, drawing the warm, earthy air down to the base of her diaphragm. She held it for four counts, then let it slide out through parted lips, releasing the tension in her neck, dropping her shoulders, letting the weight of her body settle down through her heels into the floorboards.

"Better," Mac murmured. "Now stop thinking about yourself. Stop thinking about Maya. What is Nipn doing?"

Maya focused her attention outward.

At first, there was only darkness. Then, beneath the sound of her own breath, she heard it: a slow, rhythmic, cavernous sound. It was the respiration of the wolf—deep, slow, four breaths per minute. As she listened, she realized she could feel the heat radiating from his massive skull, resting on the cedar wood three feet away.

"He is not sleeping," Mac whispered. "He is listening to the river. His ears can hear the ice grinding two miles away in the cove. He can hear the voles moving through the snow tunnels under the living fence. He knows there is an eagle perched on the dead red spruce across the ravine."

"How does he know?" Maya breathed, her eyes still closed.

"Because he is not separate from it," Mac replied. "A modern human walks through the forest like a tourist walking through a museum with headphones on, bumping into the displays. The L'nu walk through the forest like the water that flows between the rocks. When you drop your internal noise to zero, the animals do not see you as an intruder. They see you as a weather system. They see you as a tree that has decided to move."

Maya felt a sudden, strange shift in her chest—a drop in internal pressure, as if an invisible valve had opened between her ribs. The frantic, racing calculation of her mind slowed, smoothing out into a wide, quiet pool of calm awareness.

On the bench in front of her, the gnawing stopped.

Maya felt a tiny, warm weight land softly on the back of her left hand.

She didn't open her eyes. She felt four microscopic claws grip the skin of her knuckles, followed by the soft, damp touch of a tiny whiskered nose sniffing the crease between her fingers. Scar did not chitter. He did not bite. He simply sat on her hand, his little ribcage rising and falling in perfect, synchronized rhythm with her own slowed breath.

Through the contact of his tiny paws, a sudden, vivid impulse flashed across her consciousness—not words, but a rapid, sensory projection: *cold wind... sharp shadow crossing the red spruces... big curved beak... safety of the wool pocket... warm nuts.*

Maya gasped softly. "A red-tail," she whispered. "He saw a red-tailed hawk crossing the ravine."

"Aye," Mac murmured, his hand resting gently on her shoulder. "He’s warning you because he considers your sweater his home territory. If you get eaten by a hawk, his duplex gets cold. In the boreal forest, alliance is practical."

Then, against the side of her right knee, a heavy, warm mass leaned with gentle, deliberate weight.

Nipn had stood up, stepped around the potting flats, and pressed his broad, silver shoulder against her leg. His long tail gave two slow, soft sweeps against the floorboards—a silent, unambiguous greeting of absolute kinship.

"Open your eyes," Mac said softly.

Maya opened them.

The lantern room was blazing with golden winter light. Scar was looking up at her from her knuckle, his bead-black eyes wide and calm, with none of his usual manic frenzy. Nipn was looking up at her from her side, his yellow eyes deep, clear, and peaceful.

And Mac was looking at her, his weathered face softened by a quiet, profound respect.

"You have the ear, Maya," he said quietly. "The strength of a Kinap is unteachable. But the language of the forest is yours. You just have to remember how to listen."

### III. The Anatomy of the Spile and the Sugar Wood

Two hours later, they were down in the ground-floor workshop of the lighthouse base.

The transition from the sun-drenched greenhouse of the lantern room to the stone-walled forge and carpentry shop was a transition from light to iron. The lower chamber had two-foot-wide slit windows that let in narrow shafts of blue-gray daylight, but the room was illuminated primarily by the orange glow of the blacksmith’s forge and the steady, clear flame of two lard lamps hanging from the overhead beams.

The air smelled of coal smoke, hot iron, cured pine shavings, and boiled linseed oil.

Maya stood at the treadle lathe—a massive, timber-framed machine Mac had built from squared eight-by-eight tamarack posts, equipped with a heavy flywheel cast from lead scrap and concrete, driven by a wide leather belt connected to a foot treadle.

Mounted between the iron centers of the lathe was a six-inch billet of seasoned staghorn sumac, roughly two inches in diameter.

"Sumac is the king of spiles," Mac said, standing beside the lathe with a wide gouge chisel in his hand. He pointed to the end grain of the wood. "Look at the pith."

Maya bent close to the spinning wood, her foot keeping the treadle moving in a steady, hypnotic sixty-stroke-per-minute rhythm. The lead flywheel hummed with a heavy, gyroscopic momentum that smoothed out the power strokes.

At the exact center of the sumac branch was a dark, spongy core of soft white pith, five-eighths of an inch wide, surrounded by an outer ring of dense, brilliant golden-yellow heartwood.

"The pith pushes out with a heated wire," Maya said, her eyes tracking the grain. "Leaving a hollow wooden tube that won't crack when you drive it into an auger hole in freezing weather."

"And the sumac contains no bitter resins," Mac added, handing her the gouge. "Pine or spruce would taint the sap with turpentine. Birch rots in three seasons. But sumac is loaded with natural tannins that preserve the wood against fungal rot. A good sumac spile will run forty gallons of sugar maple sap every spring for ten years without fouling."

Maya took the gouge in both hands. She set the bevel against the tool rest, angled the cutting edge forty-five degrees into the spinning wood, and leaned her weight into the cut.

A long, paper-thin ribbon of yellow shavings curled off the billet, smelling of green tea and pepper.

She worked with smooth, practiced competence. In three minutes, she had turned the rough branch into a clean, tapered cylinder, three inches long, tapering from seven-eighths of an inch at the shoulder down to five-eighths at the nose. At the back of the shoulder, she turned a crisp, raised collar, followed by a shallow groove designed to catch the wire bail of a collection bucket.

"Clean shoulder," Mac observed, leaning over to inspect the cut. "That taper will seat dead-tight in a five-eighths auger hole. When the wood absorbs the sap, the sumac expands, locking the spile into the cambium layer without leaking a single drop down the bark."

She stopped the lathe, resting her foot on the timber base. She reached into her pocket, pulled out an eight-inch length of stiff iron fencing wire with one end heated red in the forge coals, and drove it straight through the soft center of the sumac cylinder.

The pith sizzled with a faint puff of aromatic white smoke.

With a quick twist of her wrist, Maya pulled the wire back out. A clean, smooth, hollow bore five-sixteenths of an inch wide ran straight through the center of the spile from end to end.

She blew through the hole. A small shower of gray ash puffed out the far end.

"One down," Maya said, setting the finished tap in a woven willow basket on the bench. "Thirty-nine to go."

"Forty taps is the limit for the western ravine," Mac said, picking up a heavy, two-man crosscut saw from the tool rack and wiping the teeth with an oiled flannel rag. "We have sixty mature sugar maples on that slope, all of them between eighty and one hundred and fifty years old. Under *Netukulimk*, you never tap a tree smaller than twelve inches across. A tree between twelve and eighteen inches takes one spile. An old grandmother tree three feet through can take three, but never more. If you bleed a tree too hard after a severe winter, the crown dies back in June and the tent caterpillars take the rest."

"What’s the sugar ratio this year?" Maya asked, picking up another sumac billet and locking it between the lathe centers.

"It will be high," Mac answered, testing the set of the saw teeth with his thumb. "The summer was hot and dry. The leaves manufactured massive stores of starch in August, and the deep freeze in December drove that starch down into the roots. When the daytime temperature crosses thirty-five degrees and the night drops below twenty, the hydrostatic pressure inside the xylem vessels climbs to twenty pounds per square inch."

Maya’s engineering brain immediately translated the biology into hydraulic math.

"The freeze-thaw pump," she said, kicking the treadle to bring the lathe back up to speed. "At night, the sub-zero temperature creates a negative pressure inside the wood fibers, sucking water up from the unfrozen soil below the root zone. During the day, as the sun warms the bark, carbon dioxide gas expands inside the cellular spaces, generating positive hydraulic pressure that forces the sugar-rich sap out through any wound in the trunk."

"Forty gallons of sap to yield one gallon of syrup," Mac nodded. "Two percent sugar by weight. That means to make twenty gallons of syrup to carry our calories through next winter, we must boil eight hundred gallons of water."

"And the evaporator pan?" Maya asked, turning the next billet into shape. "Are we using the flat galvanized sheet we hauled from the rail depot, or the copper boiler?"

"Neither," Mac said, walking to the corner of the shop where a massive rectangular metal trough stood on wooden horses. "Galvanized sheet releases zinc oxides into the boiling syrup, making it bitter and toxic over long boils. Copper is too thin; it scorches the invert sugars when the flame spikes. We use this."

He struck the side of the trough with his knuckles. It rang with a deep, sonorous *claaang*.

"Quarter-inch boiler plate," Mac explained, running his hand over the welded seams. "Three feet wide, six feet long, eight inches deep. Welded with internal baffles from the steam drum of the old sawmill. The sap enters cold at the front end, flows in an S-curve through three compartments as it thickens, and draws off continuously as finished syrup at the rear drain. We burn dead-standing grey alder underneath. Alder burns with a clear, smokeless flame that holds five hundred degrees across the firebox without dropping ash into the boil."

Maya whistled softly, admiring the layout. "A continuous-feed baffled evaporator. That cuts fuel consumption by forty percent compared to batch boiling in a kettle."

"The old Acadian farmers along the Memramcook perfected it two centuries ago," Mac said with a nod. "Efficiency is not a luxury out here, Maya. Every cord of wood you burn without producing sugar is a cord of wood you have to fell, buck, split, and haul on your back through four feet of snow."

He walked over to a stack of wide birchbark rolls resting on the drying shelf above the forge.

"While you finish turning the sumac, we need to sew twenty more *mokuks*," Mac said, pulling down a roll of prime winter bark.

The bark was a quarter-inch thick, chalky white on the outer face and a rich, golden-amber on the inside, harvested from deadfall paper birches in the high ridge country. It had been scraped clean, soaked in warm water to restore its elasticity, and cut into rectangular patterns with notched corners.

"Seamless folding," Mac demonstrated, scoring the amber face of the bark with the dull back of his skinning knife. He folded the corners up into a tapered rectangular basin, clamping the folded ears against the outside rim with split cedar pegs. "If you puncture the bottom of a sap bucket with a needle, the freezing nights will crack the seam wide open. You fold the bark like origami, clamp the rim with split white spruce root, and seal the top peg holes with a mixture of melted spruce resin, rendered bear fat, and finely powdered charcoal."

Maya watched his thick, calloused fingers manipulate the delicate bark with astonishing gentleness. "The charcoal acts as a filler to prevent the resin from shattering in the frost."

"Exactly," Mac said. "Pure pitch becomes glass at ten below; one knock against a maple trunk and it fractures into powder. The bear fat keeps the plasticizers flexible, and the carbon binds the matrix. A properly sealed birchbark *mokuk* weighs eight ounces, holds three gallons of liquid, and will outlast three generations of galvanized tin pails that rust out along the bottom roll seam."

Maya kicked the treadle, her gouge biting into the fourth sumac billet. "How many collection buckets do we have total?"

"Fifty-four," Mac replied. "Thirty-two folded birchbark buckets from last season, eight hollowed cedar troughs for the big grandmother maples, and fourteen stainless dairy pails we salvaged from the Salisbury cheese cooperative before the raiders burned the depot. That gives us one hundred and sixty gallons of surge storage. When the first big run hits after a hard frost, forty taps can throw sixty gallons of sap in six hours. If you don't have the buckets hung and the gathering sled hitched before ten in the morning, the syrup runs down the bark and feeds the porcupines."

Maya grinned, watching the sumac shavings pile like golden snow around her moccasins. "Then let's make sure the porcupines stay hungry."

"Before we head up the trail," Mac said, wiping his brow with the back of his forearm, "we need to forge the spoon bit for the brace. An ordinary twist drill tears the cambium fibers on the exit wound, leaving jagged splinters that rot and plug the spile. A traditional L'nu spoon auger cuts a clean, polished shaving like a razor, leaving the sap vessels wide open."

He turned to the forge, pumping the hand-cranked leather blower with rhythmic thrusts. A bright, incandescent cone of orange and yellow fire blossomed from the coke bed, casting dancing shadows across the granite walls of the workshop.

From the iron rack beside the anvil, Mac selected a twelve-inch length of high-carbon spring steel—a leaf salvaged from the rear suspension of an old Dodge farm truck. He clamped it in the heavy wrought-iron tongs and thrust the tapered end into the incandescent heart of the coals.

"Spark test," Mac instructed, turning to Maya. "Tell me what we're working with."

He pulled the glowing steel from the fire and touched the corner lightly to the edge of the spinning grindstone. A shower of brilliant, white-hot sparks sprayed into the darkness of the shop.

Maya watched the trajectories with an engineer’s trained eye. "Short, star-bursting trajectories that branch three times before burning out. It’s high-carbon manganese spring steel. Roughly zero point six to zero point seven percent carbon—AISI 5160."

"Aye," Mac nodded with satisfaction, transferring the yellow-hot bar to the face of the anvil. "Tough enough to take the shock of a frozen maple knot without chipping, but holding an edge keen enough to shave hair. Strike when I turn the bar."

Mac took up his four-pound cross-peen hammer in his right hand. Maya stepped to the opposite side of the anvil, gripping the eight-pound sledge in both hands, her feet planted wide on the stone flags.

*CLANG. THUMP. CLANG. THUMP.*

The rhythm was unbroken, hydraulic, and precise. Mac’s four-pound hammer set the tempo, drawing the glowing steel down into a five-eighths-inch tapered half-cylinder, cupping the blade with the rounded cross-peen until it resembled a miniature gouge with an upturned spoon nose. Maya brought the sledge down with smooth, arcing power, her shoulder and latissimus muscles driving the blow straight through the sweet spot of the steel with the clean mechanical authority of a trip-hammer.

Within four heats, the spoon auger was forged. Mac quenched the working tip in a crock of melted bear tallow, the fat hissing with a thick, sweet-smelling cloud of aromatic white smoke that coated the martensitic steel in a protective black oxide skin. He wiped the scale away with an oily rag, checked the bevel against a brass gauge, and tempered the shank over the dying coals until the surface color ran from wheat-straw to a soft, spring-tempered peacock blue.

"Quenched in tallow instead of brine," Maya observed, smelling the rich animal fat. "The slower heat transfer prevents quench-cracks along the thin spoon edge."

"Bear fat gives you the toughest temper on carbon steel in this climate," Mac said, testing the edge against his thumbnail. It peeled a paper-thin curl of horn without dulling. "Salt water cools the steel too fast; it leaves micro-fractures in the crystalline matrix that snap when the temperature hits thirty below. Tallow cools it with an oil cushion that lets the grain settle into deep, tough resilience."

Beside the forge stood the collection sled they had built during the January freeze—a six-foot toboggan framed from steamed white ash poles, its bottom clad with two-inch strips of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene scavenged from the guide rails of the old grain elevator at the rail siding. The plastic runners had been countersunk and fastened to the ash with flush copper rivets, allowing the sled to glide over crusted snow or wet mud with a coefficient of friction lower than wet ice.

"The sled harness," Maya said, picking up the rawhide breast-collar and traces she had braided from deer babiche. "Nipn tested the draft tension yesterday. With a sixty-gallon tank of sap, the hauling resistance on packed trail is less than fifteen pounds of horizontal pull. A single person and the wolf can bring two barrels down from the spruce ravine in twenty minutes."

"That's twenty miles of back-carrying saved every week of the run," Mac said, hanging the newly forged spoon auger in the brace. "A homesteader who wastes his calories carrying water when the snow can float it is a homesteader who burns out before planting season. Efficiency is survival."

### IV. The Ice Patrol and the Acoustics of the Bay

By mid-afternoon, the sun had swung around to the southwest, casting long, pale-gold ribbons of light across the vast, snow-covered barrens of the Shepody flats.

Mac and Maya emerged from the lower entry of the lighthouse, snowshoes strapped tight, bundled against the declining day. Nipn trotted twenty paces ahead, his nose buried in the powder, tracking the wandering trail of a red fox that had crossed the courtyard during the night.

Maya walked with her white ash staff gripped firmly in her right hand. Her pace was brisk, smooth, and confident. The daily discipline of snowshoeing through the heavy drifts had completely rehabilitated the deep stabilizing muscles of her right ankle; where there had once been sharp, stabbing weakness, there was now the dense, elastic resilience of conditioned tendon.

They made their way down the stepped terraces of the garden, past the frozen mounds of the Three Sisters where the corn stubble stuck up through the white crust like rows of miniature palisades, and approached the edge of the river bluff.

Below them lay the Petkotkweyak.

In the afternoon light, the river was a spectacle of terrifying, frozen grandeur. The forty-foot tides were at dead low water. The red silt channel, five miles wide at the bend, was an undulating desert of crushed, rafted ice blocks, frozen mud cliffs, and deep, shadowy canyons where the subterranean currents were rushing out toward the bay.

Mac halted at the edge of the granite ledge, resting both hands on the top of his ash longbow, which he carried unstrung as a walking staff.

"Listen," he said.

Maya stopped beside him, planting her iron-shod staff in the crust. She closed her eyes, remembering the lesson from the lantern room.

She dropped her internal static. She let the tension drain from her jaw and neck, slowing her breath until her pulse settled into a deep, rhythmic thrum.

The acoustic landscape opened before her.

It was not silent. The frozen river was screaming in a hundred different registers, like a vast, subterranean pipe organ being played by an invisible hand.

Far out in the main channel, two miles to the south, came a low, rhythmic *BOOM... BOOM... BOOM...* that occurred every eight seconds. It was the sound of colossal sixty-ton slabs of shale ice grounding against the granite reefs at the mouth of the bay, buckled by the retreating tide and dropping twelve feet into the mud.

Closer to the shore, directly below their feet, the ice was making a high-pitched, metallic singing sound—a rising *ping-ping-ping-shhh* that sounded like steel wires snapping under immense tension.

"The shelf ice," Maya whispered, her eyes opening. "It’s bridging."

"Aye," Mac said, nodding toward the red cliffs eighty feet below. "The mud is freezing at the low-water mark, but the receding tide has pulled the water out from beneath the upper ice sheets. Right now, there is a cavern twenty feet high and forty feet wide running along the base of this bluff, roofed by three feet of red salt ice. It looks solid from above, but it’s an empty dome."

"If a man stepped on that bridge..."

"He would fall through into thirty feet of freezing silt mud," Mac said. "And the next incoming bore would bury him six fathoms deep before he could draw three breaths. That’s what took Garret’s seven men in December. The river doesn't need walls, Maya. It doesn't need barbed wire or machine guns. It only demands that you respect the arithmetic of its water."

Nipn suddenly stopped at the lip of the bluff, his ears cocked forward, his body rigid as an iron statue.

A low, vibrating growl sounded in his chest.

Mac immediately dropped to one knee in the snow, pulling Maya down beside him behind the shelter of a dense juniper bush. Maya gripped her staff with her right hand, her left hand instinctively moving to the front of her Mackinaw coat where Scar was sleeping.

"What is it?" she breathed.

"Across the channel," Mac murmured, his eyes narrowing into cold slits.

Maya looked out across the five-mile expanse of red ice to the eastern bank—the desolate, abandoned shore where the old village of Dorchester Cape lay buried under three feet of unbroken snow.

At first, she saw nothing but the jagged white and red blocks of the pressure ridges. Then, her eye caught movement.

A line of figures was moving along the high-water contour of the opposite bank.

Maya reached into her coat pocket, pulled out the compact brass monocular Mac had given her for her birthday in November, and brought it to her eye.

She focused the lens against the glare of the snow.

There were five of them.

They were not moving like the starving, desperate wretches from Sussex who had staggered down the rail line to die on the ice in December. These men moved with slow, disciplined caution, spaced twenty paces apart in a tactical file. They wore white canvas over-smocks that broke their outlines against the drifts, and on their feet were wide, oval snowshoes that floated cleanly over the crust.

Across the back of the lead scout was slung a modern bolt-action rifle with a heavy target barrel and an optical scope, protected from the frost by a padded canvas sleeve. The two men behind him carried heavy packs lashed to aluminum external frames, their loads covered with waterproof gray tarpaulins.

"Military gear," Maya whispered, her voice tightening. "Look at the pacing. That's an infantry patrol file. They aren't post-holing through the drifts like town scavengers. They have winter kit."

She adjusted the brass knurling on the eyepiece, focusing on the fourth man in the file. "Mac, look at the shoulder flash on his canvas smock. It’s a stenciled black stag over a crossed sword and bayonet. That’s the old regimental badge of the North Nova Scotia Highlanders out of the Amherst armory."

Mac took the monocular from her hand, pressing the small lens to his right eye.

He watched them for two full minutes in absolute silence. His face didn't tighten; his jaw didn't clench. He simply observed them with the detached, lethal calculation of an apex predator assessing an unfamiliar pack that had crossed the watershed.

"Where are they from?" Maya asked.

"Amherst," Mac answered quietly, lowering the glass. "Or what’s left of it. Across the Tantramar marshes. There was a mechanized militia battalion based out of the armory there before the fuel went dry. When the town collapsed, the officers took the heavy equipment, the remaining diesel drums, and the armory supplies into the high stone buildings near the rail junction."

"Why are they scouting this far west?"

"Because their diesel is gone," Mac said, handing the glass back to her. "It’s been six years, Maya. Even stabilized fuel rots after five winters. The batteries are dead. The spare parts are stripped. If they want to survive the next decade, they have to abandon their concrete bunkers and look for territory that can actually feed human beings."

"They're looking for farmland," Maya said, her heart dropping into a cold, hard knot.

"They're looking for the red river," Mac corrected. "They know about the dikes. They know that the silt plains along the Petkotkweyak are the richest soil in eastern Canada. But they also know that crossing the Tantramar in deep winter is suicide unless you have a secure corridor. Look at the fifth man—the one trailing the file."

Maya brought the monocular back to her eye. "He’s carrying an optical transit. A surveyor’s leveling rod lashed to his pack frame."

"They’re mapping the high-water contour," Mac explained, his voice low and analytical. "They want to know if the rail line from Sackville can support a mechanized push, or if the marsh bridges have washed out. For three hundred years, every army that fought over the Chignecto Isthmus—the French at Fort Beauséjour, the British at Fort Lawrence, the New England raiders in the American Revolution—discovered the same brutal arithmetic: you cannot conquer the Fundy basin from the land. The mud swallows your supply wagons, the salt tides drown your infantry, and the fog cuts your throat in the dark."

"Will they cross the ice?"

Mac looked out at the five-mile desert of jagged, groaning bergs and salt-crusted crevasses that separated the two shores.

"Not in February," Mac said with flat certainty. "No man alive can cross five miles of Fundy pack ice on foot without drowning in a tidal cavern. The ice is moving eight knots twice a day. But when the spring thaw comes in April—when the river clears and the dory channels open—they will send boats."

He rose from the snow, his massive frame standing tall against the darkening sky.

"Let them come," Mac said softly. "The bay has broken bigger armies than a squad of militia from Amherst. But it means our prep must be flawless, Maya. We don't just tap the sugar bush for syrup this spring. We tap it to build our stores. We plant every terrace. We trade with Fort Folly for every pound of lead and iron they can spare. And we turn this ridge into an unassailable fortress."

He turned toward the lighthouse. "When we get back to the watchroom, we flash the Clacks to Noel. Five letters: *A-M-H-E-R-S-T*. Noel will put his dory lookouts on the Cumberland Basin. If those scouts try to skirt the south marsh toward Minudie, the Fort Folly hunters will pick up their tracks before they reach the wood line."

Maya stood beside him, her hand gripping her white ash staff until her knuckles turned white.

She didn't feel the terror that had paralyzed her in the railway culvert seven months ago. She looked at the five distant figures across the ice, then looked at the massive stone lighthouse rising behind her, the smoke curling from its warm chimney, the acres of living hedge standing guard over the root cellar, and the giant woodsman standing at her side.

"We have two months before the ice breaks," she said.

"Two months," Mac agreed. "And forty spiles to turn before dark. Let's get back to the lathe."

### V. The Hearth of the Green Sprout

Night fell over the headland with the slow, majestic descent of an obsidian veil.

The temperature outside plunged back toward twenty below, but inside the circular living snug of the Old Stone Light, the world was an island of golden, fragrant sovereignty.

The cylinder stove hummed its steady, low-frequency song, its iron belly glowing cherry-red through the secondary combustion baffle. On the heavy oak worktable, three shallow cedar seed flats sat in the center of the light, illuminated by the warm, clean flame of two lard lamps.

Beside the stove, a flat soapstone griddle rested directly on the glowing coals. On its surface, four round ash cakes—mixed from stone-ground nixtamalized Tuscarora cornmeal, hot spring water, coarse sea salt, and a spoonful of golden bear tallow—were baking to a crisp, speckled golden brown. The smell of toasted corn starch, wood smoke, and hot fat filled the snug with rich, mouth-watering sustenance.

On a wooden cutting board, Maya had flaked two fillets of cold-smoked gaspereau, pink and oily from the autumn run, alongside an earthenware crock of fermented wild leek kraut she had brought up from the root cellar.

Maya sat on the bench, leaning over the flats with a pair of fine, polished bone tweezers in her right hand.

Beside her, sitting upright on a small square of sheepskin laid across the table, Scar was watching her every move with the intense, microscopic scrutiny of an apprentice jeweler.

Maya dipped the bone tweezers into the paper packet, picked up a single, polished kernel of Gaspe Flint corn, and pressed it half an inch into the dimple of the first soil block. With her left thumb, she gently pushed the dark, friable compost over the seed, firming the soil with a feather-light touch.

"Block one seeded," she murmured.

Scar immediately let out a soft, approving *chirr*. He hopped forward two inches, tapped the smoothed dirt with his tiny front paw as if conducting an official building inspection, and looked up at Maya with his head cocked to one side.

"Satisfied, inspector?" she asked with a quiet chuckle.

Scar gave a single, decisive nod of his whiskered head, reached into the small pile of roasted pumpkin seeds Maya had placed on his sheepskin, seized one with his paws, and began to nibble it with furious speed.

Mac sat opposite her, his felling axe resting across his knees. He was running a fine carborundum slip-stone along the curved bit of the Swedish steel head, leaving a bevel that gleamed like polished silver in the lamplight.

*Shhh-wip. Shhh-wip. Shhh-wip.*

The sound of the stone on the steel was rhythmic, soothing, and ancient—the sound of an artisan who understood that an edge was not merely a convenience, but the boundary line between life and death in a world stripped of petroleum.

"Maya," Mac said without lifting his eyes from the stone.

"Aye?"

"Tomorrow, we go up to the sugar-maple ridge."

Maya paused her tweezers over the second seed flat. "The sugar bush?"

"The sugar bush," Mac nodded. "We carry the thirty sumac spiles, the two brace-and-bits, and the cedar collection troughs. We tap the lower thirty trees while the bark is frozen. When the thaw hits next week, the sap will run before the sun clears the tree line."

He set the slip-stone in its wooden box, wiped the steel bit with an oiled rag, and stood the axe against the wall beside his ash longbow.

He pulled open the heavy leather ledger resting beside the oil lamps—the homestead registry where every bushel of grain, every cord of wood, and every seasonal frost date had been recorded in iron gall ink for nine years.

"Seventy-two blocks per flat," Mac said, opening the ledger to a clean page. "Three flats gives us two hundred and sixteen hills of early flint corn. At three seeds per hill, that is six hundred and forty-eight plants. If the July rains hold, those two hundred hills will yield eighteen bushels of grain by the full moon of September. That is nine hundred pounds of dry meal—enough to feed four adults through five months of deep freeze without touching the potato bins."

Maya looked at the precise, architectural script on the parchment. "Arithmetic as a survival mechanism."

"Arithmetic is the only defense against winter," Mac replied, dipping a steel nib pen into the black ink and noting the date: *January 28. First flats seeded. Gaspe Flint and Cranberry Pole Bean.* "The raiders in the towns starve because they live by appetite. They think food is something you steal from a shelf. They don't understand that food is a calculation made eight months before the snow flies."

He walked over to the kettle on the stove, poured two earthenware mugs of steaming Labrador tea steeped with wild wintergreen and dried sweetfern, and brought one to her, setting it on the table beside the seed flats. He plated two hot ash cakes, spooned a heap of the flaked smoked gaspereau and sour leek kraut over the steaming bread, and handed her an ash fork.

"Eat your supper, Maya," he said softly, taking his seat on the oak bench.

Maya took a bite of the hot cake. The crust was crisp and nutty from the bear tallow; the interior was steaming, tender, and rich with the deep, mineral sweetness of nixtamalized maize. The salt and smoke of the gaspereau cut through the richness with sharp, clean perfection.

Scar smelled the warm tallow. He dropped his pumpkin seed, scampered up Maya’s arm, and perched on her shoulder, his black whiskers tickling her cheek as he nudged her ear with a soft, pleading trill.

"Oh, no," Maya whispered, shielding her plate with her wrist. "You don't get smoked fish, you little carnivore. It’ll give you the runs."

Scar ignored the refusal entirely. He reached out with one tiny paw, delicately plucked a single crumb of toasted corn crust from the edge of her cake, stuffed it into his mouth, and chewed with ecstatic, vibrating fury. Satisfied with his plunder, he scrambled down her flannel shirt, parted the fabric with his nose, and dived straight back into the warm, woolen cleft of her bra between her breasts.

He turned around twice, arranged his tail over his nose, let out a tiny, contented sigh that vibrated against her sternum, and fell fast asleep.

Maya smiled—a slow, deep, bone-weary smile of pure, sovereign contentment.

She reached out, picked up her mug of steaming tea with both hands, and took a long, hot sip. The bitter, pine-scented warmth spread through her chest, meeting the living heat of the sleeping squirrel and the radiant warmth of the stove.

Outside, the Petkotkweyak roared in the winter darkness, sixty feet below the granite keep. The red ice buckled, the tide surged through the black channels, and the winter wind scoured the empty barrens of the north.

Across the frozen bay, the scouts from Amherst were bedding down in cold snow holes, shivering over damp twigs and dreaming of meat.

But inside the Old Stone Light, the earth was warm, the spiles were ready, the seed was planted, and the long road to spring was open before them.
