# Chapter 7: The Currency of Firewood

---

### Part I: The First Basin of Mountain Spring

The human body in a state of acute starvation and trauma does not heal in a steady, linear progression; it collapses into deep, unmoving dormancy the moment it realizes it is no longer being hunted.

For the first forty-eight hours after I carried Maya Lin through the trunnion gate, she barely spoke twenty words. 

She lay on the spruce-bough mattress in the east guest room, submerged beneath the heavy red-and-black Hudson’s Bay wool blanket, sleeping with a terrifying, motionless intensity that looked closer to a medical coma than normal human rest. 

Her breathing was slow, shallow, and ragged, her pale face turned toward the cedar wall, her right hand still twitching periodically in her sleep as if her fingers were searching for the taped iron pipe on the floor.

I did not hover over her like a nervous mother, nor did I invade the room with unnecessary chatter. 

An L'nu hunter knows that when a wounded animal finds a dry hollow beneath a granite ledge, the first medicine is silence, cold water, and time.

---

Every four hours, while the sun climbed through the hemlocks, I walked into the room with an enameled tin basin filled with four-degree mountain spring water fresh from the cedar flume.

Her right ankle was a clinical disaster.

The lateral malleolus—the bony knob on the outside of the ankle—was completely buried beneath a hard, hot, dark-purple hematoma the size of a grapefruit. The skin across the swelling was stretched tight as drum leather, shiny and inflamed, with dark streaks of yellow-green ecchymosis spreading down into the arch of her foot and halfway up the calf muscle. 

It was a severe grade-two sprain: the anterior talofibular ligament had been stretched to the point of partial tearing, though when I gently palpated the fifth metatarsal and the fibular shaft with the tips of my fingers, the underlying bone was solid and intact.

"No fracture," I whispered to the quiet room on the morning of Day Forty-Six. "Just thirty miles of running on shredded tendons."

I took strips of clean, boiled linen sheeting, soaked them in the icy mountain water until the fabric was dripping cold, and laid them gently across the hot, throbbing joint.

Maya stirred faintly under the blanket, letting out a long, shuddering, ragged hiss of air through her teeth as the four-degree water pulled the fiery inflammation out of the tissue. 

Her dark eyelashes fluttered once, looking at me through a heavy haze of exhaustion, before her head rolled back into the fir-bough pillow and she slipped straight back into the dark.

---

Beside the basin, on the cedar nightstand, I kept a steaming enameled mug of **L'nu willow and sweetgrass broth.**

In the maritime forest, you do not need a bottle of synthetic Bayer aspirin manufactured in a chemical plant. 

The inner cambium bark of the black willow (*Salix nigra*) contains massive concentrations of **salicin**—a natural, organic glucoside that the human liver converts directly into salicylic acid, reducing systemic inflammation, cooling fevers, and dulling deep ligament pain without tearing up the lining of an empty stomach.

I peeled the fresh green bark from three willow saplings along the brook, bruised the fibers with the flat of the axe, and simmered the bark in an iron pot with dried sweetgrass, chaga mushroom chunks, and two tablespoons of dark maple syrup.

Whenever Maya woke—disoriented, her throat dry, her eyes wide with a momentary flicker of panic—I held the tin mug to her lips.

She drank with small, desperate, bird-like sips, tasting the bitter willow and the sweet maple, swallowed three mouthfuls, and collapsed back into the wool.

And then came the protein.

A starving body cannot rebuild torn ligament collagen on wild tea alone; it requires pure, digestible, bioavailable animal fat and amino acids.

Three times a day, I simmered a fresh fillet of winter flounder or striped bass taken live from the shallow-pit weirs, flaking the tender white meat into a rich, steaming broth of boiled cattail root starch, wild leeks, and coarse Fundy sea salt.

When I brought the first wooden bowl to her bedside on the evening of Day Forty-Seven, Maya propped herself up on her left elbow with trembling arms.

Her hands were shaking so violently from low blood sugar that the horn spoon clattered against the rim of the bowl.

I did not offer to feed her like an invalid. I pulled a low cedar stool beside the bed, set the bowl firmly in her lap, and laid a clean linen cloth over her knees.

Maya took the spoon in her right hand, brought the hot, salted fish broth to her mouth, and tasted it.

She stopped.

She sat completely motionless on the edge of the spruce mattress, the horn spoon suspended two inches from her lips, her dark eyes staring into the steam.

A single, silent tear welled in the corner of her left eye, tracked slowly through the dry dust on her cheekbone, and dropped into the broth.

She did not sob. She did not say a word.

She simply lowered her head and ate—slowly, deliberately, tasting every grain of sea salt, every flake of fresh flounder, and every drop of clean fat, scraping the wooden bowl until the cedar bottom was polished clean.

When she finished, she set the bowl on the nightstand, looked across the sunny room at my face, and let out a single, quiet word:

"Thank you."

"There's twelve more swimming in the weir," I told her, picking up the empty bowl. "Sleep until you're ready to stand."

### Part II: The Color in the Cheeks

By Day Forty-Nine—the fourth day of her recovery—the transformation in Maya Lin began to accelerate with the fierce, stubborn momentum of a woman who refused to be broken.

The human body is an extraordinary machine when you give it sixteen hours of sleep, clean water, and ninety grams of wild marine protein every twenty-four hours.

The pale, waxy, gray-white cast that had made her skin look like parchment when she broke through the ferns was gone. 

Her cheekbones, once sharp and hollowed out by thirty days of caloric deficit, were filling out with healthy, warm color. The dark, purple bruises beneath her eyes had faded to a light amber shadow, and her dark hair, which she had washed in a basin of warm spring water and dried in the morning sun on the porch, hung clean and glossy down her back.

More than her body: **her mind was waking up.**

She was not a passive patient. She had spent five years working high-volume triage in urban emergency rooms and community health clinics, and she looked at her own body with the cold, unsentimental eye of a trauma technician.

On the morning of Day Fifty, I walked into the guest room carrying two **custom-carved white ash crutches** I had shaped for her on the shaving horse.

I had split two straight four-foot billets of seasoned ash, steam-bent the upper arms into gentle, ergonomic curves that fit snugly beneath the armpits, padded the top rests with four inches of felted Mackinaw wool wrapped in soft buckskin, and tipped the bottom ferrules with thick plugs of rock-maple heartwood to grip the moss without sinking.

Maya was sitting upright on the edge of the bed, wearing her clean, dried denim pants and a spare gray wool work shirt of mine that was four sizes too large for her, the sleeves rolled up three turns past her wrists.

Her swollen right ankle was neatly wrapped in a tight, professional, overlapping figure-eight bandage she had constructed using clean linen strips and two split-cedar splint laths I had left on the table.

She looked at the ash crutches in my hands, then looked up at my face with a sharp, calculating tilt of her chin.

"Did you radius the hand grips?" she asked, her voice clear, crisp, and completely devoid of the raspy tremor from Tuesday.

"Three-quarter-inch oval," I said, handing them across the bed. "Grain running vertical. They'll hold two hundred pounds without flexing."

Maya took the crutches, tested the balance across her palms, slid the padded wool rests beneath her shoulders, and pushed herself up from the mattress in a single, smooth, athletic motion.

She stood on her left foot, hovering two inches above the pine floorboards, her core tight, her shoulders square.

Her right ankle hung suspended in its linen wrap, dark yellow and bruised, but completely immobilized.

She took three experimental hops across the room, testing the flex of the ash against the floor, swung her body through the doorway into the sunny main hall, and stopped beside the drafting table.

On the open table lay my large, hand-drawn 1:50,000 topographic chart of the Siknikt basin, with the Four Secure Trails, the shallow-pit weirs, the Old Stone Light, and the deadfall abatis marked in precise, archival black ink.

Maya leaned her weight onto the crutches, bent her head over the map, and studied the lines for two full minutes in absolute silence.

Her dark eyes tracked the red elevation contours, the blue drainage lines of the Petitcodiac, the black grid of the abandoned Intercolonial railbed, and the green boundary of the hemlock hollow.

She looked up from the parchment, looking at me where I stood by the woodstove with a wooden spoon in my hand.

"You didn't just build a camp," she said. Her voice was quiet, intense, and filled with professional awe. "You mapped the entire watershed as a single defensive buffer."

"It's a living map," I said, setting the kettle on the iron plate. "The land was here before the highway was paved, and it’s still here now that the highway is dead. Sit on the porch. The sun is warm, and the tea is hot."

### Part II-B: The Mechanics of the Maker and the Open Book

For the next four days, while her ligament knitted and the ash crutches carried her between the porch and the drafting table, the cabin became a workshop of open, unreserved instruction.

In trauma medicine, secrecy is a symptom of pathology; people who hoard information are usually looking for control. Maya was used to hospital administrators who guarded supply spreadsheets, and desperate men on the road who kept their maps and ammunition counts hidden in locked boxes to maintain leverage over others.

I had no secrets, because in the woods, a secret is just a piece of maintenance that didn't get done.

"If you're going to live inside a system," I told her on the sunny afternoon of Day Fifty-One, leaning an unshaped six-foot ash stave against the porch railing while she sat with her crutches propped on the cedar deck, "you need to know where every drop of water comes from, where every watt of heat goes, and how every defense works. If I take a bad fall on the river shale tomorrow and break my neck, you can't be sitting here wondering which valve opens the water flume."

Maya looked at me, her dark eyes sharp with that intense, evaluating gaze. "Most men would want to keep the keys."

"Most men are fools who think holding the keys makes them important," I said with a dry shrug, picking up a crook knife and a wooden training block. "Holding the keys just makes you a single point of failure. An engineered system needs redundancy. Come here. Let’s look at the spear."

#### 1. The Biomechanics of the Soft Palate (C1/C2 Severance)

Maya swung herself over on the ash crutches, seating herself on the low cedar bench with her splinted foot propped on a dry spruce billet. 

I set an ash bear-spear across the wooden sawbucks in front of her.

"When you came out of the treeline on Day Forty-Five," I said, pointing to the tapered ash tip, "you had five of them behind you. If I had swung a sword or an axe at their foreheads like they do in comic books, I’d have chipped the blade on the frontal bone, jammed the steel in the sagittal suture, and gotten bitten while trying to yank the tool loose. The human cranium evolved over two million years to deflect blunt and cutting trauma from the front."

"The frontal bone is three to five millimeters of dense cortical bone," Maya agreed, leaning forward, her clinical training immediately taking over. "Curved dome geometry. It disperses kinetic impact across the temporal arches. An axe blow will fracture the outer table, but unless you deliver over four hundred foot-pounds of force, you won't penetrate the dura mater reliably."

"Exactly," I said, sketching a side-profile cross-section of a human skull onto a smooth pine shingle with a carpenter's graphite pencil. "So you don't fight the dome. You use the opening that nature left in the bottom."

I drew a heavy dark line entering beneath the upper teeth, angled forty-five degrees upward through the soft palate, directly into the base of the skull.

```
       [ Curved Frontal Bone (Dense Shield / Deflects Blows) ]
                          ┌─────────────┐
                          │   CRANIUM   │
                          └──────┬──────┘
   Spear Angle (45°)             │
 ─────────────────────────▶ [ Soft Palate / Pharynx ]
                                 │
                         [ Brainstem & C1/C2 (Atlas/Axis) ]
                         ──▶ INSTANT NEUROLOGICAL COLLAPSE
```

"The roof of the mouth," Maya whispered, her eyes widening as the anatomical reality clicked into place. "The palatine bone and the thin cribriform plate. There's almost zero structural resistance behind the uvula."

"Nothing but two millimeters of soft tissue," I explained, tapping the pencil. "And directly behind the nasopharynx sits the **foramen magnum**—the great opening where the brainstem exits the skull into the spinal cord at the first and second cervical vertebrae ($C_1/C_2$, the Atlas and the Axis). If an ash point enters the open, snarling mouth and penetrates four inches upward, it completely obliterates the medulla oblongata and severs the motor cord."

"The motor algorithm terminates instantaneously," Maya said, her fingers tracing the pencil line on the wood. "No reflex biting, no dying spasm, no crawling. The peripheral nervous system loses all central input. The limbs go completely flaccid."

"And the best part is the physics," I added, holding up the six-foot pole. "You don't push the spear with your arms. You drop the butt of the ash shaft into a notch in the bedrock or brace it against a surface tree root at a forty-five-degree angle. You aim the tip at the open mouth. The infected is lunging forward at four miles an hour with two hundred pounds of momentum. **It impales itself on its own kinetic energy.** You’re not fighting their muscle; you’re just providing the immutable geometry."

Maya’s eyes moved down the shaft, noticing three specific mechanical modifications she hadn't spotted before.

"What’s this flared collar behind the crossguard?" she asked, touching a four-inch flared cone of hardened, oiled leather riveted to the wood.

"That's the fluid deflector," I said. "Infectious pathogen management. When the point enters the pharynx, cranial fluids and necrotic venous blood spurt under compression. If you have a plain wooden pole, gravity tracks that black slime straight down the shaft onto your grip and your gloves. You get a micro-abrasion on your knuckle, and you’re dead in forty-eight hours. The conical collar acts as an umbrella—it catches the forward spurt and drips the blood straight down into the dirt six feet in front of you."

"And this raised braided ring?" she asked, tracing a dark, hard ridge of pitch-soaked hemp cord two feet down the pole.

"A capillary dam," I explained. "Surface tension breaker. Any stray mist or condensation that creeps past the deflector hits that pitch ring and drips off before it reaches the clean grip zone. The front two feet of the ash are saturated in hot beeswax and bear fat so the wood doesn't soak up fluids like a sponge. When the fight is over, you wipe the steel with an oily rag, burn the rag in the pit, and your hands never touch a single pathogen."

Maya looked up from the spear to my face, letting out a low, admiring whistle.

"Universal blood and body fluid precautions engineered into an ash stave," she murmured. "That’s cleaner than a WorkSafeNB biohazard protocol."

"That's Terry Pratchett's Headology combined with basic germ theory," I smiled. "Don't fight a monster with brute strength when you can let the monster defeat itself with its own stupidity, and never let the monster's fluids touch your skin."

#### 2. The Flume Hydraulics & The River Math

Over the next two days, while I worked within ten paces of the porch, I walked her through every mechanical organ of the homestead:

- **The Split-Cedar Gravity Flume:**
  I showed her the intake basin four hundred yards up the mountain brook: the gravel sediment trap that caught sand and silt before it entered the troughs, the charcoal-and-crushed-sandstone filtration crib that removed organic tannins, and the winter bypass gate that could be flipped during heavy freezes so ice wouldn't burst the cedar joints.
- **The Tidal Weir Dynamics:**
  I explained the sixteen-foot tidal range of the Petitcodiac. I drew diagrams of the willow wing-walls, explaining how the flexible withies bent under the immense hydraulic pressure of the incoming Fundy surge without snapping, and how the retreating ebb tide funneled the migrating striped bass, American shad, and gaspereau into the low-water wicker traps.
- **The Four Secure Trails & Sighting Choke Points:**
  I laid out the entire compass layout of the ridge: the deadfall abatis that forced intruders into open sandstone gullies, the silent tripwire hoof-rattles, and why we never, under any circumstances, walked on the exposed dirt when the moss was dry.

Maya absorbed every word like dry spruce absorbing linseed oil. She asked questions about flow rates, tensile strength, cure times for pine pitch, and the exact caloric yield of salted fish per barrel.

And with every question I answered without hesitation, with every tool I placed directly into her hands, I could see the old, ugly fear in her posture melting away.

She was realizing that in this camp, knowledge was not a weapon to be used against her; it was a shared fire meant to keep the whole house warm.

### Part III: The Unglamorous Trail and the Outhouse Incident

Real post-apocalyptic survival is not an action movie filled with dramatic speeches on rooftops; it is an unglamorous, humbling, everyday struggle with the basic mechanics of human biology.

A human being with a shredded lateral ankle ligament cannot magically hover through the air to use the bathroom.

The cedar outhouse sat fifty yards down the western path from the cabin, tucked behind a thick screen of wild elderberries and young balsam firs, built over a deep, four-foot pit dug into the dry shale of the ridge.

For the first six days, getting Maya from the guest room bed to the outhouse was an exercise in absolute, rigorous, gentlemanly discipline.

She was an athletic, independent woman who had taken care of herself her entire adult life, and being physically carried or supported down a dirt trail to use a wooden latrine was an agonizing ordeal of personal embarrassment that made her jaw clench until her teeth ground together.

I made the routine as clinical, respectful, and unhurried as humanly possible.

I would stand at the guest room door, offer my left forearm as a solid, immovable post, and let her take half her weight on my shoulder while her left foot hopped along the cedar boardwalk. 

When we reached the outhouse, I would help her through the narrow doorway, ensure she was seated securely on the sanded pine bench, step backward out onto the path, and pull the cedar door firmly shut.

Then I would walk twenty paces down the trail, turn my back completely toward the building, fold my arms across my chest, and stare fixedly at the crown of an eighty-foot red spruce tree while whistling an unhurried, off-key tune to provide her with complete auditory privacy.

When she knocked twice on the inside of the pine door, I would walk back, offer my arm, and support her back to the cabin without a single word of commentary.

---

And then came the morning of Day Fifty-Three.

It had rained heavily before dawn—a warm, torrential maritime downpour that had soaked the hemlock hollow and left the green feather moss slick and spongy as a wet fleece.

Maya was feeling the return of her physical strength. Her swelling had dropped by forty percent; the sharp, stabbing ligament pain had settled into a dull, manageable ache; and her natural independence was pushing her to prove she was no longer a burden.

She refused my shoulder on the walk down.

She used the ash crutches, hopping with grim determination down the cedar planks, while I walked five paces behind her with my hands empty, ready to catch her if the wood was slick.

She made it inside the outhouse, closed the door, and latched the wooden hook.

I took my position twenty paces down the path, turned my back to the door, and looked up at the spruce canopy, listening to the morning chickadees.

Two minutes passed.

Inside the small cedar structure, I heard the faint rustle of denim as Maya finished cleaning herself, followed by the dry *scrape* of her left boot shifting on the floorboards as she reached for her crutches to stand up and hitch her work pants.

And then, everything went wrong at once.

Her left moccasin hit a patch of wet clay on the threshold. Her footing slipped. 

In an instinctive, panicked attempt to catch her balance, her injured right foot slammed down onto the floorboards with her full body weight.

The damaged ankle gave way with a sharp, agonizing *snap* of pain.

Maya let out a muffled, strangled gasp of pure shock. 

She pitched forward, lost her grip on the crutches, and before she could grab the doorframe, she **tumbled headlong through the open doorway, rolling down the low timber step and landing flat on her side in the wet green moss.**

She was completely entangled: her denim work pants were pulled down around her knees, her white linen bandage was soaked in mud, her bare hips and pale, bruised legs were fully exposed to the daylight, and she was lying twisted in the ferns, clutching her right ankle, blushing so violently that her face, neck, and ears were the color of a ripe beet.

She was completely, utterly mortified.

---

Twenty paces away on the trail, I heard the loud wooden crash, the thud of a falling body, and the sharp gasp of pain.

My instincts screamed at me to spin around, drop into a combat stance, and draw my spear.

I started to turn—and then my eyes caught the edge of the open doorway, saw the red canvas pants tangled around her shins on the moss, and my brain slammed on the emergency brakes.

I froze.

I did not turn around. I did not take a single step forward to look.

I stood with my back rigidly squared to the outhouse, my chin tilted upward at a forty-five-degree angle, my eyes locked onto the pine needles of the red spruce canopy forty feet above my head.

My ears were burning bright, fiery crimson beneath my dark hair.

I was an L'nu man who respected a woman’s dignity above all things on this earth, and I knew that if I turned around and looked down at her bare, tangled body on the ground, the shame would poison every single meal we ever shared in that cabin for the next ten years.

The forest was dead silent, save for the dripping of the rain off the cedar eaves.

I cleared my throat twice, kept my eyes glued to a brown pinecone on the highest spruce branch, and called out in my calmest, driest, most deadpan, polite voice:

"Can I come help without making you mad?"

---

The silence in the ferns stretched for four agonizing, suffocating seconds.

Down in the wet moss, Maya had buried her burning face into the crook of her left elbow, her knuckles white against the dirt, trembling with a wild, chaotic mixture of excruciating ankle pain, wounded pride, and total, unendurable embarrassment.

She heard my voice. 

She realized that I had heard the fall, that I was standing twenty paces away, that my back was completely turned, and that I was asking for her explicit permission before taking a single step.

A tiny, strangled, half-laughing, half-sobbing sound tore out of her throat.

"Yes," she whispered, her voice muffled by her arm. She cleared her throat and tried again, slightly louder: "Yes... please. Don't look."

"Eyes are shut," I said aloud.

I did not turn my head. 

I took three slow, deliberate, blind steps backward down the trail, extending my hands behind me at waist height until my calloused palms brushed the coarse canvas of her field jacket on the ground.

"Grab my wrists," I told her quietly, keeping my head turned ninety degrees toward the northern ledges. "Pull yourself up to a sit, and get your pants hitched. I'm not turning around until you tell me."

Maya reached up with both hands, gripping my leather-calloused wrists with iron strength.

She hauled her torso upright, gritting her teeth against the throb in her foot, pulled her heavy denim pants up over her hips, buttoned the waist, and cinched her belt tight.

"Okay," she gasped, her voice shaking with adrenaline and blush. "They're on. You can turn around."

I turned slowly.

I did not look at her legs. I dropped onto one knee in the wet moss, scooped her up smoothly into my arms in a single, unhurried, powerful cradle, gathered the fallen ash crutches in my left hand, and stood up.

Maya was pressed against my chest, her face still flaming hot, her dark eyes looking everywhere except my face—staring at the bark of the trees, staring at the sky, staring at the buttons of my flannel shirt.

I carried her down the cedar boardwalk in steady, silent, rhythmic strides, stepped through the cabin door, and laid her gently back onto the spruce-bough mattress.

I picked up the enameled basin, filled it with fresh four-degree spring water from the wash barrel, set a clean linen cloth on the nightstand, and walked back to the doorway.

"Ankle check in five minutes," I said quietly, keeping my tone completely clinical and unbothered. "Take a breath. Nobody saw a thing."

I closed the timber door softly behind me, leaving her to her privacy.

### Part IV: The Ghost of the Highway

By Day Fifty-Six—the end of the second full week—the relationship between us had shifted into a deep, quiet, unspoken rhythm of mutual observation.

Maya was no longer confined to the bed. 

She spent her mornings sitting in the hand-hewn cedar chair on the sunny kitchen porch, her right foot propped up on a clean wooden crate, a pair of leather shears in her hand, carefully trimming and reshaping the sixty-foot ripstop nylon panels I had hauled back from the Salisbury hot air balloon.

She was an extraordinary, meticulous craftswoman.

Her mother had been an expert seamstress in Vancouver, and Maya understood tensile seams, bias tape, and double-stitched flat-felled hems with the same instinctive geometric precision that I applied to timber joinery. 

Using an awl carved from deer bone and high-strength Spectra thread, she was transforming the massive sheets of silicone-coated nylon into four custom storm tarps, two lightweight canoe spray-decks, and three insulated ceiling baffles for the stone lighthouse.

As we worked—me on the chopping block splitting kindling, her on the porch with the nylon—we began to talk through the open air.

---

"My strategy was smart on paper," Maya said on the afternoon of Day Fifty-Seven, her bone awl punching cleanly through the yellow ripstop fabric. Her voice was reflective, quiet, carrying that dark, sharp realism that only medical workers possess. "If you looked at a textbook on societal collapse, my plan was textbook."

I set the kindling hatchet down on the chopping block, pulled a whetstone from my apron pocket, and looked up at the porch.

"Targeted high-value urban salvage?" I asked.

"Exactly," she said, pulling the Spectra thread tight with a sharp *snick*. "I was in the emergency department at the Moncton Hospital when the first cases came in on Day Zero. We thought it was a mutated rabies strain or a weaponized encephalitic neurotoxin. By Day Three, the intensive care unit was a slaughterhouse. By Day Four, the backup diesel generators for the ventilators seized, and the staff who were still alive took whatever supplies they could carry and ran."

She paused, her dark eyes looking out past the wattle fence toward the silent southern ridges.

"I didn't panic," she continued. "I took a tactical trauma pack, three boxes of broad-spectrum cephalosporins, surgical suture kits, iodine, and clean IV sets. My plan was to make surgical, low-profile runs into the independent pharmacies, dental clinics, and medical supply warehouses on the perimeter of town. The high-value goods that people ignore when they're fighting over toilet paper."

"Why did it fail?" I asked gently.

Maya let out a short, dry, self-deprecating breath that was half a laugh.

"Because the town is a biological and human meat grinder, Simon," she said. "The textbooks assume that people behave like rational actors during a disaster. They don't. They behave like drowning rats."

She set the nylon panel down on her knees, her hands resting flat on the fabric.

"By Week Two, the supermarkets turned into unbreathable methane biohazards. You couldn't walk down Main Street without vomiting from the stench of liquefied poultry. And the people who were left..."

Her voice trailed off, dropping an octave, becoming cold, flat, and razor-sharp.

"The dead are simple," she whispered. "A zombie has one algorithm: it sees you, it walks toward you, it tries to bite. You can predict a zombie with high school geometry. But the men on the highway..."

She looked down at the scar on her right knuckle where the skin had been split open weeks ago.

"There was a group at an auto-repair shop near the bridge," she said quietly. "Four men with shotguns. They offered me 'shelter and clean water.' When I saw the ropes on the back room door and tried to leave, they barricaded the exit. I had to break a man’s clavicle with my iron pipe and jump through a shattered bathroom window into an open storm sewer to get away into the dark."

She looked up at me across the sunny yard, her dark eyes filled with the cold, hard, unyielding armor that had kept her alive through thirty days of nightmare.

"They hunted me for two days along the railway corridor," she said. "Not for my antibiotics. Not for my food. They hunted me because in a world with no laws, some men decide that women are just another piece of salvage waiting to be claimed."

I sat on the chopping block, my hands resting still on the maple wood.

My pulse was steady, but my jaw was set as hard as granite.

"That's why you don't enter the town," I said, my voice low and quiet. "The city is a cage. If you step onto the asphalt, you’re agreeing to play their game on their board. Out here, the river sets the rules, the winter does the killing, and the dead do their own running."

Maya looked at the clean, four-degree water splashing into the oak barrel, looked at the eight cords of split maple drying in the sun, and then looked back at my face.

"You didn't just survive the game, Kwitn," she said softly. "You walked off the board entirely."

### Part V: The Currency of Firewood

By the evening of Day Fifty-Nine—two full weeks after she had crawled through the ferns—Maya Lin was whole.

Her right ankle was down to its normal anatomical diameter. 

The dark purple bruising had faded to a light, pale yellow-green; the anterior ligament had scarred down tight and firm; and she had spent the afternoon walking the perimeter of the wattle garden without her crutches, her step light, balanced, and completely free of a limp.

She was strong.

Her dark eyes were sharp, bright, and filled with the fierce, independent intelligence of a woman who had fought her way through hell and had regained full possession of her own physical autonomy.

And as her strength returned, **the tension in the cabin had reached a boiling point.**

---

Maya was not a fool, and she was not naive.

She had spent fourteen days living in an isolated mountain sanctuary. She had been carried in a man’s arms; she had been fed hot, fresh protein three times a day; she had been given clean mountain water, traditional medicine, a dry bed, and forty-five dollars worth of four-point wool blankets.

And for fourteen days, the man who owned the cabin had asked for nothing. 

He had not made a suggestive comment; he had not touched her skin except to adjust a cold linen compress; he had stood with his back rigidly turned to an open latrine door, staring at the sky to protect her modesty; and he had slept every single night on a narrow timber bunk fifty feet away across the hall.

To a modern woman who had just spent a month fighting off predators on the highway, **this was completely terrifying.**

Nobody gives away an oasis in the apocalypse for free. 

The human mind, when conditioned by trauma, cannot accept absolute, unconditional decency; it views unasked-for generosity as a ticking trap, a mountain of unpaid debt that is accumulating interest until the creditor finally decides to present the bill.

---

At eight o'clock in the evening, I walked down the pine hallway into the east guest room, carrying a steaming wooden bowl of smoked striped bass chowder and a fresh tin of sweetgrass tea.

The room was lit by a single beeswax candle on the nightstand.

Maya was not lying down.

She was sitting bolt upright on the edge of the pine bed, her boots laced tight, her canvas field jacket buttoned to the throat, her hands resting flat on her knees. 

Her jaw was set with clinical, terrifying intensity. Her knuckles were white against her denim thighs, her shoulders squared, her dark eyes fixed on my face with the cold, unblinking focus of a surgeon about to make a major incision.

I stepped into the room, set the wooden bowl and the tin mug on the cedar nightstand, and paused.

"Chowder's hot," I said, wiping my thumb on my apron. "Flaked bass with cattail flour and wild leek. Eat it before the fat cools."

Maya did not look at the bowl.

She looked straight through my eyes into the back of my skull.

"Let's stop dancing around it, Simon," she said.

Her voice was cold, steady, and completely devoid of tremor.

I stopped with my left hand resting casually against the pine casing of the doorframe. I blinked once.

"Dancing around what?" I asked.

Maya stood up from the bed. 

She stood on both feet, perfectly balanced, her center of gravity low, her chin lifted with fierce, defensive pride.

"You carried me through the gate," she said, her voice ringing sharp in the small room. "You treated my ligament. You fed me sixty pounds of fresh fish. You gave me clean water. You gave me a bed with wool blankets. In the world out there, nobody does that for a stranger. Nobody."

She took one step toward me, her dark eyes burning with all the built-up terror, suspicion, and defensive armor of the last sixty days.

"I’ve met enough men on the highway to know how this goes," she said, her voice dropping into a hard, clinical whisper. "So let's have it out right now. Are you expecting me to pay rent with my body?"

---

The silence in the small timber room was absolute.

The beeswax candle on the nightstand flickered once as a moth brushed the flame, casting long, dancing shadows across the hand-planed pine wall.

Outside, in the hemlock hollow, the freshwater brook was splashing quietly into the cedar troughs, and a great horned owl let out a distant, lonely *hoo-hoo-hoooo* from the high granite ridge.

I did not move my hand from the doorframe.

I did not cross my arms, I did not take a step forward, and I did not look away from her eyes.

I stood there in my wool shirt and leather apron, looking at this brilliant, fierce, terrified, magnificent woman who had survived a global apocalypse on her own two feet and was currently bracing herself to fight me for her soul.

I blinked twice in mild, genuine, unbothered confusion.

And then, I let out a quiet, dry, self-deprecating chuckle.

It was not a cruel laugh; it was not an arrogant laugh. It was the slow, amused, comfortable laugh of a carpenter who has just been asked if he wants to use a sledgehammer to drive a brass carpet tack.

I shook my head slowly, looking her right in the eye with a small, crooked, respectful smile.

"No thanks," I said, my voice completely calm and conversational. "I’d take some help with the weeding and the firewood, though."

---

Maya stared at me.

Her breath hitched in her throat.

She stood in the center of the room, her shoulders still braced for impact, her fists clenched at her sides, her brain frantically searching for the trap, the hidden angle, the double meaning in the words.

There was none.

There was only a thirty-four-year-old Mi'kmaw carpenter leaning against a doorframe with woodchips in his hair, smelling of pine sap and river mud, looking at her like she was a fellow tradesperson who had just asked a slightly eccentric question on a job site.

"The... weeding?" she whispered.

"The Three Sisters garden is an acre and a half," I said, ticking the items off on my fingers. "The pole beans are choking out the north corn hills. The squash needs thinning. I've got two hundred pounds of summer striped bass down in the shallow weirs that need to be split, salted, and hung in the cold smokehouse before the blowflies catch the scent. And my cousin Chris is bringing sixty pounds of welding scrap and an anvil up the rail trail next week, which means we have four cords of hardwood that need bucking with the crosscut saw."

I looked at her hands—strong, capable, calloused hands that knew how to suture a wound, sew a sail, and wield an iron pipe.

"If you can use an ash hoe and a crooked knife without cutting your fingers off," I added gently, "your rent is paid up until the spring of 2030."

---

The rigid, iron armor that Maya Lin had worn around her heart for two months did not crack.

It dissolved.

She stood in the center of the candlelit room, looking at my open face, and suddenly let out a breathless, startled, half-hysterical laugh that broke through the silence like sunlight through a storm cloud.

She brought both of her hands up to her face, burying her eyes in her palms, her shoulders shaking as the crushing, suffocating weight of two months of fear, suspicion, loneliness, and survival terror rolled completely off her back and crashed onto the pine floorboards.

She wasn't crying from sorrow; she was crying from the pure, staggering, overwhelming shock of finding a safe place in a ruined world.

I stepped back out into the main hallway, giving her the room.

"Eat your chowder while it's hot, Maya," I called through the open door, my voice warm with quiet good humor. "Tomorrow morning at six o'clock, we start on the garden."

I walked down the hall to the workbench, blew out the kitchen lamp, and lay down on my narrow bunk beside the tools, listening to the peaceful, living breath of the house.
