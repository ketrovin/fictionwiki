# Chapter 1: The Day the Clock Stopped Breathing

---

### Part I: The Grain of the Ash and the Silence of the Wire

The battery in the little yellow transistor radio gave out at twelve minutes past two in the afternoon, but not before the announcer in Halifax made a sound like a man trying to swallow his own tongue.

It was not a scream. A scream belongs to a human being who has seen an oncoming truck, recognized the shape of catastrophe, and possesses enough oxygen in the bellows of his lungs to file a loud, futile complaint against the universe. This was something far messier, far more degrading: a wet, hitching gasp that bubbled up through a throat filling with fluid, followed immediately by the dull, hollow clatter of a molded plastic microphone bouncing off a linoleum floor. 

Then came two seconds of muffled, chaotic scuffling—the heavy, dragging sound of winter overcoats being hauled violently across a floor strewn with broken coffee mugs—a sharp, high-pitched inhalation that ended in a wet tearing noise, and then the carrier tone.

The carrier tone was clean. It was pure, indifferent, and electric: a flat, ninety-cycle sine wave generated by a bank of automated vacuum tubes and copper coils sitting in an air-conditioned transmitter vault outside Bedford Basin. The machine did not know that the man who had been speaking into its microphone was currently having his windpipe separated from his collarbone. The machine had been designed by engineers in Montreal to broadcast six thousand watts of frequency-modulated signal across the Atlantic provinces until a human hand flipped the breaker, and since no living hand was left in the studio to touch the board, the machine simply continued to sing its empty, metallic note into the empty blue sky.

I did not hit the radio. I did not jiggle the red plastic tuning dial, nor did I curse at the telescoping aluminum antenna that had been pulled out to its full five-foot extension and propped against the rough-sawn spruce frame of the shaving horse. When a tool stops doing its job, getting angry at the metal is the first sign of a man whose mind has begun to slip its moorings.

I laid my crooked knife down on the white pine shaving bench, the curved blade resting flat on a bed of fresh, curling wood ribbons that smelled of green sap and bitter wintergreen. I reached over, picked up the yellow radio by its molded handle, and slid the grooved battery compartment cover off with the ball of my thumb. 

The two AA alkaline cells inside were unbranded, painted in cheap red-and-black enamel, and they were hot. A thin, crusty ring of white potassium carbonate had already begun to flower around the negative contact spring, weeping out of the crimped steel seal like salt on a drying hide. 

I popped the batteries out with the tip of my thumbnail, wiped the leaking chemical crust against the greasy leather of my work apron, and set them inside an old blue Player’s Navy Cut tobacco tin that sat on the corner of the workbench. 

Batteries were a loan from an empire that had forgotten how to balance its books. If you could not sharpen a tool on a river stone, if you could not steam-bend it from a green cedar shoot, or boil it down from spruce gum and rendered bear fat, you did not truly possess it. You were merely leasing it from a supply chain that lived four thousand miles away on a container ship registered in Monrovia. The moment the ship stopped burning heavy bunker oil, your battery was nothing more than an expensive cylinder of toxic dirt.

I sat back down on the three-legged chopping block, pulled my knees up until the thick denim of my work pants stretched tight across my thighs, picked up the crooked knife, and went back to shaving the belly of the ash spear.

Outside the open three-walled timber shed, the early June sun was sitting high and bright over the crown of the hemlock hollow, pouring warm yellow light through the gaps in the spruce boughs. The light fell across the forest duff in clean, dancing coins, catching the dust motes that rose from my shaving bench with every draw of the blade. 

There were no sirens out here on the creek. 

There was only the dry, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the high-carbon steel peeling paper-thin shavings from the white ash, the sharp *tchic-tchic* of a red squirrel scolding a blue jay forty feet up in the white pine canopy, the cold, gurgling murmur of the freshwater brook twenty paces behind the cabin, and far off to the south, beneath the wind, the low, deep, subterranean sucking roar of the Bay of Fundy pulling half a billion tons of liquid mud and saltwater out of the Petitcodiac basin.

Down in the valley, six miles to the southeast, where a hundred and fifty years of colonial surveyors, municipal bureaucrats, and real estate developers had spent millions of dollars trying to convince themselves that the great river belonged to an English colonel named Robert Monckton, there were undoubtedly plenty of sirens.

There were probably people fighting with tire irons in the aisles of the Walmart on Mountain Road, loading the trunks of leased sedans with forty-eight-packs of bottled water and enough bleached paper towels to wrap an Egyptian pharaoh. When modern civilization catches a fever, its first instinct is never to check the wind, look at the water, or find a defensible ridge. Its first instinct is to pull a plastic credit card through an electronic magnetic reader and purchase three hundred dollars worth of things it cannot eat.

I had planned on being completely done with the town by the autumn of 2028.

My schedule had been drawn up in the back pages of a waterproof field notebook three winters ago: a quiet, unhurried, five-year retreat from the five-day-a-week grind of framing subdivisions for suburban contractors to living on the ancestral land the way my grandfather had lived before the Trans-Canada highway cut the district into asphalt quarters.

Step one was the camp: a low-profile, post-and-beam timber shelter framed from eight-inch red spruce, tucked deep into the hemlock hollow between two granite ledges where the winter gales blowing off Shepody Bay would skip clean over the cedar roof shingles without catching the eaves.

Step two was the water: running a split-cedar gravity flume down from the high mountain spring on the north ridge and sinking the subterranean root cellar four feet deep into the cold, damp clay beneath the frost line.

Step three was the food: weaving the shallow-pit tidal weirs out in the red mud of the river basin, learning the exact lunar calendar of the shad and tomcod runs, and building the wattle garden fences to keep the white-tailed deer from browsing the tops off the flint corn.

Step four was walking into the provincial revenue office on Main Street on a Tuesday morning, laying my registered tax documents on the counter, and informing the clerk that she could forward any future correspondence to the salt marshes.

I was sixty percent of the way through step one, forty percent of the way through step two, and had three weirs working in the mud when the world decided to choke on its own tongue four years ahead of schedule.

"Typical," I muttered aloud. My voice sounded flat and dry in the quiet woods, but it was steady. "Nobody ever checks the tide tables before they throw a catastrophe."

I pulled the curved steel blade toward my chest. The crooked knife sang against the tight grain of the wood, throwing a curling white ribbon three feet long that drifted down to join the fragrant pile between my moccasins.

### Part II: The Name, the River, and the Birchbark Craft

My legal birth certificate, filed thirty-four years ago in a green steel cabinet in Fredericton under the seal of the Lieutenant Governor of New Brunswick, recorded my name in black typewriter ink as *Kwitn Simon*.

*Kwitn*. 

To the white clerks who worked the municipal counters in town, the word was an unpronounceable knot of consonants, something to be squinted at through bifocals before they gave up and called me "Kevin" or "Quentin." 

To anyone who spoke *L’nui’simk*, the word was as clear and ancient as the smell of woodsmoke on a riverbank. 

It is pronounced **Gwee-dn**.

It is the oldest word my people have for a vessel upon the water: the birchbark canoe.

When you are born into the Siknikt district with a name that literally means *the boat that carries people across the river*, you have two choices in this life. You can spend thirty years pretending you are just another guy in steel-toed boots who drives a rusted pickup to a job site and watches hockey on a screen, or you can buy yourself a crooked knife, walk into the black spruce swamps with an axe, and learn the physics of the craft you were named after.

I built my first sixteen-footer when I was twenty-two years old, working in the shade of my grandfather’s barn behind the marsh.

I did not use fiberglass cloth, epoxy resin, or marine plywood from a hardware store. Modern fiberglass is a coward’s material: it is stiff, heavy, smells like chemical poison when you cut it, and when you strike a submerged granite boulder in a fast tidal current, it shatters into jagged white needles that cannot be mended in the bush.

A true *kwitn* is an extension of the living forest.

You go into the mature paper birch groves in late June, when the sap is running heavy between the inner bark and the wood, and you find a tree three feet through the trunk with clean, straight bark free of heavy branch knots. You offer a pinch of tobacco to the roots, cut a single vertical slit down the northern face of the trunk with a sharp crooked knife, and peel the bark off in a single, continuous sheet sixteen feet long and four feet wide, smelling of clean wintergreen and damp earth.

For the ribs, you fell a straight northern white cedar, splitting the heartwood along the radial grain with wooden wedges until you have sixty flexible laths, five-eighths of an inch thick. You build a steam box out of a hollow spruce log propped over a trench fire, boil the cedar laths in the steam for forty minutes until the lignin in the wood softens like boiled leather, and bend them around an oak building frame pegged into the hard-packed dirt.

For the thread, you do not use nylon string. You take a digging spud into the black spruce bogs, pull up fifty yards of *wapnaqn*—the long, tough, trailing root fibers of the spruce—and split them down the center with your teeth while they are still wet. When you sew the birchbark gore seams with spruce root, the fiber swells as soon as it touches the river, sealing the stitch holes tighter than any rubber gasket.

And for the pitch, you melt down two gallons of raw white spruce resin in a cast-iron kettle, stir in a double handful of finely powdered wood charcoal from the hearth to give it body and UV resistance, and temper it with a cup of rendered black bear fat so the pitch remains flexible in freezing water instead of cracking like glass.

When that sixteen-foot canoe was finished, she weighed exactly fifty-four pounds. 

A single man could hoist her onto his shoulders by the center cedar thwart, settle the padded wooden yoke across the back of his neck, and carry her five miles across the rugged portage trails between the Petkotkweyak and the Shediac river basin without stopping to blow his wind. 

She drew less than three inches of water with two grown hunters, four pack baskets of smoked meat, and an iron cookpot in her belly. When you pushed off from the red mud into the brown tidal current, she didn't fight the river; she flexed with the water, bending over the waves like the skin of a living sturgeon.

A car is an arrogant machine. A car demands a four-lane strip of crushed limestone and petroleum bitumen laid across the earth; it demands five hundred gas stations, an electrical grid, a factory in Ontario to make replacement alternators, and an army of men in orange vests to patch the frost heaves every April. The moment the power cuts out, the car becomes a two-ton cage of painted steel that cannot cross a three-foot drainage ditch.

A cedar canoe demands nothing except a man with two arms and a river that knows which way the ocean lies.

If the town six miles away was going to tear its own throat out because the gas pumps stopped humming, the river would not care. The Petkotkweyak had been rising and falling thirty vertical feet twice every single day since the Laurentide ice sheet retreated twelve thousand years ago. The moon pulled the water; the water filled the mud; and a man who knew how to balance on four inches of cedar could travel from the Bay of Fundy to the Gulf of St. Lawrence without leaving a single footprint on the earth.

### Part III: The Geometry of the Bear-Spear

By half-past four in the afternoon, the ash shaft was straight as a surveyor’s laser and smooth as a beach pebble.

I stood it upright against my right collarbone. Six feet, two inches of seasoned white ash, harvested during the dead of winter three years ago from a straight-grained sapling that had grown on the shadowed north side of a granite bluff. 

Wood that grows on the north slope grows slowly: the annual growth rings are packed tight as the pages of a dictionary, making the timber dense, springy, and almost impossible to split along the ring porous boundary. White ash is the absolute king of shock wood. When an ash baseball bat hits a ninety-mile-an-hour fastball, or when an ash spear takes the full charging impact of a six-hundred-pound animal, the long, interlocking cellulose fibers flex, absorb the energy across their entire length, and spring back true, where oak would shatter and pine would snap like dry celery.

The spearhead was an eight-inch leaf blade, forged and ground by hand from a leaf-spring taken off a 1978 Massey Ferguson tractor that had been rotting in a field behind my cousin Chris’s machine shop.

High-carbon 5160 spring steel. 

I had drawn the temper in a bed of hardwood charcoal, worked the bevels on a coarse sandstone wheel turned by a foot treadle, and finished the cutting edges on an Arkansas hard-oil stone until the blade could slice through a sheet of wet birchbark under its own weight. The blade had a thick, reinforced central spine—a distinct diamond cross-section three-eighths of an inch thick at the ridge—tapering down to two razor-sharp cutting wings that met at a needle point designed to penetrate heavy hide without deflecting off bone.

The tenon on the ash shaft had been whittled down to a precision taper with the crooked knife.

I heated a small iron pot of black pine pitch over the woodstove embers, painted the wood until the boiling resin soaked deep into the open pores of the ash, and drove the tapered socket of the steel blade home with three heavy blows of a rock-maple mallet.

Then came the rawhide collar.

I took a two-inch strip of wet deer rawhide, soaked for twelve hours in cold spring water until it was as soft and translucent as fresh tripe, and wrapped it in a tight, overlapping spiral over the steel socket, pulling every wrap with forty pounds of shoulder tension. As rawhide dries, the protein fibers shrink with immense hydraulic force, contracting by nearly twenty percent. When that collar cured in the dry air, it would lock the steel socket to the ash tenon with thousands of pounds of permanent pressure, turning the head and the shaft into an unbreakable, solid unit.

Then I addressed the piece that modern fiction writers always omit: **the crossguard.**

Twelve inches below the base of the steel blade, I used a half-inch Scotch-eye auger to drill a clean, perpendicular hole through the center of the ash shaft. 

Through that hole, I drove a five-inch toggle bar of seasoned rock maple—dense, heavy, shaped like a cigar, with rounded ends that would not catch in the hazel brush. I lashed the maple toggle crosswise against the shaft using twenty feet of braided #36 tarred bank line, painting the cordage with hot pitch until it set like hard black rubber.

The bear guard. The boar toggle.

Terry Pratchett wrote once that the trouble with heroes is that they are generally very loud, remarkably stupid, and almost never live to see the harvest. 

In every post-apocalyptic book, comic, and television show I had ever browsed in the used bookstores in town, the survivors fought the infected with six-inch hunting knives, aluminum baseball bats, or fire axes. 

They fought like Hollywood stuntmen, and they died like idiots.

They ignored basic mechanics.

If you thrust a straight, unbarred spear through the chest of a charging two-hundred-pound animal—or a two-hundred-pound human corpse driven by an unfeeling viral motor—the creature does not politely stop moving simply because it has cold steel through its lungs. The momentum of its mass carries it directly down the length of the wooden shaft, sliding along the timber until its face is twelve inches from your nose. 

By the time the dead man realizes his heart is no longer pumping, his teeth have already met through the muscle of your neck.

The maple crossbar changes the physics entirely.

When the blade enters the target, the crossguard hits the breastbone, collarbone, or rib cage with a dead, immovable stop. It arrests the entire charging momentum of the body five feet away from your chest. It allows the hunter to brace his weight against the earth, use the creature's own kinetic energy to complete the penetration, and maintain absolute, unbreachable standoff distance without ever allowing a snapping jaw within arm's reach.

"Standoff," I said to the empty shed, testing the balance of the spear across the palm of my left hand. The point hovered level, three inches above the dirt. "Never wrestle what you can hold at five feet."

I picked up my crooked knife, slid it into its waxed leather sheath on my left hip, slung the willow fish basket over my right shoulder, and turned toward the trees.

Before I checked the shallow-pit weirs down on the mudflats, I needed to climb the ridge.

I needed to see what the town looked like when the clock stopped running.

### Part IV: The Reconnaissance of Wheeler Boulevard

The northern spur of the **Dobson Trail** started eighty yards behind the camp, cutting through the hemlock hollow along the western bank of the brook before climbing steeply through a sandstone fracture onto the high ridges of Albert County.

It was an ancient trail. Long before the town below had been laid out in rectangular grids of asphalt, long before the first Acadian settlers had built their sod dikes along the Petitcodiac marshes in the seventeenth century, the L'nu had walked these high ridge routes to travel between the tidal waters of Siknikt and the southern coastal highlands of the Bay of Fundy.

The forest here was mature, deep, and heavily shaded. 

The eastern hemlocks and red spruces were two centuries old, their massive trunks rising three feet thick before branching into a dense, interwoven canopy eighty feet above the earth. Beneath that canopy, the forest floor was free of heavy underbrush: just a rolling carpet of green feather moss, brown spruce needles, and low clusters of wood ferns.

I did not walk down the center of the path where modern day-hikers had worn the soil down to hard-packed red clay. 

Clay is slick when damp, and on a dry day, a heavy boot heel striking hard ground makes a dull, hollow *thump* that carries through the soil for two hundred yards.

I walked the soft shoulder of the trail in my oil-tanned, double-soled moose-hide moccasins.

When you walk in traditional moccasins, your relationship with the earth changes completely. You do not stomp forward on your heels like a man in steel-toed work boots on a concrete sidewalk. You walk with a low center of gravity, landing lightly on the outside of your midfoot, rolling your weight inward across the ball of the foot with your knees slightly bent. 

Your toes feel the ground through the leather before you commit your full weight. If there is a dry spruce twig hidden beneath the needles, you feel the tension before the wood snaps; you shift your balance two inches to the left, step on the damp moss, and pass through the trees without making a louder sound than an owl shifting its wings on a branch.

It took twenty-five minutes of steady, silent climbing to reach the northern terminus of the spur, where the hemlock ridge ended abruptly in a steep, forty-foot sandstone bluff overlooking the southern bypass of Wheeler Boulevard.

I stayed four paces back inside the deep, cool shadow of an ancient hemlock, dropped onto my right knee in a thick patch of ostrich ferns, and brought the ash spear across my thighs.

---

Down below, the modern world was tearing its own throat out in the boiling afternoon sun.

The four-lane highway bypass, where it crossed the old Canadian National rail corridor, was a catastrophic, smoking graveyard of crushed fiberglass, twisted aluminum, and shattered safety glass.

A heavy tractor-trailer hauling fifty thousand pounds of refrigerated milk and butter had jackknifed across the median, its aluminum trailer split open down the side like a burst paper bag. Thousands of white plastic gallon jugs had spilled across the baking asphalt, bursting under the impact and spreading a vast, white, frothing lake of milk across all four lanes that was already beginning to sour and curdle in the thirty-degree heat.

Three passenger sedans, a commercial plumbing van, and a pickup truck hauling an aluminum boat trailer had plowed into the wreckage at seventy miles an hour, their front ends telescoped into a crumpled, smoking knot of steel that was burning with a thick, sooty, oily black plume that rose straight into the blue sky.

The sound that rose from the road was deafening.

It was not a human sound; it was a wall of mechanical rage. Dozens of drivers, trapped in the miles-long gridlock backing up toward the Petitcodiac river crossings, were leaning their chests and forearms against their steering wheels, filling the valley with a relentless, blaring chorus of electric horns that echoed off the concrete sound barriers like a thousand screaming saws.

People were running through the lanes between the stalled cars—men in business shirts with their ties ripped off, women dragging screaming toddlers by the wrists, people carrying plastic laundry baskets piled high with canned soup and boxes of cereal. 

Across the street, at the glass-fronted convenience store beside the Irving gas pumps, a mob of forty or fifty people was actively tearing the steel security bars off the front doors with a heavy chain hooked to the bumper of a pickup truck. The glass shattered with an explosive *crash*, and the crowd surged through the jagged opening, punching and clawing at each other over cases of bottled water, potato chips, and cartons of cigarettes.

And then, from behind the burning milk tanker, the first of the dead came through the smoke.

There were five of them.

They did not move like the sick people you see in hospitals. They did not limp with the cautious, protective posture of a human being nursing an injury.

They moved with a terrible, uncoordinated, mechanical momentum—a frantic, jerking shamble that was faster than a walk but lacked any of the graceful balance of a living runner. Their clothes were shredded, soaked in dark, dried stains that looked like black crankcase oil across their chests. Their arms hung wide, their fingers hooked into rigid claws, and their heads were tilted upward at awkward, unnatural angles, their jaws hanging completely open.

A man in a navy blue suit stepped out from behind an idling minivan, carrying a steel tire iron in both hands. 

He was shouting—a frantic, terrified, high-pitched scream, telling them to back away, telling them he was armed.

The foremost corpse did not pause. It did not slow down to assess the weapon. It simply lunged forward on its heels, throwing its entire two-hundred-pound weight directly into the man’s chest.

The man swung the tire iron with all his strength.

The steel bar caught the corpse across the left side of the jaw with a sickening, wet *crack* that broke the bone and sent a spray of dark, coagulated fluid across the white paint of the minivan.

The corpse did not even blink. It did not register pain. It did not lose its balance. 

Its broken jaw swung sideways on a hinge of torn skin, but its momentum never faltered. It slammed the man back against the hood of the car, pinned his arms with its dead weight, and buried its broken teeth into the soft meat of his throat, tearing upward with a violent, animalistic wrench of its neck.

The man’s scream died in a wet, bubbling gurgle.

From fifty yards down the gridlocked lane, someone leaned out of the window of a commercial flatbed truck and began firing a semi-automatic handgun—wild, panicked *bang-bang-bang-bangs* that cracked through the air, punching holes through the sheet metal of surrounding cars, shattering a rear windshield, and sending the looting mob at the gas station stampeding in all directions across the boiling highway.

I knelt in the ferns, watching through the spruce needles.

My pulse was thrumming in my temples, but my breath remained slow, cold, and rhythmic in my chest.

*The Idiot Plot.*

Terry Pratchett had coined the term in one of his essays: a story that only works because every single participant behaves with the absolute minimum amount of common sense required to keep the disaster moving forward.

Every single human being on that asphalt was participating in the plot. 

They were clinging to the two-ton metal boxes that had trapped them; they were clustering in the bottlenecks where the dead were thickest; they were firing irreplaceable brass cartridges into bodies that could not feel pain; and they were fighting each other for cardboard boxes of processed food while fifty feet away, the freshwater creek was flowing cleanly toward the sea.

"Nope," I whispered softly into the spruce boughs. "Not participating."

I rose smoothly to my feet, stepped three paces backward into the deep shade until the rock lip of the bluff cut off the sightline to the burning road, and turned my back on the town.

I did not run. Running makes noise, clouds the judgment, and wastes glucose. 

I simply walked the soft, moss-cushioned deer path back into the ancient green silence of the hemlock hollow, letting the sound of blaring horns, burning rubber, and dying men fade behind the eternal murmur of the running water.

### Part V: The Shallow-Pit Weirs of the Petkotkweyak

Where the freshwater brook cut through the southern sandstone bluff and widened out into the tidal basin of the Petkotkweyak, the air changed completely.

The smell of burning plastic and gasoline vanished, replaced by the clean, sharp, ancient scent of the maritime coast: wet red mud, rotting bladderwrack kelp, high-tide sea salt, and the rich, mineral-heavy aroma of exposed river loam.

The tide was three hours past the peak of the flood, the brown water receding rapidly toward Shepody Bay at a speed of eight knots, exposing hundreds of acres of gleaming, wet red mudflats that curved around The Bend like a vast sheet of hammered copper beneath the late afternoon sun.

Fifty yards out from the high-water mark, sitting in the intertidal zone, were my three **Shallow-Pit Tidal Weirs.**

---

Modern white culture views fishing as an act of sportsmanship: an individual with an expensive graphite rod, a mechanical reel, and a box of painted plastic lures, standing in cold water for eight hours trying to convince a single fish to bite a piece of steel.

My ancestors had looked at the Bay of Fundy five thousand years ago and realized that the river was already doing all the work. You did not need to hunt the fish; you merely built a room in the mud, opened the door when the tide came in, and closed it when the tide went out.

I had spent two months of grueling, back-breaking labor constructing these three weirs during the spring neap tides.

I had felled seventy straight black spruce and northern white cedar poles, four inches thick and six feet long, sharpening the heavy butts into chisel points with the felling axe. Walking out onto the mudflats at dead low water, I drove those posts four feet deep into the stiff, heavy river clay using a twenty-pound wooden pile maul, setting them in two long, converging wings that formed a wide V pointing downstream toward the bay.

Between the upright posts, I wove dense wattle panels made of fresh alder shoots and green balsam fir boughs, lashing the brush tight with tarred bank line.

When the thirty-foot Fundy tide surged up the river—a muddy, brown wall of water that rose at the rate of an inch every thirty seconds—it completely submerged the weirs by ten or fifteen feet. The fish (striped bass, flounder, tomcod, and sea-run brook trout) swam freely over the top of the brush, feeding on the rich marine worms, sand shrimp, and crustaceans in the flooded shallows.

When the tide turned and began its violent, rushing retreat back to the open ocean, the water filtered quietly out through the porous wattle wings, but the fish were guided down the converging funnel of the V toward the apex.

At the apex of each weir, I had excavated a circular basin four feet deep and eight feet across, digging through the soft silt down to the hardpan blue clay. I lined the floor and walls of the basin with smooth, flat river stones hauled from the creek bed.

I walked out along the elevated cedar-plank catwalk that stretched from the high-tide bank to the center of the mudflats, the seasoned planks creaking softly under the soles of my moccasins.

I reached the apex of Weir Number One, knelt on the timber platform, and looked down into the stone-lined basin.

Even with the main tide three miles away down the bay, two and a half feet of cold, clean, highly oxygenated seawater remained trapped in the rock pool.

Three large winter flounder—flat, mottled brown against the sandy floor of the basin, their two eyes gleaming on the upper side of their heads—were resting quietly on the bottom, their gills pulsing with slow, rhythmic efficiency. 

Beside them, hovering near the stone rim, was a fat, twenty-inch striped bass. Its silver scales were gleaming under the surface, the clean black horizontal lines along its flanks distinct and sharp, its tail fin making gentle, sweeping adjustments against the pool's gentle vortex.

They were alive. They were calm. They were completely fresh.

---

This was the critical genius of the shallow-pit design.

If I had caught these fish in a standard nylon gillnet or a commercial trap, they would have suffocated six hours ago when the tide dropped. They would have baked in the thirty-degree June sun on the exposed mud, their delicate oils oxidizing, turning rancid, and filling the river valley with a foul, rotting fish stench that would have drawn every gull, eagle, coyote, and wandering corpse in the district straight to my landing.

In the stone retention pit, the fish were swimming in clean, cool water. 

They could stay alive in that basin for four days without losing a single gram of protein. If a roaming pack of infected wandered along the high bank, or if a three-day summer rainstorm pinned me inside the cabin, I did not lose a single calorie. My pantry was swimming in the river, completely fresh, odorless, and silent, waiting for me to dip a net into the pool whenever I was ready to eat.

*Netukulimk.*

The foundational law of our people: take only what the land offers, take only what you need for the day, treat the living creature with absolute respect, and leave the rest swimming for tomorrow.

I knelt on the plank, took my hand-woven spruce-root dip net from the basket, and lowered the mesh into the pool. 

I scooped the middle flounder out of the water with a single, smooth, lifting motion. The flat fish gave one wet, heavy flap against the spruce mesh before I brought it onto the cedar catwalk, placed the flat of my left palm firmly over its eyes, and pressed the sharp tip of my crooked knife cleanly through the base of the skull behind the gills.

The flounder stiffened once, shivered across its dorsal fin, and went completely limp.

No panic. No lactic acid souring the meat. No wasted pain.

I rinsed the blade in the cool water of the pool, packed the fresh fillet between two thick layers of wet sweetgrass in the bottom of my willow basket, and stood up to wipe the sweat from my forehead.

That was when the blue jays up on the high ridge behind the landing began to scream.

### Part VI: The Anatomy of the First Kill

A blue jay is the gossip and the alarm bell of the Acadian forest.

If a barred owl is roosting in the dark hemlocks during the daytime, the jays gather in the upper branches and make a persistent, rhythmic, scolding *churr-churr-churr*, annoying the predator until it shifts its perch. If a red-tailed hawk glides across the clearing, the woods go dead silent in a split second, every bird freezing beneath the canopy.

A blue jay only screams—that raw, metallic, tearing screech that sounds like brass plates being ground together in a vise—when something large, heavy, and completely uncoordinated is crashing through the hazel brush like a falling log.

I did not jump. I did not scramble.

I stepped quietly off the cedar catwalk, backed four paces into the dense shadow of a massive spruce blowdown on the high-water bank, and lowered myself onto my right knee in the damp ferns.

I set the willow fish basket behind a mossy root where the sweetgrass would mask its scent, brought the six-foot ash bear-spear across my chest in both hands, and waited.

Twenty yards up the bank, where the deer trail emerged from the alders, the brush parted with the dry, splintering snap of breaking wood.

---

The man who came through the hazel bushes had been dead for roughly twenty-four hours.

He had been a road worker in life: he was wearing heavy denim work pants stained with asphalt tar and diesel grease, and a faded yellow-and-orange high-visibility vest that had been ripped down the left ribcage, exposing gray, torn flesh and dried black blood that looked like tar against his ribs. 

He was missing his left work boot; his bare foot was swollen, purple-gray, encrusted with dried red clay and pine needles, dragging uselessly behind him as he lurched forward. His left arm was hanging limp at his side, the humerus dislocated from the shoulder socket and turned outward at an ugly, unnatural ninety-degree angle.

His face had already completed the first stage of the post-mortem transition. 

The skin was a mottled, waxy gray-green, stretched tight across the cheekbones, the lips pulled back dryly against the teeth in a permanent, rigid snarl. His eyes were milky, clouded with the opaque white film of dead corneas that had dried in the wind, but they were tracking. 

He did not look at the birch trees. He did not look at the sky. 

He caught the scent of the fresh flounder in my basket on the light breeze blowing off the mudflats, or perhaps his dead ears picked up the faint splash of the striped bass in the weir basin below the bank.

His head snapped toward the creek with a wet, clicking sound in his upper cervical vertebrae.

A low, dry, vibrating rattle tore out of his chest—the sound of air being forced through dead, dried vocal cords by the involuntary muscular spasms of an infected diaphragm.

He did not stop to look at the footing. He did not see the steep red clay bank.

He threw his shoulders forward, his good right boot driving hard into the dirt, lunging down the slope directly at my head with all the blind, unthinking momentum of a falling boulder.

He was moving at roughly four miles an hour—heavy, clumsy, with his jaw hanging completely unhinged, opening six inches wide to expose his blackened gums and broken teeth.

He was aiming his mouth directly at my face.

---

*Open wide for the dentist.*

I did not stand up. Standing up raises your center of gravity and gives away your balance on a muddy slope.

I stayed on my right knee, planted the flat, seasoned butt of the white ash spear firmly against the massive, exposed taproot of the ancient hemlock behind me, and locked the heel of my right moccasin over the wood until the spear was anchored directly into the bedrock of the hill itself.

I gripped the ash shaft with both hands—my left hand forward, palm down, four inches behind the maple crossbar; my right hand back near my hip, locking the timber into my core.

I angled the high-carbon steel point upward at a thirty-degree angle, centering the leaf blade directly in the middle of his open, gaping mouth.

The dead man took two sliding, chaotic strides down the clay, his good boot slipping in the silt, his entire body weight falling forward under the acceleration of gravity.

He lunged mouth-first onto the point.

The high-carbon steel blade entered the open mouth cleanly, with zero deflection.

There was no resistance from the skull dome—just the soft, wet, muffled *thunk* of razor-sharp steel slicing through the thin membrane of the soft palate, punching through the porous bone at the base of the cranium, and severing the brainstem and C1 vertebra with a sharp, distinct *crack* that vibrated through the ash shaft like a struck tuning fork.

The rock-maple crossguard caught his collarbone and sternum with a dull, heavy shock.

The impact drove the hemlock root into my heel, but the spear did not give an inch.

The rattle in the dead man’s throat died the microsecond the steel severed the central nervous system.

The frantic, clawing motion of his hands stopped mid-swing. The motor impulses in his legs vanished completely; his knees gave way, his torso folded forward over the crossbar, and he hung there, suspended five feet from my chest, an empty suit of clothes filled with dead meat, pinned to the hemlock root like a beetle on a collector's pin.

I took a slow, deep breath of the salt air through my nose. 

My pulse was thrumming in my forearms, but my hands were steady on the wood.

"Rule number one," I whispered to the dead man. "The skull is a helmet. The mouth is an open door."

I kept my left hand on the shaft, brought my right moccasin up, placed the thick moose-hide sole firmly against the center of his high-visibility vest, and shoved with my leg.

The high-carbon steel blade slid smoothly back out through the shattered teeth with a faint, wet scrape.

The body folded sideways, slipped off the grassy bank, and rolled down into the thick, bottomless red mud of the tidal basin with a soft, heavy *thump*.

The Fundy mud went to work immediately.

The red clay silt of the Petitcodiac basin is a non-Newtonian fluid: when a heavy, unmoving object falls into it, the mud liquifies under the initial shock, then settles around the mass like wet cement. Within sixty seconds, the corpse had sunk six inches into the red clay, anchored by its own dead weight.

In three hours, the thirty-foot tidal bore would come roaring up the channel from Shepody Bay—twelve miles an hour of churning brown water that would submerge the mudflats under twenty feet of ocean, rolling the body along the gravel bed, out into the deep salt channels where the dogfish, crabs, and bottom scavengers would have the bones stripped white before the weekend.

The blue jays on the ridge watched for another minute, decided the show was over, and flew off toward the north spring to hunt for beetles.

I pulled a handful of clean, coarse marsh grass from the bank, wiped the high-carbon steel blade until the metal was bright and dry, and inspected the rawhide lashing on the maple crossguard. 

The wood was sound; the pitch hadn't cracked; the steel was true.

I picked up my willow fish basket, checked the angle of the sun dipping behind the western hills, and turned back toward the hemlock hollow.

### Part VII: The Hearth, the Ledger, and Captain Vimes

By eight o'clock in the evening, the forest was fully dark.

Inside the cabin, the heavy hemlock plank door was barred with a four-inch timber of seasoned white ash, dropped deep into the hand-mortised oak brackets on either side of the frame. 

The small cast-iron woodstove was ticking quietly in the corner, its draft damper closed down to a quarter-turn, burning split sugar maple with zero visible smoke leaving the stone-lined chimney flue. On the flat iron top of the stove, an eight-inch cast-iron skillet was sizzling with two thick fillets of fresh flounder, seasoned with coarse rock salt and a pinch of dried wild leek flakes from last spring’s foraging cache.

Beside the stove, a single beeswax candle was burning in a brass saucer, casting a warm, steady, yellow circle of light across the pine plank workbench.

I had laid my tools out in clean, oiled rows: the crooked knife, the drawknife, the whetstones, the file, and my three fresh field notebooks with their waterproof grid pages.

I opened the top notebook, dipped my archival drafting pen into the black inkwell, and drew a clean, precise, scaled cross-section of the southern bypass intersection, marking the three-car pileup, the prevailing wind direction, the location of the deadfall barrier I would construct across the Dobson Trail spur tomorrow morning, and the precise depth of the tidal weir basin.

At the top of the page, beneath the date, I wrote four lines in neat, draftsman's block letters:

> **DAY ZERO: THE COLLAPSE**  
> *Grid Failure: 14:12 AST. Halifax carrier tone dead.*  
> *Water Pressure: Estimated 48–72 hours remaining in municipal towers.*  
> *Highway Status: Lethal bottleneck. Avoid all paved surfaces.*  
> *Weir Status: 3 flounder, 1 striped bass retained. Netukulimk intact.*  
> *Defense Status: First contact dispatched on mudflats. Spear geometry verified.*  

I set the pen down in the brass groove, took a sip of the hot chaga and sweetgrass tea from my tin mug, and leaned back into the hand-hewn cedar chair.

From the shelf above the bench, my hand reached out naturally, bypassing the heavy military engineering manuals and tactical survival guides, and pulled down a battered, dog-eared paperback with a brightly illustrated cover: Terry Pratchett’s *Guards! Guards!*.

I opened it to page seventy-two, where Captain Samuel Vimes of the Ankh-Morpork City Watch was explaining his socioeconomic theory of boots: how a rich man could spend fifty dollars on a pair of good leather boots that would keep his feet dry for ten years, while a poor man who could only afford ten-dollar cardboard-soled boots would spend a hundred dollars over the same time and still have wet feet every winter.

I took a bite of the flounder—hot, flaky, sweet with the clean taste of the cold salt river—and listened to the night wind outside the timber walls.

Far off to the south, beyond the quiet hemlocks and the stone ridges, a single car horn was still blaring in the ruins of the town, lonely and stupid, running its battery down to zero in the empty dark.

I turned the page, adjusted the candle, and went back to reading.
