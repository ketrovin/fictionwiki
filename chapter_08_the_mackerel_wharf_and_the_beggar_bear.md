# Chapter 8: The Mackerel Wharf and the Beggar Bear

### Days 58–60 (Mid-August, Year 1)
**Location:** The Old Rail-Ferry Terminal Wharf & The Creek Camp, Siknikt  
**Protagonist:** Kwitn "Gwidn" Simon  
**Companions:** Maya Lin, Nipn (Eastern Timber Wolf), The Fundy Black Bear  

---

### I. The Iron on the Spur (Day 58)

The morning fog over the marsh was not the thick, gray grease of early spring, but the clean, sweet mist of mid-August that smelled of ripening blackberries, tidal cordgrass, and drying pine needles. 

At four in the morning, long before the sun had cleared the ridge behind the stone light, the gravel trackbed along the river was already ringing with the muffled, rhythmic thump of heavy rubber tires and the dull slide of wooden runners.

Kwitn stood by the rail siding, checking the lashings on the two massive 150-quart commercial marine coolers strapped across the center deck of the pump-car. They were thick-walled, rotomolded beasts of white polyethylene, scavenged weeks earlier from an abandoned seafood packing plant down near the wharf. Between them sat four 20-gallon split-spruce firkins lined with coarse Tantramar gray salt, two coils of 50-pound braided handline, a canvas sack of seasoned green alder smoking chips, and Mac’s flat iron cooking griddle.

Maya walked out of the cabin path carrying two heavy canteens of cold spring water and a tin box of dried thyme and sea salt. She was wearing her high-laced leather boots, her canvas pants tucked neatly into wool gaiters, and her ash walking stick gripped loosely in her left hand. 

She walked without a hitch. The swelling in her ankle had vanished completely, her stride even and balanced on the loose track ballast.

"You're loading enough ice chests to outfit a commercial trawler, Simon," she said, setting the canteens in the center crate.

"Mackerel don't make appointments," Mac said, pulling a taut-line hitch tight on the manila rope. "When the schools hit the ferry slip on the falling tide, you either catch two hundred pounds in four hours or you spend the winter chewing on dry turnip and wishing you had oil in your belly. Mackerel is fat. In January, when the wind comes off the ice at thirty below, fat is the difference between sleeping warm and waking up with your ribs showing."

Maya looked down the long, straight pair of oxidized steel rails that cut through the spruce woods toward the coast. "How far to the old ferry slip?"

"Seven miles down the spur," Mac said. "Slight downgrade most of the way. Coming back with three hundred pounds of smoked fish is where the exercise happens."

He walked to the rear of the handcar. The heavy ash walking-beam handle was already seated in its cast-iron rocker box. He reached down to the main pinion gear with a tapered iron drift punch, knocked the heavy bronze linchpin into place, and locked it with a spring cotter. 

"Step up," he told her, offering a steady hand. "Nipn’s already running the brush."

A low gray shape drifted out of the alder thicket ahead. Nipn trotted onto the centerline of the tracks, shook the morning dew from his thick silver ruff, and cast his yellow eyes down the steel rails. With a single soft puff of breath, the wolf broke into an effortless, loping trot, pacing twenty yards ahead of the car as their forward scout.

Maya stepped onto the wooden deck of the handcar, gripping the crossbar of the walking beam. Her hands were small against the thick ash handle, but her grip was sure and steady.

"How does the rhythm work?" she asked.

"Simple physics," Mac said, taking his place on the opposite side, facing her. "When my side goes down, yours comes up. Don't fight the rise. Just let the beam lift your hands, and when it peaks, drop your weight through your heels. We don't arm-wrestle it; we let our body weight do the pumping."

"Got it," she said. "Push and drop."

Mac released the foot-lever brake. The heavy maple friction blocks pulled back from the iron wheel rims with a dull wooden clunk. 

"Ready?"

"Ready."

Mac leaned forward, dropping his chest and driving the ash beam down. The car shuddered, the bronze journal bearings whined in their grease, and with a heavy *chunk-chunk, chunk-chunk*, the iron wheels bit the steel rails. Maya met the counter-stroke with perfect timing, sinking into her knees and letting her weight drive her handle down as his rose.

Within fifty yards, the heavy handcar found its momentum. The early morning wind rushed through the spruce canopy, washing cold salt air over their faces as the tracks rolled smoothly under the iron wheels.

### II. The Bottleneck at the Old Slip

The old rail-ferry terminal sat on a long, rocky spit jutting out into the deep tidal channel of the bay. 

Decades ago, before the modern highway bridges and causeways had been built, massive steel-hulled rail ferries had berthed here, taking entire freight trains across the water to Prince Edward Island. All that remained now was a graveyard of heavy timber cribbing, towering black creosote pilings, rusted steel mooring dolphins, and the weathered clapboard ruins of the old ticket office and freight sheds.

The tracks ran straight down the spit onto the main pier, flanked on both sides by eight-foot-high chain-link fences topped with rusted razor wire—a narrow, sixty-yard cattle-chute built by harbor authorities during the chaotic early weeks of the collapse to control the desperate crowds trying to reach the last outgoing boats.

Mac stood on the foot brake three hundred yards out, easing the handcar to a slow, silent glide.

Nipn stopped at the head of the fenced chute, his nose raised high in the offshore wind, sampling the salt air that blew off the mudflats. The wolf dropped into a low crouch, his ears swiveling toward the abandoned town houses clustered on the ridge half a mile to the west, then looked back at Mac and gave a single, quiet wag of his bushy tail.

"Track's clear," Mac murmured. "Wind is blowing hard out to sea. That's our insurance policy. Anything moving in town is downwind; they won't smell the smoke or hear the water."

They rolled the handcar down the narrow corridor of fences, the iron wheels clicking softly over the rail joints until the bumper logs of the ferry slip brought them to a gentle stop at the edge of the deep water.

Before he touched a single basket or line, Mac reached into his leather tool roll. He pulled out the tapered iron punch and his small copper hammer. With two sharp, muffled taps, he backed out the bronze linchpin from the walking-beam drive crank, slid the pin into his coat pocket, and lifted the long white-ash pump handle completely out of its cast-iron rocker socket.

He laid the ash handle into the deep belly of the empty cooler and clicked a heavy brass padlock around the wheel spokes and the rail.

Maya leaned against the rail, watching him with an amused smirk. "You really don't trust anybody, do you?"

"I trust people to look for an easy ride," Mac said mildly, wiping a trace of sheep tallow from the gear teeth. "A rolling handcar on clean rails is an invitation for every lazy wanderer within five miles. But a dead two-ton sled of solid cast iron with no pump handle and a locked wheel? That's not a getaway vehicle. That's a back injury. If someone wants to steal three hundred pounds of steel, they're welcome to drag it on their hands and knees."

He walked to the outer gate of the chain-link fence—the single entrance connecting the rail spit to the mainland. In the center of the latch, tucked discreetly behind a tangle of wild rose hips, he hung a smooth, polished Fundy agate wrapped in waxed hemp cord—one of Maya’s fully charged pain stones, brimming with the compressed, fiery memory of twenty-four hours of acute ankle trauma.

"Thief-bait," Mac muttered, making sure the stone hung right where an uninvited hand would reach for the latch pin. "Anyone fumbles that gate barehanded, they'll get a reminder to mind their own business."

Maya shook her head, though her eyes were warm. "You're wicked, Kwitn Simon."

"I'm just a carpenter who hates uninvited company," he said with a grin. "Now let's build a chimney."

### III. The Driftwood Kiln & The Green Alder Rungs

At the far end of the wharf, sheltered from the spray by a stack of weathered salt-creosote timbers, sat an old 55-gallon steel oil drum that had been cut in half by dock workers years ago for burning trash.

Mac inspected the drum. The steel was heavy, thick, and lined with a clean crust of sea salt rather than petroleum sludge. 

"Perfect," he said.

Working together with the calm, practiced rhythm they had developed over the past month, they turned the wharf into a mobile curing station:

1. **The Firebox:** Mac set four flat beach stones in the bottom of the drum to elevate the firebed, using dry driftwood cedar slivers and birchbark to start a small, coal-hot base fire.
2. **The Smother Chips:** Once the cedar coals glowed cherry-red, he smothered the flame with wet, green alder branches cut from the marsh edge and sweet apple-wood chips from his canvas sack. A dense, thick, fragrant plume of pale blue-white smoke rolled up from the drum, caught the offshore breeze, and drifted out over the wide, open bay away from the shore.
3. **The Smoking Rungs:** Across the top rim of the drum, Maya laid twelve green alder rods spaced three inches apart, perfectly notched to hold thirty split mackerel by their tails without slipping into the coals.

"Fire's breathing," Maya said, resting her palm near the rim to gauge the temperature. "Warm, slow, and sweet. Not hot enough to bake the meat, just right to dry the oil."

"That's the trick with mackerel," Mac said, opening the wooden tackle box. "You bake 'em too fast and all the rich oil runs into the fire and burns. You want the smoke to kiss the fat until the skin turns into copper leather."

He pulled out two fifty-pound spools of braided green nylon handline. Each line was rigged with a heavy four-ounce lead torpedo sinker and four fluttering sabiki lures—small, sharp stainless hooks dressed with iridescent white fish skin and silver tinsel that looked identical to frantic sand-eels in the murky tide.

He handed one wooden spool to Maya. "High water just turned twenty minutes ago. The tide is ripping around the old ferry pilings. That's where the baitfish get pinned against the timber. Drop your lead right into the back-eddy, let it sink until the line goes slack, then jig it up three feet and let it flutter down."

Maya took the line, her fingers feeling the rough, honest bite of the braided nylon. "Let's see if the trauma nurse can catch breakfast."

### IV. The Silver Boil

She stepped to the edge of the weathered timber wharf, sixty feet out over the swirling green depths of the bay. 

The tide was racing out, creating a massive, foaming eddy behind the cluster of giant creosote pilings. She unspooled the heavy lead sinker and dropped it.

*Whirrrrr-zip.*

The nylon peeled off the wooden spool between her fingers, slicing down through the green water. Ten feet. Twenty feet. Thirty feet.

*Slack.*

The lead hit the bottom mud. Maya clamped her thumb on the spool, lifted her arm high in a sharp, three-foot jig, and let the lures flutter down on the current.

Before the lead even settled, the line was nearly torn out of her hand.

*THUMP-THUMP-THUMP-THUMP.*

The nylon went vibratingly rigid, singing against the wooden rail like a struck guitar string. The heavy pull wasn't the slow, sulking drag of a bottom flounder or the steady weight of a cod—it was a manic, frantic, high-frequency shudder of pure muscle and speed.

"Mac!" Maya yelled, her boots planting firmly on the deck planks. "I got something! Multiple somethings!"

"Don't let 'em have slack!" Mac shouted, stepping up beside her with his line already in the water. "Haul hand-over-hand, smooth and steady!"

Maya hauled. The line came up dripping cold sea foam. Her arms worked with fierce, athletic rhythm, hauling the heavy braid hand-over-hand over the gunwale timber.

Up through the emerald green water came a thrashing, foaming hurricane of silver, electric green, and shimmering violet. 

All four hooks were buried in the jaws of four massive, fourteen-inch Atlantic mackerel. Their backs were marked with vivid, tiger-striped bands of dark iridescent emerald, their bellies pure polished silver, their sickle-shaped tails vibrating so fast they were a blur of spray.

"Look at them!" Maya laughed, swinging the thrashing string of fish over the wharf planks. They hit the wooden deck with a manic, rhythmic *slap-slap-slap-slap*, throwing diamond beads of saltwater across her canvas trousers. "Four at once!"

"Told you," Mac said with a broad grin, his own line suddenly snapping taut with a violent jerk. "The whole school is jammed right under the timber cribbing!"

Mac hauled his own line up, bringing four more fat, thrashing torpedoes over the side.

Within forty-five minutes, the wharf was a whirlwind of rhythmic, joyful work:
* Maya dropped, jigged, and hauled, her eyes shining, her laughter echoing over the open water as string after string of glittering mackerel came flying over the rail.
* Mac stood at the split-table—an overturned heavy wooden fish crate. With his razor-sharp carbon-steel split-knife, he worked with terrifying speed: one slice behind the gills to bleed, one slit down the belly to clean the entrails directly into the tidal current below, and one smooth, shallow cut down the spine to split the fish flat into a butterfly fillet, ready for the brine.
* Cleaned fish were dipped in a bucket of cold sea brine, rubbed with a handful of Tantramar salt, and handed directly to Maya to string onto the green alder smoking rungs over the steel drum.

By ten in the morning, the first sixty mackerel were hanging in the sweet, thick alder smoke, their skins turning a rich, shimmering amber-bronze, the fragrant fish oil sizzling quietly as it dripped onto the cedar coals.

### V. Maya at the Griddle (*"Let Me Cook"*)

By noon, the tide had ebbed to its lowest point, exposing vast, glistening red-mud flats beneath the wharf pilings. The frantic bite of the school slowed as the fish followed the deep channel out to the open bay.

Maya’s forearms were glistening with salt spray, her sleeves rolled up past her elbows, her knuckles red from the cold sea water. She wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, looking down at the two massive 150-quart coolers. 

One cooler was already half-full of golden, hot-smoked kippers laid flat between clean layers of dry cedar shavings. The other cooler held eighty pounds of fresh, gutted mackerel packed in coarse gray salt.

Mac was still bent over the cleaning bench, his shoulders working as he split the last dozen fish.

Maya walked over, reached out, and caught his wrist. Her fingers were cold, but her grip was firm.

"Simon," she said, her voice dropping into that calm, authoritative tone she used when ordering an orderly around an ER bay. "Put the knife down."

Mac blinked, looking up at her with grease on his cheek. "I’ve only got twelve more to dress—"

"The fish aren't going anywhere. You've been on your feet bending over that crate for four hours straight without a drink of water. Sit down."

"Maya, I can finish the batch—"

"I didn't ask," she smiled, gently prying the handle of the split-knife from his fingers and laying it clean on the pine bench. "You’ve done all the heavy lifting, you pumped the handcar seven miles, and you built the smoker. I’m not here to be a decorative passenger. Let me cook."

Mac looked at her steady, determined eyes, then down at his own aching lower back. He let out a long, quiet breath, took off his grease-stained work gloves, and sat down on an overturned spruce nail-keg with his back against the sunny side of the ticket shed.

"Yes, ma'am," he said mildly.

"Good boy," Maya teased.

She turned to the small bed of hardwood coals Mac had maintained beside the main smoke drum. She set the heavy, seasoned cast-iron griddle directly over the embers until the iron gave off a dry, steady hum of heat.

From the clean wash-bucket, she picked four of the freshest, thickest mackerel—each over a pound and a half, with firm, cold flesh that smelled like the deep Atlantic ocean and cucumber.

Using Mac’s small crooked carving knife, she scored the iridescent green skin with tight diamond crosshatches so the intense heat wouldn't curl the edges. She rubbed each fillet with a pinch of coarse Tantramar salt, cracked black peppercorns from her pouch, and a dusting of dried wild sweetfern leaves she had dried in the stone light.

She laid the fillets skin-side down onto the sizzling iron.

*SSSSSSSSSSSS-Tssssss.*

The sound was an explosive, mouth-watering hiss. The thick layer of rich omega fish fat under the skin rendered instantly, bubbling into a golden, frying froth that crisped the skin into dark, crackling lace. The aroma—sweet, oceanic, savory, and rich as butter—drifted up into the cool sea breeze.

She flipped the fillets with the flat of her blade. The underside was pure, snow-white meat, firm and flaking in juicy tiers.

She scooped two sizzling fillets onto a wide, fresh-split slab of red cedar and handed it to Mac along with a wooden fork.

"Eat," she ordered, sitting down beside him on the low timber curb with her own steaming cedar plate. "Before the grease gets cold."

Mac blew on the steaming fish, broke off a generous flake of crisp-skinned white meat, and lifted it to his mouth.

The skin shattered with a delicate, salty crunch, releasing a flood of rich, piping-hot, savory fish oil that coated his tongue, balanced perfectly by the earthy pine-sweetness of the wild herbs and the mineral punch of the Tantramar salt. 

Mac closed his eyes, chewing slowly. A quiet, deep sigh of pure satisfaction rumbled in his chest.

"Well?" Maya asked, watching him closely with a small, expectant smile.

"Maya," Mac said honestly, looking at her with genuine reverence. "If you served this in a restaurant in Halifax, people would pay fifty dollars a plate and thank you for the privilege."

Maya let out a rich, throaty laugh, leaning her shoulder against his as she took a bite of her own fillet. "It’s not magic, Simon. It’s just fresh fish and hot iron. But... yeah. It’s pretty damn good, isn't it?"

"Best thing I've eaten since the world went sideways," he said.

They ate in comfortable, easy silence, watching the gulls wheel over the sparkling red mudflats, the warm August sun soaking into their wool shirts, the rhythmic *thump-thump* of the tide against the creosote pilings filling the quiet space between them.

### VI. The Beggar Bear

It was twenty minutes after lunch when Nipn suddenly rose from his resting spot under the ticket shack.

The wolf did not growl. He did not raise his hackles. He simply stood up, stepped to the outer edge of the timber wharf, and looked down the rocky shoreline toward the mouth of the tidal creek.

Mac set down his water canteen and stood up, reaching casually for his ash bear-spear leaning against the rail.

A hundred yards down the beach, ambling out of the spruce scrub onto the open red mud, was a massive, glossy-black Fundy black bear. 

It was a full-grown male, easily four hundred and fifty pounds, with a wide, dish-faced snout, thick rounded ears, and a dense, luxuriant late-summer coat that rippled like black silk over massive shoulders. The bear was not starving or desperate; it had spent the summer gorging on wild blueberries, ant hills, and marsh grass, but its keen nose had caught the unmistakable, irresistible perfume of two hundred pounds of fresh, oily mackerel.

The bear stopped at the water's edge, fifty yards from the wharf. It sat down on its massive haunches, rested its heavy forepaws on its knees like an oversized hound, tilted its head, and sniffed the air with deep, wet snorts.

It looked directly at Mac, then at the smoke drum, then at the water.

Maya stepped up beside Mac, her hand dropping instinctively to the grip of the heavy hunting knife at her belt. "Mac... that is a very large bear."

"That's a Fundy boar," Mac said softly, his voice calm and relaxed. "He’s lived in these spruce ridges his whole life. Look at his ears—no scars, no torn cartilage. He’s fat, smart, and lazy."

"He looks like he wants lunch."

"He doesn't want trouble," Mac chuckled quietly. "He smells three hundred pounds of fish and he knows humans are easier to beg from than chasing trout in the rapids."

Mac picked up his wooden handline spool. "Watch this."

He walked to the edge of the deep water on the seaward side of the wharf, baited the hooks with leftover mackerel guts, and dropped the lead into the falling current. 

Within two minutes, the line went rigid. Mac hauled hand-over-hand, bringing up a thrashing, fourteen-inch green-striped mackerel.

He unhooked the fish, walked to the landward railing, wound up his arm, and launched the fresh, flapping mackerel through the air.

The fish flew fifty feet in a gleaming silver arc, landing with a wet *thud* on the flat red mud thirty yards from the bear.

The black bear didn't flinch. It lumbered forward on all fours, sniffed the fish once, scooped it into its jaws with a single, crunching snap of its teeth, swallowed it whole in three seconds flat, and sat right back down on its haunches.

It looked back up at Mac, licked its black chops, and gave a low, appreciative *chuff*.

"Look at that," Maya whispered, eyes wide with amazement. "You just tipped a four-hundred-pound bear."

"Netukulimk," Mac said with a dry grin, dropping his line back into the water. "You take from the river, you share with the land. Besides, a full bear is a polite bear."

For the next two hours, while Maya packed the cooled smoked fillets into the massive coolers and secured the salt firkins, Mac stood on the wharf jigging fish for the beggar on the beach. Every five minutes, another fresh mackerel sailed through the air. 

*One. Two. Five. Ten. Fifteen fish.*

The bear ate every single one with methodical, dignified gluttony. By two in the afternoon, with its belly round as an oil drum and its black fur glistening in the sun, the giant bear let out a massive, rumbling yawn that showed three inches of clean white canine teeth. 

It turned, waddled slowly up the beach with a lazy, swaying gait, pushed through the alder bushes, and vanished into the deep spruce woods.

"Well," Maya laughed, wiping down the cleaning bench. "I think you made a friend."

"I think I made an expensive habit," Mac sighed, shaking his head. "Let's get this car loaded before he brings his cousins."

### VII. The Heavy Haul Home

Loading the handcar was serious work. 

Between the two 150-quart coolers—one packed to the brim with over a hundred pounds of golden-smoked kippers, the other loaded with ninety pounds of hard-salted mackerel in brine—plus the four salt firkins and their gear, the handcar was carrying nearly **four hundred pounds of cargo**.

Mac strapped the heavy white coolers over the center axle with double manila lashings, balancing the load so the weight rested directly over the iron wheels rather than tilting the deck.

He took the white-ash pump handle from the cooler, slid it into the cast-iron rocker socket, tapped the bronze linchpin into the crank, and spread the spring cotter. He unlocked the brass padlock from the rear wheel, coiling the chain into his pack.

"Ready for the seven-mile workout?" Maya asked, stepping up to the forward handle.

"This is where we earn the fish," Mac said, taking his post. "Steady rhythm. Don't rush the strokes. Just let your weight drop through your boots."

They started out of the fenced chute, Nipn leading the way at a calm, watchful trot.

The ride back was completely different from the morning drift. The slight downgrade that had carried them effortlessly to the coast was now a steady, three-percent uphill grade through the inland spruce forest. 

The iron wheels bit into the rails with a heavy, grinding crunch: *clack-CLACK, clack-CLACK*.

Maya leaned into the work with fierce determination. Her face was flushed with exertion, a sheen of sweat catching the late-afternoon sun on her neck, but her breathing was rhythmic and deep. Whenever the grade steepened around a ridge curve, she threw her entire body into the downward stroke, her boots gripping the oak deck planks.

Mac watched her with quiet, profound admiration. There was no whining, no complaining, no asking to stop. She pulled her weight on the beam with the relentless, unyielding grit of someone who understood that survival wasn't a spectator sport.

By five in the afternoon, the stone lighthouse tower rose above the hemlock canopy ahead, its weathered sandstone shining like red gold in the low rays of the setting sun.

Mac engaged the foot brake, bringing the loaded handcar to a smooth stop at the camp siding.

Maya rested her forehead against the cool ash handle, panting softly, her arms trembling from four miles of continuous pumping. 

"We... made it," she gasped, looking up with a proud, exhausted grin.

"We made it," Mac said softly, stepping around the cooler and handing her a canteen of cold spring water. "You did incredible, Maya."

She took a long, deep drink, wiped her chin, and looked at the mountain of fish on the deck. "Two hundred pounds of protein. We’re going to eat like kings this winter."

"Yes, we are," Mac said.

### VIII. The Yard Guest

They spent the next hour carrying the gear up the timber steps to the homestead compound:
* The smoked fillets were hung in the dry, screened rafters of the stone root cellar, where the cool, constant forty-degree subterranean air and dry cedar shavings would keep them perfect until spring.
* The salted mackerel in the firkins were stored along the cellar’s stone wall under tight-fitting oak lids weighted down with Fundy cobblestones.

As the sun dipped behind the western ridge, painting the sky in long streaks of violet and burnt orange, Mac walked out to the garden palisade to secure the trunnion gate for the night.

He took two steps through the inner archway and stopped dead in his tracks.

Sitting right in the middle of the Three Sisters garden path—tucked comfortably between the sweet-corn mounds and the high red-spruce palisade—was a massive, glossy black shape.

It was the bear.

The four-hundred-and-fifty-pound boar was sitting on its rear end, leaning back against a stout spruce post, lazily scratching the back of its thick neck against the rough bark. It had not touched a single ear of corn, nor had it trampled the squash vines; it was just sitting there, enjoying the evening breeze, chewing contentedly on a mouthful of wild clover.

When it saw Mac and Maya walk through the gate, the bear turned its broad, furry head, blinked its small brown eyes, and gave the exact same low, friendly *chuff* it had made on the beach.

Maya froze, her hand flying to her mouth in pure, stunned disbelief. 

"Mac..." she whispered, her eyes wide as saucers. "Is that... is that the *same bear*?"

Mac stood there for three long seconds, looking from the giant bear to the untouched corn stalks, then down at Nipn, who had walked up to the fence line, sniffed the bear's foot from ten feet away, and casually lay down on the porch planks with his chin on his paws as if an enormous four-hundred-pound bear in the backyard was completely standard operating procedure.

Mac threw his head back and let out a loud, booming laugh that echoed all the way down the tidal creek.

"Well, I'll be damned," Mac chuckled, wiping tears of amusement from the corners of his eyes. "Look at it, Maya. Followed the tracks seven miles uphill because it thinks this is an all-inclusive fish resort."

Maya tilted her head, inspecting the bear's round, furry silhouette. "So... is it a he or a she?"

"Maya, I am not bending down with a lantern to inspect a four-hundred-pound wild bear's undercarriage," Mac said deadpan. "I respect personal boundaries. If the bear wants us to know its business, it can leave a note on the porch. Long as it eats fish and stays out of the squash, its name is Bear."

Maya burst into bright, bubbling laughter, leaning against his shoulder. "Fair enough."

### IX. The Mud on the Paws & The Shoreline Guard (Days 59–60)

The bear was entirely wild, but it wasn't stupid. In a province filled with rotting, shuffling corpses and scarce food, it had discovered a quiet, peaceful paradise where nobody shot at it, the smells were sweet, and fresh mackerel fell out of the sky. It quickly figured out the rules of the camp: don't step on the corn mounds, don't mess with the wolf, and behave.

Better yet, the bear found its own job.

The western edge of the homestead along the tidal mudflats had always been Mac’s defensive weak spot—the steep silt banks were impossible to fence against the sixteen-foot tides. But the bear decided the sunny gravel ledge above the fish weirs was the finest afternoon napping spot in Siknikt.

On Day 59, three waterlogged, mud-caked walkers dragged themselves out of the low-tide channel, staggering up the bank toward the smell of the fish-cleaning bench.

The bear was sleeping in the warm sun. It opened one brown eye, irritated by the wet, gurgling noise. 

Without even standing up to its full height, the bear rose onto its front paws, swung a massive, dinner-plate-sized foreleg, and swatted the lead walker across the neck. The wet, rotten corpse was launched ten feet sideways into the chocolate mud with a sickening *CRACK* of shattered cervical vertebrae. The bear swatted the second one down with a single, contemptuous backhand that caved in its ribcage, and stepped on the third.

It let out a huff of mild disgust, wiped a smear of black silt off its claw against a driftwood log, and lay right back down to sleep.

Mac, watching from the top of the flume with his bear-spear in hand, just whistled softly. He walked down the steps, carried a fat four-pound striped bass from the retention pen, and tossed it right between the bear's front paws.

"Good shift," Mac told it. "Take five."

The bear crunched the bass down in two bites, licking its lips with profound satisfaction.

But the red Fundy mud was treacherous on bear paws. By the evening of Day 60, after hours of patrolling the tidal mudbanks, the heavy, iron-rich clay had baked into hard, painful balls between the bear's footpads and thick black claws. The bear was sitting by the porch bench, whimpering softly and chewing awkwardly at its front paw, unable to dislodge the dried mud cakes.

Maya watched it from the cabin door for five minutes.

"Grab me a wooden bucket of warm water," she told Mac, tying her hair back. "And that soft bristle horse-brush from the tool chest."

"Maya..." Mac said cautiously. "That is still four hundred pounds of apex predator."

"I’ve debrided gangrenous diabetic foot ulcers on combative lumberjacks, Simon," Maya said calmly, picking up a clean linen cloth and a small bowl of melted tallow mixed with spruce-pitch salve. "This bear just has sore feet."

She walked out onto the grass, moving with slow, quiet confidence. She sat down on a low cedar stool three feet from the giant animal, placing the steaming bucket between them.

"Hey, big guy," Maya said in that soft, soothing, unshakable nurse voice she used in triage. "Let’s take a look at those feet."

The bear stopped chewing its paw. It looked at her, sniffing the warm, sweet-scented steam rising from the chamomile and warm water. 

Slow as molasses, the giant beast extended its right front leg and laid its massive, heavy, mud-caked paw directly across Maya’s knees.

Maya didn't flinch. She dipped the soft brush into the warm water, working gently into the sensitive crevices between its black leather pads. With steady, patient strokes, she softened the hardened clay, washing the grit away into the bucket. She picked out a sharp piece of jagged shale lodged near the center claw, cleaned the raw spot with a dab of spruce salve, and dried the pads with a soft cloth.

The bear closed its eyes, letting out a long, deep, rumbling groan of sheer, unadulterated bliss that vibrated the cedar planks under their feet.

When she finished both front paws, the bear gently rested its broad, furry snout right against Maya’s denim-clad knee, giving her hand a slow, wet, sandpaper lick.

Maya smiled, scratching the thick fur right behind its rounded ears. "You’re a good boy. Or girl. Whoever you are."

From that morning on, the dynamic of the homestead shifted into something equal parts absurd and miraculous.

Whenever Maya walked out into the yard to do her morning rounds, the bear rose from its clover bed, shook out its heavy black coat, and ambled five paces behind her like an overgrown, four-hundred-and-fifty-pound puppy.

If Maya stopped by the Three Sisters mounds to pick runner beans, the bear sat down on its haunches, tilted its head, and watched her work with deep, sleepy brown eyes. When she carried a wooden basket of dried laundry up from the creek line, the giant beast lumbered beside her at a polite, respectful distance, occasionally leaning in to give her sleeve a soft, warm sniff.

Nipn and the bear worked out an effortless, wordless division of labor: the wolf ranged high along the northern ridges and scouted the inland railway tracks, while the bear acted as Maya’s personal, heavy-artillery escort inside the perimeter palisade.

Mac leaned against the porch railing with his mug of Labrador tea, watching Maya walk across the grass with a four-hundred-pound wild apex predator trotting dutifully at her heels like a prize golden retriever.

He took a slow sip, shaking his head with a quiet, dry grin.

"You know, Maya," Mac called out, "most nurses just get a thank-you card or a tin of shortbread from their patients. You got a four-hundred-pound shadow."

Maya looked back over her shoulder at the bear, who immediately sat down and gave a soft, happy *huff*, then looked up at Mac with a radiant, teasing smile.

"That's because I have excellent bedside manner, Simon," she said, tossing a handful of wild clover between the bear's paws. "Take notes. You might learn something."

Mac laughed, the sound warm and easy in the morning air, and stepped down off the porch to join them in the garden.
