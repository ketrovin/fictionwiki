# Chapter 7: The Wolves of the Rail and the Red Trestle

---

### Part I: The Stubborn Leg & The Secret Departure

The desire to pull one’s own weight is a stubborn, burning fire that no amount of comfortable blankets or warm fish broth can easily quench.

By the ninth morning since her rescue from the culvert, Maya was done being a patient.

She woke before dawn in the quiet sleeping alcove of the hemlock cabin. Outside, the world was wrapped in a thick, cool blanket of Fundy river fog—a silent, gray pea-souper that rolled off the salt mud, blotting out the high black spruces and muffling every sound until the entire valley felt like a private room carved from white cotton.

In the warmth of the alcove, Scar was still fast asleep.

The red squirrel was wedged into his established headquarters: curled into a soft, fiery russet crescent between the generous curves of her breasts, his hindquarters anchored firmly into the fleece lining of her underwire brassiere, his black nose buried beneath his bushy tail. His breath came in microscopic, rhythmic puffs against her sternum, warm and steady as a purring ember.

Maya looked down at the little lump in her unbuttoned green flannel shirt.

"Wake up, tenant," she whispered, keeping her voice lower than the sigh of the wind in the eaves. "The landlord has business up the track."

Scar didn't move. He merely let out a faint, squeaky sigh, wiggled his toes deeper into the soft cotton lining, and pressed his head closer to her warm skin.

Maya smiled, but her jaw was set with quiet, unyielding determination.

She swung her legs over the edge of the split-spruce bed frame.

For nine days, her left fibula had been wrapped in clean birchbark laths, padded with sterile sphagnum moss, and soaked in Mac’s concentrated decoctions of comfrey root, wild willow bark, and cold river clay. The savage, purple swelling that had made the joint look like a bruised cantaloupe had receded completely. The skin was pale, clean, and cool to the touch. When she rotated the foot, the deep, sickening grinding of loose bone fragments was gone—replaced by the stiff, tight, knitting resistance of fresh osteoid tissue.

Mac had told her she needed another week of horizontal rest.

Mac was a Kinap who could carry a four-hundred-pound timber on his shoulder without breathing hard, but Mac didn't understand the bitter, humiliating poison of feeling like dead weight. Maya had survived four years of collapse in Truro and two years of warlords in Sussex by being faster, smarter, and more useful than everyone around her. Living on another person's charity—even the generous, sacred hospitality of the *Netukulimk* hearth—gnawed at her pride like rust on a plowshare.

Beside the bed stood her new walking tool: not the bulky Y-crutches she had used for the past week, but a sleek, thirty-eight-inch walking staff Mac had hand-carved from a straight sapling of white ash.

The staff was an engineer's dream: peeled of its outer bark, shaved down to an elegant, octagonal shaft that tapered from one-and-a-half inches at the grip to three-quarters of an inch at the base, shod with a turned-brass ferrule salvaged from an old nautical boathook, and capped with an ergonomic oval palm-knob carved from dense rock maple.

Maya reached out, gripping the smooth ash handle firmly in her right hand.

She stood.

She planted her sound right boot on the rag rug, took a slow, deep breath through her nose, and gingerly brought her left foot down onto the hemlock floorboards.

She shifted twenty pounds of her body weight onto the healing ankle.

A sharp, stiff ache flared along the outer edge of her lateral malleolus, radiating up her calf muscle—not the white-hot, bone-snapping agony of the break, but the tight, complaining protest of shortened tendons and dormant ligaments.

She leaned her upper torso onto the ash staff.

The wood flexed half an inch beneath her palm, absorbing the load with that clean, resilient springiness inherent to white ash.

She took a step. Then another.

*Tap-step. Tap-step.*

She wasn't running, and she certainly wasn't marching, but she was upright, mobile, and walking under her own power with a single hand-held cane.

"Not dead yet," she murmured to the dark room.

Between her breasts, Scar felt the vertical motion, poked his whiskered snout up through her collar, blinked twice in the gloom, and let out a tiny, sharp *chip* of protest.

*The horizontal platform has been abolished,* Scar complained, his telepathic voice gravelly with sleep. *The duplex is vibrating. Where are the cracked seeds?*

"Quiet, fuzzball," Maya thought back, reaching for her heavy canvas work vest hanging from the wall peg. "Mac is sleeping by the front door. If we wake him, he'll carry me back to bed like a sack of oats and lecture me on orthopedic biomechanics until noon."

She pulled the fleece-lined vest over her shoulders, zipping it halfway up from the waist, leaving the top wide open so Scar had plenty of breathing room and an unobstructed observation platform over her collar. She strapped her small canvas shoulder haversack across her chest, slipping a small forged pry-bar, a brass caliper, and an empty two-quart tin can into the pouch.

Her objective was clear, practical, and entirely necessary.

Yesterday, while helping Mac align the draisine speeder on the siding, she had noticed that the heavy iron wheel journals were bone-dry. The railway car could cruise at twenty-five miles an hour with its new 3:1 overdrive transmission, but running chilled-iron bearings on steel axles without heavy extreme-pressure grease would gall the bronze sleeves within ten miles. Mac had a tub of rendered mutton tallow, but tallow broke down under high rotational friction and washed away in the rain.

Maya knew where real grease was.

One mile north of the homestead promontory, where the abandoned CN spur crossed the Stony Creek drainage, sat the old **Hillsborough Siding Depot**—a concrete loading dock, an abandoned maintenance shed, and two rusted, forty-foot steel boxcars that had been sidelined and forgotten when the rail line went dead.

Maya had spotted the siding from the rail speeder when Mac had hauled her in on Day Three: the maintenance shed still had its corrugated steel roof intact, and the yellow stenciled letters on the padlocked freight door read: *C.N. MAINTENANCE OF WAY — LUBRICANTS & TRACK HARDWARE.*

In that shed were drums of heavy lithium-moly chassis grease, spare roller chains, and hardened steel tap-and-die sets.

If she could hike the mile of flat, level railway ballast with her ash staff, pry open the service door, fill her two-quart tin with heavy grease, and return before Mac woke up to stoke the morning forge, she wouldn't just be an invalid eating his corn cakes.

She would be a partner who had secured the mechanical lifeline for their voyage to Fort Folly.

Maya slipped out the side window of the sleeping alcove, using her ash staff to ease herself down onto the soft moss of the garden terrace without making a sound.

The morning air was cold, damp, and tasted intensely of salt and wet spruce needles.

Nipn was nowhere in sight—likely running the outer perimeter along the mud bluffs, checking the low-tide weir for stray flounder.

Maya turned north, planting her sound boot and her ash cane into the crushed clam-shells of the path, slipping through the gap in the living willow fence, and climbing the gentle gravel embankment up to the main railway bed.

The twin ribbons of ninety-pound continuously welded steel rails cut through the gray river fog like two straight, dark iron roads disappearing into the northern woods.

Maya adjusted her grip on the ash staff, settled her shoulders, and began to walk.

*Tap-step. Tap-step.*

Between her breasts, Scar curled his hindquarters securely into the bridge of her bra, rested his cream-colored front paws against her collarbone, and raised his black nose into the wind.

His long black whiskers twitched with sudden, keen alertness.

*The air smells of cold iron,* the squirrel transmitted, his beady eyes peering into the mist. *And wet leaves. No owls yet. But the duplex should not wander too far from the stone tower. The woods are full of large things with many teeth.*

"I have a cold steel pry-bar and a thirty-eight-inch hickory-hard ash stick, Scar," Maya whispered, her breath puffing white in the fog. "Anything that comes near us is getting a broken kneecap."

The squirrel gave a doubtful flick of his ear, ducked down half an inch into the warm cleavage hollow, and kept his eyes glued to the dark spruces lining the tracks.

### Part II: The Shadow on the Ballast

The hike along the abandoned railway line was eerily silent.

The dense river fog clung to the ground like wet wool, limiting visibility to thirty yards. On either side of the raised ballast bed, thick stands of black spruce, tamarack, and balsam fir crowded the right-of-way, their drooping branches dripping with heavy condensation that fell into the ferns with a steady *drip-plip-drop*.

Maya maintained a steady, measured pace.

*Tap-step. Tap-step.*

Her healing fibula throbbed with a dull, rhythmic pulse, but it held firm. Her body was young, strong, and fueled by nine days of rich Fundy fish protein, wild greens, and pure spring water. With every hundred yards she covered, the stiffness in her calf muscles loosened, her stride lengthened, and her confidence grew.

"See?" she murmured to Scar, who was peeking over her collar like a ship's lookout. "One mile is nothing. We'll be at the depot in ten minutes."

At the three-quarter-mile mark, the track curved gently to the northwest, following the rim of the deep sandstone gorge carved by Stony Creek.

Through the thinning mist, the concrete loading ramp of the Hillsborough Siding Depot materialized out of the hemlocks:

A low, weed-choked platform of cracked reinforced concrete, bordered by an overgrown spur siding that peeled off the main line. Resting on the rusty spur rails sat an old yellow CN caboose with shattered windows, and behind it, a heavy, dark-red forty-foot steel boxcar, its sliding side door rusted open about three feet.

Beside the boxcar stood the maintenance shack: a sturdy building of corrugated iron sheets bolted to a heavy cedar timber frame, its heavy padlock hanging loose from a rusted hasp.

Maya’s eyes lit up with triumph.

"Jackpot," she breathed.

She stepped off the main line, her boots crunching softly on the wet crushed-granite ballast, making her way toward the open maintenance shed.

She reached the corrugated door, pulled out her forged pry-bar, and levered the rusted steel panel open.

The interior was dry, shadowed, and smelled wonderfully of machine oil, asphalt, and stale pine dust.

Maya set her ash staff against the doorframe, pulled the two-quart tin from her haversack, and stepped inside.

The salvage was even better than she had dared to hope:

Along the back timber wall sat three sealed five-gallon metal pails with green-and-white lettering: *IMPERIAL OIL CHASSIS GREASE — EP-2 HIGH LOAD.* Beside them on a heavy timber shelf lay two wooden crates containing unopened industrial roller chains, three sets of steel bearing scrapers, and a wooden box containing a complete high-carbon steel tap-and-die set from 1/4-inch to 1-inch National Fine thread.

"Yes!" Maya whispered, her heart soaring with professional joy. "Real EP-2 moly grease! Mac won't believe this!"

She used her pry-bar to pop the metal ring off the nearest five-gallon pail. Inside lay forty pounds of thick, dark-gray, lithium-complex grease—smooth, buttery, completely unoxidized, and smelling of pure industrial petroleum perfection.

She took a flat wooden shingle from the floor and began packing the two-quart tin with heavy grease.

Inside her flannel shirt, Scar was not looking at the grease.

The squirrel’s head had snapped ninety degrees to the north. His ears were flattened tight against his skull, his long whiskers vibrating so violently that Maya felt the trembling through her breastbone.

A sharp, electric jolt of pure panic shot across the mental link between them:

*THE BEASTS OF COLD IRON!* Scar screamed in her mind. *THE GROWLING SMOKE! THEY ARE COMING DOWN THE TRACK! RUN! RUN! RUN!*

Maya froze, the grease-covered wooden shingle suspended in her hand.

"Scar, what is it?" she whispered, her blood running suddenly cold.

Through the silence of the foggy woods, a sound reached her ears—a sound that made every muscle in her body turn to ice:

*BRRRRRRRRRR-BAP-BAP-BAP!*

*WHRRRRRRR-WHRRRRRRRR!*

The harsh, rasping, unmistakable whine of small-displacement two-stroke motorcycle engines.

Not one. Three of them.

They were coming from the north along the railway right-of-way, their knobby dirt tires churning through the wet ballast, their unbaffled exhausts echoing off the sandstone gorge like angry hornets.

Maya’s breath locked in her throat.

*Vance’s scouts.*

The Clacks signal from Auntie Elizabeth flashed through her mind like a red-hot branding iron: *Three days ago raider patrol from Salisbury spotted on Route 106... probing old culverts with motorbikes... they seek the woman who fled.*

They hadn't turned back. They had found the abandoned railway corridor and followed the tracks south!

Maya dropped the wooden shingle, grabbed the sealed grease tin, shoved it into her haversack, and snatched her white ash staff from the doorframe.

She slipped out of the maintenance shed, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird, scanning the fog-shrouded tracks.

The mist was lifting fast under the morning sun.

Fifty yards up the main line, three dark silhouettes tore through the thinning fog:

Three rugged, stripped-down enduro dirt bikes—a green Kawasaki, a red Honda, and a battered black Yamaha—their plastic fairings caked in dried road mud, their headlights fitted with rusted wire cages.

Astride the bikes were three men.

They were dressed in road scavengers' armor: greasy leather motorcycle jackets reinforced with riveted tire treads, heavy steel-toed combat boots, and dirty canvas work pants. Across their backs were slung hunting rifles and rusted iron crowbars. The lead rider—a tall, skeletal man with a greasy rat-tail braid and a jagged scar across his lip—wore a full-face motorcycle helmet with the plastic visor torn off, revealing yellowed teeth bared in a feral grin.

The lead rider spotted the fresh boot prints in the wet ballast.

Then, his eyes lifted and locked squarely onto Maya.

He slammed his boot onto the rear brake.

*SKRRRRRRRRRRRRRRR!*

The rear knobby tire locked up, slewing sideways across the crushed granite in a spray of gray stones.

He kicked the bike into neutral, blipped the throttle with a loud, tearing *BRAAAP-BAP-BAP*, and pointed a gloved finger at her.

"WELL, LOOK AT WHAT JUST CAME OUT OF THE BUSHES!" the lead rider screamed over the engine noise, his voice raw with sadistic triumph. "BOYS! SHUT 'EM DOWN! IT’S VANCE’S LITTLE GREASE-MONKEY!"

The other two bikes slid to a halt, boxing in the spur track, cutting off her retreat back to the main line.

Maya stood on the concrete loading dock, her knuckles turning white around her white ash staff, her jaw setting like Albert County granite.

She was trapped.

### Part III: The Cornered Prey

Maya didn't run.

To run on an ankle that was only nine days out of a fracture, across sixty yards of loose, ankle-twisting railway ballast against three motorized dirt bikes, was an arithmetic calculation with a certainty of failure: she would make thirty yards, her foot would roll on a granite cobble, and she would go down in the dirt with zero leverage and zero balance.

Instead, she backed up three measured steps until her shoulder blades hit the solid, heavy steel plate of the rusted red boxcar.

She had the high ground of the concrete platform. She had iron at her back. And she had thirty-eight inches of straight-grained, seasoned white ash in her hands.

Inside her flannel shirt, Scar was in a state of absolute, apocalyptic terror.

The squirrel was shivering so violently that Maya felt his tiny ribs vibrating against her own chest. He had burrowed down as deep as he could go between her calcium cannons, his head wedged behind the left cup of her bra, but his black, beady eyes were peeking out through the open fourth button, dilated with wild, feral fury.

*THE RUST-VERMIN!* Scar chattered in her mind, his mental voice a frantic shriek. *THEY SMELL OF BURNT BLACK WATER AND DEAD WEASELS! HIT THEM WITH THE HEAVY ASH! CRACK THEIR CRANIUMS!*

"I hear you, Scar," Maya thought back, her internal voice cold, hard, and steady as ice. "Stay inside. Don't move unless I tell you."

The three men kicked their kickstands down, letting their engines idle with a rhythmic, stinking *chug-chug-chug* that filled the clearing with blue two-stroke smoke.

They dismounted.

The lead rider—the rat-tailed man with the scarred lip—stepped forward onto the concrete dock. In his right hand, he carried a three-foot length of three-quarter-inch galvanized pipe, tapping it rhythmically against his thigh: *thwack... thwack... thwack.*

The second man was short, stocky, and built like a fire hydrant, carrying a rusty four-foot tire iron and a bundle of heavy nylon zip-ties tucked into his belt. The third, younger and wiry, unslung a battered single-barrel twelve-gauge shotgun from his shoulder, cradling it in the crook of his arm with a greasy, leering smirk.

"Vance is real pissed off at you, Maya," the rat-tailed leader sneered, stepping closer, his bloodshot eyes raking over her form. "You cost him sixteen gallons of fuel and two weeks of machine-shop downtime when you broke through the wire in Sussex. He promised fifty gallons of high-test gasoline to whoever brings you back."

He stopped five paces away, his gaze dropping from her face to her unbuttoned green flannel shirt.

His eyes widened slightly as he took in the full, womanly curves of her figure beneath the fleece-lined vest—and the unmistakable, twitching black nose of a red squirrel peeking out from the deep hollow of her cleavage.

A filthy, mocking laugh tore out of his scarred mouth.

"Well, well, well," he chuckled, looking back at his two partners. "Look at the mechanic. Living out in the trees like a backwoods queen. What’ve you got tucked in between those sweet tits of yours, girl? A pet rat?"

"It's an Acadian red squirrel, you illiterate ape," Maya said, her voice dropping into a flat, razor-sharp register that held zero trace of fear. "And he has considerably better hygiene and higher intelligence than all three of you combined."

The stocky man with the tire iron let out a guttural snort, stepping up beside the leader.

"Got a sharp tongue on her, Cody," the stocky man grunted. "Vance said we had to bring her back alive. He didn't say she had to be in one piece. And he sure as hell didn't say we couldn't break her in before we load her on the rack."

Cody, the leader, grinned—a slow, predatory, sickening smile that revealed rotten, brown-stained incisors.

"Vance won't know the difference," Cody murmured, shifting the pipe to his left hand, stepping two paces closer, his right hand reaching out toward her collar. "Come here, girl. Drop the stick and play nice, and maybe we won't drag you through the briars behind the bikes."

Maya didn't drop the stick.

She waited.

She waited until Cody's dirty, calloused fingers were six inches from her jacket lapel, his weight committed forward onto his front foot.

Then, she struck.

Maya didn't swing the staff like a baseball bat; she drove it like a Roman spear.

She stepped forward on her sound right boot, using her hips and core to drive the brass-shod tip of the white ash staff straight forward in a blinding, two-handed piston strike.

*CRACK!*

The brass ferrule struck Cody squarely in the center of his chest, right over his sternum.

The impact was brutal.

The breath exploded out of Cody's lungs in a violent, wheezing gasp: *"OOF!"*

The force of the strike lifted the two-hundred-pound man off his heels, throwing him backward three yards into the gravel ballast, where he landed hard on his tailbone, clutching his chest, gagging for air.

"STAY BACK, YOU VERMIN!" Maya shouted, her dark eyes blazing with incandescent fury as she brought the ash staff back to a defensive high-guard.

For a split second, the other two raiders froze in shock.

Then, the young one with the shotgun cursed, dropping his shoulder to swing the heavy twelve-gauge barrel level with her chest.

"YOU BITCH!"

Before he could pull the hammer back, an explosion of red fur erupted from Maya’s shirt.

Scar didn't run away.

The tiny, eight-ounce squirrel launched himself straight out of Maya's cleavage in a breathtaking, four-foot horizontal leap, flying through the air like a guided russet missile.

*FWUMP.*

Scar landed squarely on the shotgunner’s face.

His needle-sharp claws dug into the man's nose and eyelids; his teeth sank with psychotic, single-minded vengeance into the soft flesh of the raider’s left earlobe.

"GAHHHHHH! GET IT OFF ME! GET IT OFF!" the shotgunner shrieked, dropping the weapon into the weeds, flailing his arms frantically as the squirrel tore at his hair and screeched ear-piercing boreal curses into his skull:

*EVICTION!* Scar screamed telepathically, practically vibrating with berserker bloodlust. *DIE, COLD-SMOKE APE! DIE!*

The distraction bought Maya two precious seconds.

She pivoted to face the stocky man with the tire iron, sweeping the heavy ash staff in a low, vicious arc aimed at his knees.

*THWACK.*

The ash struck his right shin, eliciting a loud yelp of pain, but the stocky man was heavy, armored in thick leather, and braced for the blow. He didn't fall.

He lunged forward with an animalistic roar, throwing his entire two-hundred-and-twenty-pound bulk straight at her.

His heavy shoulder struck Maya in the chest.

The impact slammed her violently against the rusted steel door of the boxcar.

*KABANG.*

The breath was knocked from her lungs in a sharp, painful gasp. Her white ash staff was torn from her grip, clattering down onto the concrete platform out of reach.

Her splinted left ankle buckled beneath the sudden lateral load, a sharp, sickening spike of pain shooting up her shin as she slid down against the iron door, her back pinned against the rusted steel.

The stocky man clamped two beefy, dirt-encrusted hands onto her shoulders, pinning her flat against the boxcar wall.

His face was inches from hers, reeking of stale tobacco, foul breath, and cheap corn moonshine.

"Got you, you little wildcat," he panted, his heavy knee pressing between her thighs, pinning her sound leg against the iron. "Now let's see how loud you scream when I—"

He reached down with his right hand, grabbing the front of her canvas vest, tearing the zipper apart with a loud, violent *SCREEECH* of teeth.

Maya didn't scream.

She bared her teeth, raised both her hands, and drove her thumbs toward his eyes with everything she had left in her body.

"GET YOUR FILTHY HANDS OFF ME!" she snarled.

The man jerked his head back, dodging her thumbs, his left hand coming up to wrap around her throat, cutting off her air, slamming the back of her head against the steel plate.

Black spots danced before Maya’s vision. Her lungs burned. Her fingers clawed desperately at his iron grip.

*So this is it,* the cold, bitter thought drifted through her darkening consciousness. *After all the miles... after the bogs... after finding the hearth...*

And then, from thirty yards away up the railway cutting, the world ended.

### Part IV: The Arrival of the Kinap

The sound that heralded the arrival of the master of the red river was not a human battle cry.

It was not a shout of warning, nor was it the sharp crack of a rifle shot.

It was a deep, terrifying, atmospheric vibration—a low, subterranean rumble that began deep in the bedrock of the sandstone gorge and traveled upward through the railway ballast, vibrating through the rusted steel plates of the boxcar with the acoustic weight of an oncoming earthquake.

The air in the clearing seemed to drop five degrees in a single heartbeat.

The raider pinning Maya against the iron door froze, his dirty fingers still clamped around her throat, his bloodshot eyes widening in sudden, instinctual alarm.

"What the hell is—"

Above them, thirty feet up the sheer vertical sandstone face of the railway cutting, the spruce trees tore apart.

A human body did not climb down the cliff.

It dropped.

Mac descended from the high spruce ridge like a falling granite boulder, clearing twenty vertical feet of empty air in a single, terrifying leap that blotted out the morning sun.

*KABOOOOM!*

He struck the crushed-granite railway ballast with both heavy leather boots.

The sheer, staggering kinetic energy of his landing cratered the gray stones, sending a spray of sharp shale flying twenty yards in every direction. The ground shuddered beneath the impact, rattling the heavy iron wheels of the forty-foot boxcar.

Mac didn't roll. He didn't stumble. He didn't drop to a knee to absorb the shock.

He absorbed forty thousand foot-pounds of falling momentum directly into his thighs and hips with that unyielding, terrifying Kinap density, rising to his full six-foot-five height in a single, fluid, terrifying surge of coiled violence.

His face was completely devoid of human expression.

There was no rage in his eyes. There was no hot, frantic fury.

There was only the cold, ancient, absolute void of a rogue ocean wave breaking over a sinking schooner. His pale blue eyes were pale as Arctic glacier ice, his bearded jaw locked like pig-iron, his massive, scarred hands hanging loose and open at his sides.

Beside him, bursting from the alders like a silver ghost, was Nipn.

The great wolf didn't bark. He bounded across the ballast with ears pinned flat, his lips curled back to expose three inches of ivory fangs dripping with white foam, his low, vibrating growl vibrating in the raiders' teeth.

The young raider who had been wrestling with Scar on the ground scrambled backward in terror, clawing frantically through the wet weeds for his dropped twelve-gauge shotgun.

"C-CODY! LOOK OUT! THERE'S A GIANT IN THE BUSHES!"

Mac didn't look at the kid. He didn't look at the leader.

His ice-blue eyes locked squarely onto the stocky man whose dirty hands were pinned around Maya's throat.

Mac moved.

He did not run. He closed the twenty paces between the railway track and the concrete loading dock in two blinding, ground-eating strides that blurred the morning air.

The stocky man barely had time to turn his head.

Before he could release Maya's throat, before he could raise his iron pipe, Mac's left hand shot out like an iron piston.

Mac’s massive, calloused fingers closed around the raider's right wrist.

*CRUNCH.*

The sound was sickeningly loud in the quiet morning air: the crisp, wet, explosive snap of eight carpal bones and two forearm tendons being pulverized under eighteen hundred pounds of hydraulic Kinap grip pressure.

The stocky man’s eyes bulged until the whites were completely bloodshot.

A high-pitched, wet shriek of agonizing, incomprehensible pain tore out of his mouth: *"AIIIIIEEEEEE-GAAAAAAH!"*

Mac didn't pause.

Without breaking his forward momentum, his right hand reached down, gripped the thick leather of the man's work belt, and hoisted the two-hundred-and-twenty-pound raider completely off his feet with a single arm.

Mac spun on his heel in a half-circle, using the man’s own mass as a centrifugal pendulum, and launched him through the air.

*CRASH!*

The stocky man flew fifteen feet across the loading dock, slamming broadside into the corrugated iron wall of the maintenance shed.

The galvanized steel sheets buckled inward three inches beneath the impact. The heavy timber stud behind the wall snapped with a loud *KRAK*, and the man slid down into the nettles like a sack of wet sand, his pulverized wrist dangling limp, his breath gone, his eyes rolled back into his skull.

Maya dropped to her knees against the boxcar wall, gasping for air, her hand clutching her bruised throat, coughing violently as the cool mountain air rushed back into her burning lungs.

"Maya," Mac’s voice dropped into a low, rumbling hum that cut through the ringing in her ears. "Stay down."

Behind Mac, the rat-tailed leader, Cody, had scrambled up from the ballast where Maya's staff had thrown him.

Seeing his partner broken in half in two seconds, Cody's mind snapped into cornered, feral desperation. He realized there was no running away on foot from a man who dropped thirty feet out of a tree and threw two-hundred-pound men through iron walls.

With a hoarse, ragged roar of terrified panic, Cody reached into his boot, drew a nine-inch, heavy-spine carbon steel pig-sticker bowie knife, and charged Mac’s exposed back.

"DIE, YOU BASTARD!"

Cody drove the knife forward with all his running weight, aiming the point straight between Mac’s shoulder blades.

Mac didn't even turn his head.

His Kinap senses—attuned to every whisper of displaced air, every scrape of steel against leather, every shift of weight on the gravel—tracked the strike with absolute, effortless clarity.

At the very last microsecond, as the steel blade was three inches from his homespun wool coat, Mac took a half-step sideways.

The knife hissed harmlessly through empty air.

Mac’s right hand whipped out, his thick thumb and forefinger locking around Cody's wrist like a forged tool-steel vise.

Cody’s forward momentum stopped dead in mid-stride, his feet kicking out from under him as if he had run into a steel cable stretched across the track.

Mac tilted his head, looking down at the struggling, rat-tailed man with cold, sovereign detachment.

"You brought a nine-inch blade into my valley," Mac said softly, his deep baritone carrying the unhurried weight of an ancient oak tree. "To steal a woman under the protection of my hearth."

"LET GO OF ME!" Cody screamed, thrashing his legs, spitting blood from his split lip, trying with his left hand to claw at Mac’s eyes. "VANCE WILL FLAY YOU ALIVE! VANCE HAS FORTY RIFLES IN SUSSEX!"

"Vance is thirty miles away," Mac replied, his voice terrifyingly calm. "And you are standing in my woods."

Mac applied two degrees of rotational torque to Cody's wrist.

*CRACK-SNAP.*

The distal radius and ulna of Cody's forearm shattered like dry hemlock kindling.

The bowie knife slipped from his paralyzed fingers, clattering harmlessly onto the granite ballast.

Cody let out an ear-piercing howl of pure agony, his knees buckling beneath him, dropping to the dirt like an empty coat, clutching his broken, deformed arm against his stomach, weeping in the gravel.

Over by the ditch, the third raider—the wiry teenager who had finally managed to kick Scar off his bloody ears—had scooped his single-barrel twelve-gauge shotgun out of the weeds.

His hands were shaking so violently that the muzzle danced in a frantic, six-inch circle. His face was covered in bloody gouges from the squirrel’s claws, his eyes wild with incomprehensible terror.

"B-BACK OFF!" the kid shrieked, cocking the heavy external hammer of the shotgun with a loud *CLIC-CLAC*. "I'LL BLOW YOUR HEAD OFF, OLD MAN! I SWEAR BY THE FIRES I'LL PUT A HOLE IN YOU!"

Mac slowly turned to face him.

He didn't duck. He didn't dive for cover behind the concrete dock.

He stood fully upright, broad chest square to the twelve-gauge bore, his pale blue eyes staring down the length of the rusted steel barrel into the trembling teenager's soul.

Beside the boy, Nipn stepped out of the shadow of the caboose.

The wolf didn't charge. He merely closed the distance to four paces, his amber eyes glowing with ancient, predatory intelligence, his belly rumbling with a low, bone-chilling hum that promised immediate, throat-tearing death if the boy's finger twitched.

"Put the gun down, son," Mac said, his voice flat, low, and quiet.

"I-I'LL SHOOT!" the boy sobbed, tears cutting clean tracks through the grease on his cheeks. "I'LL PULL THE TRIGGER!"

"If you pull that trigger," Mac rumbled, taking a slow, heavy step forward onto the ballast, "the lead shot will pass through my shoulder. It will hurt. It will make me bleed. And in the three seconds it takes you to reload that single-shot pipe, I will take that gun from your hands, wrap the barrel around your neck, and feed the rest of you to the river. Lower the iron."

The boy looked at Mac's mountain of a chest.

He looked at the sixty-pound gray wolf four feet to his left, whose fangs were gleaming white in the morning light.

He looked at his leader, Cody, curled in a fetal ball in the weeds, weeping over a shattered forearm.

The boy's knees gave way completely.

The twelve-gauge shotgun slipped from his trembling hands, falling into the wet grass with a soft *thud*.

The boy collapsed onto his knees in the dirt, burying his face in his bloody hands, sobbing uncontrollably:

"Don't kill me... please... don't let the wolf eat me... I was just following Cody... Vance said he'd shoot my brother if I didn't ride..."

The clearing went dead silent, broken only by the boy's ragged sobs, the groaning of the man pinned against the maintenance shed, and the distant, eternal roar of Stony Creek rushing through the gorge below.

Total duration of the confrontation: twenty-two seconds.

### Part V: The Demolition of the Machines

Mac didn't spare another glance at the weeping teenager.

He turned on his heel, stepped up onto the concrete platform, and dropped to one knee beside Maya.

The terrifying, icy mask of the *Kinap* vanished instantly, replaced by an expression of fierce, protective gentleness that softened every hard angle of his weathered face.

"Maya," he whispered, his large, scarred hands reaching out to hover over her shoulders, not touching her until she nodded. "Are you whole? Did he break the skin on your throat?"

Maya drew a long, shuddering breath, her hand coming away from her neck. Her skin was marked with red, angry fingermarks where the stocky man had clamped down, but the trachea was intact, and her airway was clear.

"I'm... I'm whole, Mac," she rasped, her voice rough and breathless. "My throat is bruised, and my ankle took a hard knock against the boxcar, but nothing is re-broken. I’m alright."

Mac reached behind him, stripped his heavy, wool-lined pea-coat off his broad shoulders, and wrapped the warm, heavy fabric around her trembling torso, covering her torn flannel shirt.

The coat smelled cleanly of woodsmoke, cedar resin, and the deep, reassuring warmth of the hearth.

Between her breasts, a tiny, furious red head popped up from the fleece cup of her bra.

Scar was covered in dirt, dried pine needles, and a smear of grease across his whiskers, but his black eyes were blazing with victorious, homicidal glee.

He scampered up onto Maya's collarbone, turned his little face toward the broken raiders in the ballast, puffed out his chest until he looked like a russet tennis ball, and let out an ear-piercing, triumphant boreal battle screech:

*CHRRRRRRRR-CHIP-CHIP-CHIP!*

*WE HAVE CRUSHED THE COLD-SMOKE VERMIN!* Scar screamed telepathically across the clearing. *THE HIGH DUPLEX REMAINS UNCONQUERED! I BIT THE SHOOTING-APE IN THE EAR! HIS BLOOD TASTED OF CHEAP SALT! THE LANDLORD HAS DEFENDED THE PREMISES!*

Maya let out a weak, breathless laugh that turned into a ragged cough, raising one finger to gently stroke the squirrel’s fiery fur.

"You were magnificent, Scar," she whispered. "An absolute lunatic, but magnificent."

Mac looked at the squirrel, a faint, wry smile touching his beard, before his blue eyes swept over the concrete platform and found the two-quart tin can of EP-2 moly grease and the wooden box of tap-and-die cutters sitting in her canvas haversack.

He looked at the tools. Then he looked at Maya.

His eyebrows rose in slow, comprehension-filled amazement.

"You walked a mile down the tracks on a nine-day-old fracture..." Mac murmured, "...to scavenge grease for the draisine?"

Maya looked down at her hands, a fierce, defensive flush of bronze rising to her cheeks.

"I'm not dead weight, Mac," she said, her voice trembling slightly with fierce, stubborn pride. "The wheel journals were dry. You need those bearings greased before we take the rails to Fort Folly, or the bronze sleeves will burn out in ten miles. I wasn't going to sit on the porch like a useless invalid while you did all the heavy lifting."

Mac stared at her for five long seconds.

Then, he let out a deep, soft sigh that was half-chuckle and half-reverence, shaking his massive head.

"By the salt and the shale, woman," Mac rumbled, his voice thick with profound respect. "You have the stubbornness of a Fundy mule and the heart of a timber wolf. But next time you decide to mount an industrial salvage expedition, tell your partner so he can bring his axe."

He reached out, retrieved her white ash staff from the gravel where it had fallen, inspected the straight grain to ensure it hadn't cracked, and handed it back to her.

"Now," Mac said, rising to his full height, his tone hardening back into that cold, uncompromising edge of frontier justice. "Let's deal with the machines."

He stepped off the concrete platform, walking down onto the railway ballast where the three enduro dirt bikes sat: one still idling with a greasy *chug-chug-chug*, the other two lying on their sides in the ferns.

Cody, still groaning on his knees, looked up through tears of pain, his eyes widening in horror as he realized what Mac was about to do.

"No... no, man... not the bikes..." Cody wheezed, his voice cracking. "Those are two-fifties! Pre-collapse Japanese engines! They're worth more than gold in Sussex! Take our guns! Take our fuel! Don't touch the bikes!"

Mac didn't answer.

He walked to the green Kawasaki.

He reached down with his bare right hand, gripped the heavy tubular steel handlebars, planted his boots against the railway ties, and heaved upward.

*SCREEEEEECH-SNAP!*

The four hardened steel mounting bolts sheared off the aluminum triple-tree clamps like dry spaghetti. Mac ripped the handlebars completely off the machine, tearing the throttle cables, front brake lines, and wiring harness in a shower of severed copper and black rubber.

He dropped the mangled bars into the dirt.

Then, Mac bent his knees, gripped the rear subframe with his right hand and the front telescopic fork tubes with his left, and engaged his Kinap strength.

With a low, guttural grunt that rattled the stones beneath his boots, Mac lifted the three-hundred-and-twenty-pound running motorcycle cleanly off the ground, raising the machine above his head like an Olympian pressing a barbell.

The rear wheel was still spinning, spitting gravel into the air.

Mac turned toward the edge of the railway cutting, where the sixty-foot sandstone cliff dropped sheer into the roaring, boulder-choked rapids of Stony Creek.

He threw the motorcycle.

The green machine sailed thirty feet through the empty morning air, turning end over end, before striking the jagged red boulders at the bottom of the gorge.

*KABLAM-CRUNCH-SHATTER!*

The gas tank ruptured on a granite point. The aluminum engine cases split wide open, spraying forty weight oil and gasoline across the wet rocks. The frame snapped at the steering head, the rear wheel buckled into a pretzel, and the churning brown waters of the creek swallowed the twisted iron carcass, tumbling it downstream toward the Petitcodiac.

Cody let out a dry, strangled sob, burying his face in his good elbow.

Mac turned toward the second bike—the red Honda.

"WAIT! MAC, STOP!" Maya shouted from the loading dock, leaning heavily on her ash staff, hobbling forward to the edge of the concrete.

Mac paused, his hands already hovering over the Honda's fuel tank, looking back at her with an arched eyebrow.

"You want to throw this one yourself?" he asked.

"No, you magnificent brute!" Maya yelled, her engineer's heart screaming in agony at the destruction of viable technology. "That Honda has an unbroken 520 O-ring drive chain! It has an unpitted Mikuni slide carburetor, two clean NGK spark plugs, a twelve-volt sealed AGM battery, and four gallons of pure, unmixed high-octane gasoline in the tank! Do not throw fifty pounds of irreplaceable industrial gold into a river!"

Mac blinked. He looked down at the red bike, then back at Maya's furious, grease-smudged face.

A slow, broad grin spread across his beard.

"My apologies, chief engineer," Mac smiled, stepping back with his hands raised. "The machine is yours."

Maya limped down the wooden ramp, leaning her weight firmly on the ash cane in her right hand, carrying her haversack and pry-bar in her left.

Between her breasts, Scar peeked out, watching the motorcycle with intense suspicion, but seeing Maya's calm, predatory focus, he settled down to supervise.

Maya worked with the swift, ruthless efficiency of a master mechanic stripping a prize:

She popped the fuel line off the petcock, draining three-and-a-half gallons of clear, sweet-smelling high-octane gasoline directly into the clean metal pails she had dragged from the shed, sealing them with tight friction lids.

Using her small wrench set, she pulled the primary drive chain—twenty-four feet of hardened steel links that would serve as the secondary drive on their rail draisine. She unscrewed the two spark plugs, wrapping them in an oiled rag, unbolted the carburetor, and unhooked the compact lead-calcium battery that could power their optical Clacks lantern for six months.

She backed away from the stripped frame, wiping a smear of black grease from her chin, looking at Mac with fierce, cold satisfaction.

"Alright," Maya nodded, stepping back onto the platform. "The carcass is dead. You may destroy the remains."

Mac didn't bother lifting this one.

He picked up a twelve-pound railway track sledge from the maintenance bench.

He swung the sledge in a high, circular overhead arc, bringing the hardened steel face down squarely onto the center of the Honda's aluminum crankcase.

*KABAAAANG!*

The cast-aluminum engine block shattered like a dinner plate. The crankshaft was bent double, the cylinder head was sheared off its studs, and the transmission gears were crushed into a pile of useless metallic teeth.

Mac kicked the ruined frame off the embankment into the gorge, watching it tumble down the shale to join the first bike in the rushing brown water.

The message of the river was complete:

The machines were dead. The steel was broken. And the power of the road was dust.

### Part VI: The Decrees of the Sovereign Valley

Ten minutes later, the clearing had been cleared of weapons, fuel, and iron.

The young, wiry raider was bound securely to the heavy oak workbench inside the maintenance shed, his hands tied behind his back with his own nylon zip-ties, his face cleaned of blood, weeping quietly in the gloom.

"You'll be out in twenty-four hours when the wood dries and the zip-ties stretch," Mac told the boy through the doorway, his tone stern but devoid of cruelty. "There is water in the barrel by the door. If you have any sense left in that young skull of yours, when you get loose, you will walk south toward the fishing coves of Cape Enrage and learn how to pull a cod-line like an honest man. If you ever show your face on this railway corridor again, Nipn will not wait for my command."

The boy nodded frantically, his eyes wide with terrified gratitude. "I swear... I swear by the tides, mister... I'll never touch a rifle again..."

Mac shut the heavy timber door, dropping the exterior iron bar into place.

Out on the railway ballast, the stocky man who had choked Maya was slowly regaining consciousness, groaning and retching in the weeds, his shattered wrist bound tightly in a clean strip of canvas Maya had provided from her medical kit.

And kneeling in the sharp granite stones between the twin steel rails was Cody.

The rat-tailed leader’s face was ashen, slick with cold sweat from the shock of his compound radius fracture. His right arm was tucked into his jacket, cradled against his ribs, his breath coming in shallow, shuddering gasps.

Mac stood over him like an ancient granite monolith, his boots planted wide across the ties, the twelve-gauge shotgun he had unloaded and bent into a U-shape resting in his hands like a piece of twisted scrap wire.

Nipn sat two paces behind Cody, his cold black nose hovering six inches from the man's neck, his yellow eyes tracking every twitch of the raider's tendons.

Maya stood beside Mac, resting comfortably on her white ash staff, her good boot taking her full weight, her canvas vest zipped up around the warm, furry ball of Scar, who had his front paws planted on her collarbone like a judge about to hand down a capital sentence.

Mac tossed the bent shotgun into the ditch with a heavy *clatter*.

He looked down at Cody.

"What is Vance’s strength in Sussex?" Mac asked.

His voice wasn't loud, but it held that deep, resonant, subterranean timbre that made the gravel beneath Cody's knees vibrate.

Cody swallowed hard, his voice cracking into a ragged whisper.

"T-thirty-five men," Cody stammered, terrified that any hesitation would bring the wolf's fangs into his jugular. "Maybe forty. But half of 'em are sick with the river fever, and the fuel is almost gone. We only had two drums of kerosene left at the highway truck stop. That’s why Vance sent us down the tracks... he heard rumors that the old Acadian dikes down on the Petitcodiac had salt pans and dried fish... he was planning to bring two flatbed trucks down Route 106 before the harvest..."

Maya let out a cold, bitter laugh from the loading dock.

"Two flatbed trucks?" she said, her dark eyes flashing with contempt. "Route 106 has four washed-out concrete culverts between Salisbury and Hillsborough, and the bridge over the Memramcook was blown with dynamite four years ago. Vance couldn't get a wheelbarrow down that road, let alone a flatbed."

"He... he didn't know that," Cody wheezed, tears of pain leaking from his swollen eyes. "He just knew you ran this way... and he thought if we dragged you back, you could fix the diesel pumps at the refinery..."

Mac stepped forward, his heavy leather boot coming to rest on the steel rail three inches from Cody's knee.

"Listen to me, scavenger," Mac said, his voice dropping into that terrifyingly cold register that held the finality of winter ice.

Cody looked up, his jaw trembling.

"You came into this valley to drag an autonomous human being into servitude," Mac said, his pale blue eyes pinning the man to the ground. "Under the ancient laws of *Netukulimk*, you are not a man. You are a rabid coyote that has blundered into a protected clearing. By every law of the woods, I should throw your carcass into the Stony Creek gorge with your motorcycles."

Cody’s breath hitched in his throat, his face draining of whatever color remained.

"P-please..." he choked out.

"I am not going to kill you," Mac continued, his tone immovable as a mountain. "Because a dead dog cannot carry a message."

Mac reached down with his right hand, gripped the collar of Cody's heavy leather riding jacket, and with a single, effortless rip, tore the jacket from the man's shoulders, throwing it onto the salvage pile beside Maya.

Then, Mac pointed a thick, scarred finger at Cody's heavy motorcycle boots.

"Take them off."

Cody blinked through his tears. "What?"

"Take off your boots," Mac repeated, without raising his voice by half a decibel. "And your socks."

"M-man... it’s forty miles of shale and briars back to Sussex..." Cody sobbed, his voice cracking with pure despair. "My feet will be shredded to the bone! The black flies will eat me alive!"

"Then you had better walk fast," Mac replied coldly. "A man who hunts women across the mud does not deserve leather between his skin and the earth. You will walk every single mile back to Vance on your bare, bleeding feet. Every time a sharp stone cuts your heel, every time a briar tears your calf, you will remember that you are alive only because the woman you tried to violate showed you mercy."

Cody looked at Maya.

Maya stood tall on the concrete dock, her chin held high, her eyes cold, hard, and unblinking.

Inside her shirt, Scar poked his whiskered snout out, bared his tiny incisors, and gave a sharp, spitting hiss that sealed the decree.

Cody broke completely.

With his trembling, uninjured left hand, he unbuckled his heavy combat boots, pulling them off his feet, stripping away his wool socks, leaving his pale, soft city feet resting on the freezing, razor-sharp granite ballast of the track.

Mac reached down, pulled Cody to his feet by his belt, spun him toward the north, and gave him a single, firm shove down the railway ties.

"Walk, scavenger," Mac's voice echoed through the hemlocks like a rolling thunderstorm. "Go back to Sussex. Tell Vance that the Petkotkweyak belongs to Kwitn. Tell him the girl is sovereign under the shield of the Old Stone Light. And tell him that if one more motorized beast comes south of the Salisbury line, I will not wait at the river. I will walk into Sussex with thirty men from Fort Folly, and we will tear his compound down to the foundation stones."

Cody didn't look back.

He stumbled down the railway bed on his bare feet, his knees shaking, his broken arm clutched to his belly, whimpering with every step as the sharp granite stones cut into his soles, disappearing into the northern fog like a ghost retreating from the dawn.

Beside Mac, Nipn lifted his muzzle toward the gray sky and let out a long, magnificent, spine-chilling howl that rolled five miles down the gorge, declaring to the entire valley that the northern gate was closed, defended, and sovereign.

### Part VII: The Return to the Hearth & The Sacred Union

By the time the noon sun had burned the last silver tendrils of fog from the Petitcodiac valley, the journey home had become a slow, triumphant procession.

Mac had refused to let Maya walk a single step on her bruised ankle.

He had dragged an old four-wheeled wooden baggage trolley from the maintenance shed—a low, flat timber cart built for hauling rail freight—greased its iron wheel spindles with a handful of the salvaged EP-2 moly grease, loaded the three sealed tins of high-octane gasoline, the tool boxes, the primary drive chain, and their haversacks onto the front, and laid his heavy wool pea-coat across the rear timbers to form a cushioned seat.

Maya sat on the trolley like an empress on a moving throne, her splinted left leg propped straight across the freight tins, her white ash staff resting across her lap like a scepter.

Mac walked behind the cart, his massive hands resting on the oak push-bar, propelling the four-hundred-pound load down the smooth steel rails with that effortless, unhurried Kinap strength that made fifty yards vanish in thirty seconds.

Beside the rails, Nipn trotted smoothly through the ferns, his silver coat gleaming in the warm June sun, his head held high, his nose sampling the clean, peaceful air of an unthreatened valley.

And wedged firmly, snugly, and unapologetically between Maya's calcium cannons was Scar.

The squirrel was exhausted from his heroics. He was curled into a tight, warm ball inside the fleece cup of her bra, his chin propped over her third button, his eyes half-closed as he watched the spruce trees slide past at ten miles an hour. Every few minutes, he would let out a soft, purring whistle of supreme comfort, nuzzle his warm nose into her cleavage, and drift back to sleep.

Maya looked down at the little creature, then back over her shoulder at the massive, bearded giant pushing the trolley with untaxed grace.

The adrenaline of the morning had completely ebbed away, replaced by a deep, hollow ache in her muscles, a throbbing warmth in her bruised throat, and an overwhelming, humbling realization that tightened her chest.

"Mac," she said softly, her voice still rough from the raider's grip.

Mac slowed his pace, looking down at her with warm, clear blue eyes.

"Aye, Maya?"

"I'm sorry," she whispered, looking down at her scarred, grease-stained hands resting on the ash staff. "I was an idiot. I thought I had to prove that I wasn't dead weight. I thought... after Sussex, I thought if I didn't produce something valuable every single day, if I couldn't carry my own food, someone would decide I wasn't worth keeping around."

Mac stopped the trolley in the middle of the sun-drenched railway crossing, right where the private spur branched off toward the homestead bluffs.

He walked around to the side of the cart, dropping to one knee on the gravel ballast so his eyes were level with hers.

His large, scarred face held zero trace of judgment or reproach. It held only that deep, ancient, immovable warmth that felt like sitting in front of a stone hearth during a December gale.

"Listen to me, little sister," Mac said gently, reaching out with his broad, rough thumb to wipe a streak of dried black grease from the soft curve of her cheek. "You listen to every word I am about to say, and you carve it into your bones."

Maya met his gaze, her dark eyes shining with unshed tears.

"In the old world of the cities," Mac said softly, "people measured human worth like livestock in an auction barn. How many hours could you work? How many numbers could you punch into a computer? How much money could you make for someone else? And when you broke down, they threw you into the ditch and replaced you with a new part."

He laid his hand flat over her knuckles on the ash staff.

"That world is dead, Maya," Mac said, his baritone vibrating through the wood. "And it deserved to die. Here, under the law of *Netukulimk*, human worth is not measured in output. You are a sister of this hearth because you have an honest heart, because you carry the spark of life, and because you survived the frost to sit at our fire. You do not have to buy your bread with bruises. You do not have to prove your right to breathe this air."

A single tear escaped Maya's eye, tracing a clean path through the road dust on her cheek.

Mac smiled—a slow, radiant, brotherly smile that made the lines around his eyes crinkle with warmth.

"Besides," Mac added with a dry chuckle, nodding toward her lap, "you designed a three-to-one compound overdrive transmission from scrap iron, you spark-tested spring steel from a rusty truck, you brought home four gallons of high-test gasoline and forty pounds of moly grease, and your resident squirrel nearly tore a man's ear off with his bare teeth. If that is what you call being 'dead weight,' then I hope to the tides we find three more of you before the winter."

Maya burst into a bright, breathless laugh that shattered the last lingering shadows of the morning's trauma.

She reached up, wrapping her arms around Mac's thick neck, burying her face into the rough wool of his shoulder.

Mac held her—solid, steady, and immovable as an anchor dropped in sixty fathoms of blue water—patting her back with his broad palm until her trembling ceased.

Inside her shirt, Scar poked his head up between her breasts, looked at Mac's bearded jaw three inches away, gave a soft, approving *chrrr*, and licked a speck of salt from Maya's collarbone before curling back into his nest.

Ten minutes later, the trolley rolled into the crushed clam-shell courtyard of the homestead.

The hemlock cabin stood warm and welcoming in the afternoon sun, a thin plume of fragrant birch smoke curling from the stone chimney.

Mac helped Maya off the cart, carrying her up the cedar porch steps and settling her comfortably into the high-backed ash armchair by the hearth. He brought a basin of warm spring water, a soft linen cloth, and washed the dust, sweat, and road mud from her face, arms, and neck with gentle, methodical care.

From the root cellar, he brought fresh comfrey leaves, pounding them in a stone mortar with cold river silt and wild mint, applying a fresh, soothing poultice to her bruised throat and her stiff left ankle, binding the joint with clean linen strips.

On the cast-iron cooktop, the heavy Griswold skillet was soon sizzling again:

Six fresh fillets of winter flounder, fried to golden, flaky perfection in sweet butter; a mound of steaming, hot johnnycakes baked from Auntie Elizabeth’s flint meal; and a steaming earthenware teapot filled with wild chamomile, dried raspberry leaves, and clover honey to soothe her bruised throat.

Maya sat in the armchair, wrapped in Mac’s heavy pea-coat, savoring the rich, buttery fish and the sweet, nutty corn cakes.

On her chest, nestled securely between her calcium cannons, Scar was feasting on his own private banquet: three fat butternut halves and a small pile of roasted pumpkin seeds Mac had laid out on a clean cedar shingle resting on her lap. The squirrel was stuffing his cheek pouches until his face looked like a pair of fuzzy russet oranges, occasionally pausing to reach up and groom a loose strand of Maya's dark hair with his tiny front paws.

Beside the hearth, Nipn lay stretched out in the sunbeams, his belly full of fish heads, his silver tail giving a lazy, rhythmic thump against the hemlock floorboards.

Outside the thick cabin walls, eighty square miles of Acadian wilderness stretched out under the summer sky: peaceful, wild, and utterly sovereign.

The raiders were broken. The northern gate was closed. The draisine was greased and ready for the rails.

And as the great Fundy tidal bore began its afternoon surge up the red gorge below, Maya closed her eyes, leaned her head back against the carved ash spindles, and felt the unbreakable, eternal peace of a woman who was finally, undeniably safe.

### Part VIII: The Greased Journals & The Midnight Pact

By nightfall, the summer air had settled into that crisp, cool stillness that always followed a Fundy clearing gale.

The sky arched over the Petitcodiac like a vast dome of black velvet, pierced by thousands of diamond-sharp northern stars that cast faint, silver highlights along the steel rails of the spur line. Down on the mud flats, thirty square miles of red sediment were breathing in the cold Atlantic brine as the evening tide rolled up from Shepody Bay.

Inside the cabin, the meal was finished, the trencher plates were washed and drying on the rack, and the bruised ache in Maya’s throat had softened under the healing warmth of hot chamomile and clover honey.

Mac pulled on his wool work waistcoat, took down two brass hurricane lanterns from the ceiling beams, and lit the braided cotton wicks.

"Come, sister," Mac said softly, his voice warm with anticipation. "Before we sleep, there is one last debt of engineering to settle."

Maya reached for her white ash staff resting against the table.

She stood.

Her left ankle, freshly poulticed with cold comfrey and bound in clean linen, took forty pounds of her weight with only a mild, dull stiffness. Leaning her palm onto the rock-maple knob of the staff, she walked across the hemlock floorboards with smooth, confident balance.

*Tap-step. Tap-step.*

Between her breasts, Scar felt the shift in momentum. The little squirrel poked his whiskered nose up through the open fourth button of her flannel shirt, rested his front paws on her collarbone, and blinked at the lantern light with sleepy, contented curiosity.

"You don't have to carry me, Mac," Maya smiled, stepping through the front door onto the cedar porch. "The leg is holding."

"I see it is," Mac rumbled, holding the lantern high to illuminate the crushed clam-shell path. "The bone knows it has work to do."

They walked down the gentle slope to the railway siding, where the four-wheeled ash draisine stood parked beneath the overhanging spruce boughs.

Mac set one lantern on the tamarack deck and the other on the ballast beside the rear axle.

He brought out the two-quart tin of EP-2 lithium-moly grease Maya had brought back from the Stony Creek maintenance shed, along with a bundle of clean, teased cotton waste from the tool chest.

Maya sat on an upturned nail keg beside the rear wheel, her ash staff resting between her knees, watching with an engineer’s critical eye.

"Pop the journal covers first, Mac," she directed, pointing with her thumb. "Clean out any grit before you pack the grease."

Mac took a small flat cold chisel and tapped the four cast-iron journal box lids.

The spring-loaded covers snapped open with a sharp, metallic *clink*.

Inside the heavy cast housings, the cylindrical bronze sleeve bearings surrounded the polished journals of their newly forged 5160 steel axle. The metal was clean, but dry as autumn birchbark.

Maya reached into the tin with a wooden paddle, scooping up a thick, buttery dollop of dark-gray moly grease.

She handed it to Mac.

"Pack it into the bottom cavity first," Maya instructed, her voice crisp with technical authority. "Saturate the cotton waste until it looks like soaked moss, then tuck it beneath the journal. As the axle turns, the cotton acts like an oil wick, lifting a continuous film of grease against the rotating steel under hydrodynamic pressure."

Mac worked with that terrifying, delicate precision that made his massive, calloused fingers look like a surgeon’s instruments.

He packed the four journal boxes methodically: pressing the lithium grease into every crevice, saturating the cotton packing, smoothing the surface, and snapping the spring lids shut with a solid, weather-tight *thwack*.

Next, they took the heavy 520 motorcycle chain Maya had stripped from the red Honda.

Mac wrapped the chain around the twenty-four-tooth countershaft sprocket and the sixteen-tooth drive pinion on the rear axle. Maya adjusted the slotted tensioner blocks with a forged wrench, aligning the sprockets until the chain had precisely half an inch of vertical deflection—tight enough to prevent tooth-skipping under shock load, loose enough to avoid parasitic bearing drag.

She dipped her fingers into the grease tin, running a thin, even coat of molybdenum disulfide along the hardened roller links.

"Now," Maya said, wiping her fingers on an oily rag, looking up at Mac with a challenge dancing in her eyes. "Give her a test."

Mac climbed onto the tamarack deck of the handcar.

He did not grab the oak walking beam with both hands. He did not brace his boots against the deck timbers.

He simply reached down with his right index finger, hooked the tip of his finger over the ash pump handle, and applied five pounds of downward pressure.

The result was pure mechanical poetry.

With that single five-pound touch, the 3:1 compound transmission engaged with a faint, musical *click*.

The two-stage chain drive spun silently. The four twenty-inch chilled-iron wheels revolved on their freshly greased bronze bearings with zero friction, zero squeal, and zero vibration.

The four-hundred-pound draisine surged forward along the spur track, gliding through the darkness with the terrifying, whispered speed of an owl swooping through the hemlock canopy, coasting fifty yards down the siding until Mac touched the boiled-maple brake lever, bringing the machine to a smooth, progressive halt without a single spark.

Mac stood on the deck in the starlight, his hands resting on the oak thwart, looking back at Maya with sheer, unbounded wonder in his eyes.

"By the salt and the shale," Mac breathed, his voice carrying the awe of a man who had witnessed a marvel of physics. "A single finger. You can push half a ton of steel and wood with a single finger."

Maya leaned on her white ash staff, rising from the nail keg, a radiant, triumphant grin illuminating her bronze features in the lantern light.

"That is what happens when you respect the tolerances, Mac," she said softly. "The machine doesn't fight the earth. It rides the arithmetic."

Mac stepped down from the deck, walking over to stand beside her in the cool grass.

Together, they looked at the completed machine sitting on the siding:

The heavy white ash frame gleaming with linseed oil.

The forty-foot mast stepped firmly into the oak block, its white ripstop canvas sail furled neatly along the boom.

On the forward cargo deck, strapped securely beneath heavy oilcloth tarpaulins, rested their trade goods for Fort Folly:

Sixty pounds of split, smoked spring gaspereau, golden-brown and fragrant with sweet birch smoke; two heavy wooden firkins of coarse white sea-salt harvested from the sandstone evaporation pans; four sealed linen bags containing Auntie Elizabeth’s heritage squash, bean, and flint corn seeds; and the three sealed metal cans holding four gallons of pristine, high-octane gasoline for Noel’s forge blower.

To the south, eight miles down the red river gorge, the dark water of Shepody Bay shimmered in the starlight.

The road to the north was closed. The raiders in Sussex had their answer. The gate of the valley was secure.

And at dawn, when the great Fundy tidal bore swept past the bluffs, the southwest wind would fill the white sail, the chilled-iron wheels would sing against the steel rails, and the fortress of the bends would take to the road.

Maya looked down at her chest.

Between her breasts, Scar had popped his whiskered snout out from the fleece of her bra, resting his front paws on her collarbone, looking down the long, straight iron track with bright, fearless black eyes.

The squirrel gave a soft, rhythmic *chrrr* of deep, unshakeable contentment, nuzzled his head against her skin, and closed his eyes.

Maya reached up, her hand resting over her heart, feeling the steady, warm pulse of the tiny creature, the cool river wind on her face, and the immovable presence of the man standing at her side.

"We go to Fort Folly at first light, Kwitn?" she asked softly, speaking his true name with absolute, effortless clarity.

Mac looked down at her, his pale blue eyes reflecting the northern stars, a slow, gentle smile touching his weathered face.

"At first light, Maya," Mac replied softly. "The rails are clear, the wind is waiting, and the people of the red river are ready."

High above them on the promontory, seventy-four granite steps held centuries of human memory safe and dry within the Old Stone Light.

Down in the gorge, forty feet of cold ocean water swept past the bluffs, carrying the ancient song of the tide to the listening woods.

And beneath the quiet stars, the hearth was defended, the seed was kept, and the new world was awake.
