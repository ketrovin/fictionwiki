# Chapter 13: The Clacks of the Salt Ridge

### I. The Shutter on the High Gallery

The wind that came off the Cumberland Basin an hour before dawn had lost the razor iron of January, but it was not gentle. It smelled of rotting kelp, cold salt flats, and the deep, sour breath of the exposed tidal silt twenty fathoms below the cliff edge. It beat against the western curve of the lighthouse lantern room with a steady, resonant drumming—the heavy timber framing of the sixty-foot tower groaning in its iron tie-rods, anchored twelve feet down into the solid red sandstone of the headland.

Maya stood inside the glass cupola, her palms resting flat against the curved copper sill. The glass was four inches thick in the lower panes, ground plate from an older century, pitted here and there by eighty years of airborne sand and frozen spray, but clear enough where Mac had scrubbed the salt rind away with hot lye water. Beside her, resting upright against the copper-clad doorframe, stood her white ash staff. Its iron ferrule clicked faintly against the checkered cast-iron deck plates whenever the tower shifted in the gust. 

Her right hand did not let go of the wood. Her fingers had learned its grain during the three weeks of slow, stubborn healing: the tight white rings of the second-growth trunk Mac had split with cedar wedges, the three-inch oval swell near the throat where her grip balanced the lower taper, and the hard bronze shoe bolted through the base. It was four inches taller than her shoulder, oiled with six coats of raw flaxseed until it felt warm even in the dawn chill. When she breathed, the deep ache under her left fourth rib was no longer a hot knife; it was a tight, scarred stitch of muscle, grumbling when the humidity climbed, but holding its ground. Her hip took her full weight now without the sickening, hollow trembling of the first fortnight.

Inside the deep, fleece-lined pouch of her woolen overshirt, tucked tight between the heavy wool of her mended sweater and the warm swell of her chest, Scar was a motionless three-pound ball of dark russet fur. Only the rhythmic, rapid flutter of his ribs and the wet black nub of his nose peeking out through the top button betrayed that he was awake. His small black claws were hooked securely into the heavy rib-knit yarn over her sternum. The squirrel did not care about the tactical geography of southern New Brunswick, nor did he care about the shattered rail line running south through the Tantramar marshes; he cared that the temperature between Maya’s flannel layers was ninety-eight point six degrees, that her heartbeat was a steady, rhythmic thrumming under his chin, and that the pocket smelled of parched corn kernels and dried beach cranberries. In exchange for that warmth, he was an engine with ears that could catch the click of a rifle safety at four hundred yards through a gale.

Mac was kneeling on the southern quadrant of the catwalk deck, his back to the wind, adjusting the tension wire on the Clacks frame. 

The signaling shutter was his own design, built during the dead weeks of February from salvaged white cedar slats, copper telegraph wire, and two counterbalanced brass piano hinges stripped from an upright Baldwin that had rotted in a collapsed summer cottage down near Dorchester Cape. It stood five feet high on a swiveling ash pedestal bolted to the exterior iron gallery railing, facing southwest along the sightline that cleared the timber ridge of Fort Folly and dropped straight down the throat of Shepody Bay.

"Louver three is dragging," Mac said, his voice flat beneath the whistle of the wind. He didn't turn around. He was wearing his grease-cured moose-hide vest over two layers of boiled grey wool, the sleeves rolled to his forearms despite the frost on the copper flashing. His broad hands, scarred across the knuckles from thirty years of cold iron and green timber, held a pair of long-nosed pliers forged from an old railway spike. "The salt damp swelled the cedar rebate. Give the foot pedal a kick, Maya. Let me see the return spring bite."

Maya shifted her weight onto her right heel, her ash staff planted firm on the iron plates, and stepped down onto the forged treadle linked to the lower lever rod.

The seven cedar louvers snapped open with a sharp, wooden *clack-clack-clack*, revealing the double parabolic kerosene reflector mounted behind them. The reflector was a twenty-inch curved dish of silver-plated copper, salvaged from the locomotive headlight of a dead Canadian National diesel engine down in the Moncton freight yards. Behind its focal point burned a four-wick whale-oil lantern, its mantle glowing with a piercing, sun-white incandescence that cut through the predawn haze like a clean blade.

Maya released the treadle. The counterweight—a three-pound lead fishing sinker on a bronze cable—dropped smoothly, and the seven louvers slapped shut, extinguishing the beam instantly.

"Good," Mac said, sighting along the top battens toward the dark, humpbacked shoulder of Fort Folly five miles across the grey water. "Clear throw. The fog is lying low in the mud gutters, but the ridge is high enough to catch the pulse. Give them the muster roll first. Two long, three short, hold on the red."

Maya took her stance, her left hand steadying her frame against the copper mullion, her right hand clamped to the polished ash staff, her right boot working the treadle with deliberate, rhythmic pressure. 

*Clack. Clack.* Two two-second open pulses, sending two great white shafts of light stabbing across the dark waters of the bay. 

*Clack-clack-clack.* Three quick half-second staccato bursts. 

Then she dropped the brass color-slide into the slot before the lantern lens. The next beam flared blood-red against the low cloud ceiling—the sovereign signature of the Petitcodiac lighthouse, the signal that Noel and Auntie Elizabeth knew across twenty miles of timber and salt marsh.

They waited. The wind took the black smoke from the lantern vent and whipped it away north toward the bends. Maya pulled the collar of her mackinaw tighter against her throat. Beneath her woolen layers, Scar’s nose twitched; a tiny paw reached up, patted the curve of her collarbone with cold, leathery pads, and tucked back down into the fleece.

"Five miles of water," Maya muttered, squinting south into the grey gloom where the sky met the dark crest of the Maringouin Peninsula. "If Noel's spotter is asleep by the stove, I’m going to make him haul the next load of pig iron up the cliff by hand."

"Noel doesn't sleep after four in the morning," Mac said, standing up and wiping his pliers with an oily rag. "Not when the ice is getting soft. In the spring of twenty-five, when the gorge broke at Salisbury, he was on the mudflats before the sun was over the spruce, pulling stranded sturgeon out of the drift before the eagles could pick their eyes."

Three miles south-southwest, near the highest contour of the Fort Folly ridge, a needle-point of yellow light blinked out of the dark trees.

*One. Two. Pause. One, two, three.*

"Auntie Elizabeth is on the glass," Mac murmured, his dark eyes narrowing as he read the flicker. "She uses the brass transit we took off the hydrographic survey cutter. She counts three seconds between pulses. Read it out."

Maya watched the distant pinprick flare and die against the black timberline. Her mind moved through the cipher grid they had scratched into the lead plate on the chart table: 

*V-A-N-C-E. S-T-I-R-R-I-N-G.*

The light paused for five seconds, then began a rapid, rhythmic flutter:

*A-M-H-E-R-S-T. W-H-A-L-E-B-O-A-T-S. E-I-G-H-T. C-R-E-W-S. F-O-R-T-Y. P-I-N-T-L-E. B-R-O-W-N-I-N-G-S.*

Maya’s jaw tightened. "Eight whaleboats. Forty men. Pintle-mounted Brownings. That matches what the scout on the sugar ridge spat out before his chest gave out."

"Forty conscripts and ten militia regulars to hold the gunwales," Mac corrected quietly. "Vance isn't sending his veterans to pull eight-foot ash oars through four knots of Petitcodiac mud. He’ll put six farm boys from Cumberland County on each thwart with an NCO in the stern sheet holding a service automatic to their ears. And the Brownings will be fifty-calibers stripped from the armory's half-tracks."

"Can a fifty-caliber mount to a cedar whaleboat without ripping the gunwale out on the first burst?" Maya asked, watching the distant yellow light continue its staccato clatter across the bay.

"Not if you fire abeam," Mac said. "The recoil will roll a twenty-six-foot boat right onto its beam-ends in this tide. But if they mount them on the stem post over the apron with an iron knee, firing straight ahead on the flood, they can lay two hundred rounds of ball across the mud shelf before the bow even touches the gravel."

The distant light flickered three long pulses, then went dark.

*M-E-E-T. R-A-I-L. S-I-D-I-N-G. N-O-O-N.*

"They're coming up on the handcar," Maya said. She stepped off the treadle, pulled the brass red-slide, and locked the Clacks louvers with the iron hook. "Noel wants the iron."

"Noel wants the rifles," Mac said. "The two Timberwolves we pulled off the ridge. Fort Folly has twenty men who can shoot, but half of them are carrying single-shot Cooey twenty-twos and rusty bolt-action Lee-Enfields with the rifling shot smooth forty years ago. A match-grade three-oh-eight with a Schmidt and Bender twenty-four-power scope is worth more than five ton of hay down on the point."

Maya leaned on her staff, feeling the satisfying solidness of the ash beneath her hand. "We aren't giving those rifles away for free, Mac. I spent three hours boiling the grease out of their bolts and cleaning the mud out of their recoil lugs. We need lead. We need gaspereau. And if we don't get seed potatoes that haven't sprouted to mush, we're going to be eating boiled cattail roots until August."

Mac looked down at her, the faint ghost of a smile creasing the weathered red-brown leather of his cheeks. "We don't give anything away on this river. Noel knows the weight of salt as well as I do. Go down and check the sled. Put the grease boxes on the runners. We move as soon as the tide hits low slack."

### II. The Currency of Timberwolf Steel

The railway siding at the foot of the Belliveau bluff was buried under three feet of crusty, refrozen drift, but the iron rails of the old Intercolonial spur had begun to show through where the southern sun struck the embankment. 

Mac pulled the heavy cedar transport sled behind him, leaning into the broad chest harness he had braided from half-inch hemp rope and cured moose-hide. The sled runners, shod with two-inch strips of cold-rolled leaf spring steel, hissed over the frozen mud with the sound of tearing canvas. On the sled, wrapped in greased wool blankets and strapped down with raw rawhide tumplines, lay the two captured PGW Timberwolf .308 rifles, their heavy fluted stainless barrels packed in tallow, alongside three five-round steel box magazines and forty-eight rounds of match-grade Lapua brass.

Maya walked four paces behind him on the wind-scoured ballast, her ash staff clicking rhythmically against the exposed ties. She carried no load on her back—Mac had flatly refused to let her shoulder a pack until the spring equinox—but her right hand held the staff with a loose, ready grip, her shoulder muscles rolling easy beneath her mackinaw. Inside her coat, Scar was awake now, his head poking out past her third button, his small black eyes darting across the grey birch scrub that choked the drainage ditches. His whiskers twitched in the crosswind, tasting the air for the burnt-grease tang of weapons oil or the sour musk of unfamiliar men.

Around the bend of the cut, where the spruce gave way to the wide, bleached expanse of the Tantramar marsh flats, the rhythmic iron squeal of an ungreased axle drifted down the breeze.

A moment later, the handcar crawled into view along the rusty iron of the main line.

It was a four-man pump car of nineteenth-century manufacture, its walking beam rocking up and down like a seesaw in the hands of two broad-shouldered young Mi'kmaw men wearing grease-stained caribou jackets and sealskin caps. Standing in the center of the platform, leaning on a heavy oak steering brake, was Noel. 

He looked older in the pale March light than he had in the autumn, his long grey hair pulled back in a rawhide queue that whipped across his broad, flat cheekbones. Over his chest he wore a heavy canvas shooting coat lined with sheared beaver fur, the pockets sagging with shotgun shells and brass wire. Slung across his back by a strip of webbing was a short-barreled Remington 870 twelve-gauge, its blueing worn silver around the receiver.

As the handcar slowed, Noel leaned on the brake shoe with his full weight. The cast-iron blocks screamed against the rusty wheel rims, showering sparks onto the frozen gravel, and the car shuddered to a halt fifty feet from the siding switch.

"Mac," Noel called out, his voice gravel-rough from fifty years of tidal salt and woodsmoke. He stepped off the iron deck onto the frozen ties, his snowshoes lashed to the car's railing behind him. He looked down at the sled, then up at Maya, his sharp black eyes resting on the ash staff in her hand. "You're walking upright, girl. Auntie Elizabeth said you took three broken slats from a rifle butt on the sugar ridge."

"Two cracked, one bruised," Maya said evenly, not leaning on the staff, keeping her spine straight and her chin up. "And the man who swung the butt isn't swinging anything anymore."

Noel grunted, a short, approving rumble in his throat. "Good ash, that staff. Straight-grained. Mac cut it from the ridge behind the iron brook, didn't he? He always takes the ash that grows out of the rock seam. Stiff enough to vault a drainage ditch, springy enough to break a collarbone."

"It does what it's told," Maya said. 

Scar let out a sudden, high-pitched *chirr* from her chest—a warning trill no louder than a chickadee—and retreated two inches deeper into her fleece. Noel’s eyes snapped down to the bulge in her mackinaw, his hand twitching an inch toward his coat pocket before he caught himself.

"Still got that red spitfire in your shirt, I see," Noel said, his weathered face softening into an iron grin. "Auntie Elizabeth said that thing screeched loud enough to rattle the stove lids at Fort Folly when the raiders hit your sap camp."

"He doesn't like cold wind," Maya said, resting her left hand over the pocket, feeling the rapid, reassuring thrum of the squirrel's heart through her palm. "And he doesn't like strangers. What did you bring from the point?"

Noel gestured with his chin toward the flat deck of the handcar. The two young men on the pump beam—brothers from the Augustine family down at Dorchester—stepped aside, pulling back a greasy tarpaulin.

"Four pigs of iron," Noel said, ticking them off on his stubby, scarred fingers. "Forty pounds each, cast from the ballast keels of the old gypsum schooners that rotted in the Dorchester wharf. Good, soft grey iron, easy on the chisel, tough under the hammer. Two kegs of salt gaspereau, put up in brine last June before the weir rotted out. Sixty pounds of Kennebec seed potatoes—firm eyes, kept dry in the root cellar under five feet of dry sawdust. And three bushels of yellow flint corn that's been grown on the Fort Folly ridge since my great-grandfather had his hearth there."

Mac stepped forward, his boots crunching on the cinder ballast. He walked to the handcar, reached down with one hand, and lifted one of the forty-pound iron pigs by its cast lug as if it were a loaf of soda bread. He struck the metal with the corner of his pliers.

The iron gave a dull, clear *tonk* that resonated without buzzing.

"No sulfur," Mac said, nodding. "Good grey scrap. It'll weld clean to the spruce logs."

"And the guns?" Noel asked, his voice dropping an octave, his gaze shifting to the long bundle on the cedar sled.

Mac knelt, untied the rawhide tumplines, and folded back the greased wool.

The two Timberwolf rifles lay side by side on the bleached cedar planks, their matte-black synthetic stocks clean of grit, their heavy fluted stainless steel barrels gleaming with a light sheen of rendered bear grease. Beside them, Mac had laid out the three magazines, each loaded with five brass rounds, and the forty-eight loose Lapua cartridges nested in a dry cedar box packed with dried moss.

Noel walked to the sled slowly, as if approaching a skittish horse. He knelt in the snow, his thick fingers hovering over the cold steel of the first rifle before he touched it. He lifted the weapon with practiced, reverent care, balancing its twelve-pound mass across his forearms. He brought the butt to his shoulder, swung the barrel toward the distant timberline of the Tantramar marshes, and looked through the glass.

The 24-power Schmidt & Bender optic drew the morning light out of the grey sky, making the frosted branches three hundred yards away jump into razor-sharp relief. Noel worked the bolt. It slid back with the slick, mechanical *snick-click* of precision toolroom steel, cocking on the forward throw with zero play in the raceway.

"Vance's personal guard carry these," Noel whispered, his breath clouding the cold air. "The sniper platoon from the Amherst armory. They had four of them when the trouble started in the winter of twenty-four. How did two of them end up on your ridge?"

"A five-man patrol came up the ice gutter from the mouth of the Memramcook," Mac said, his tone flat, stripped of all boasting. "They had snowshoes and climbing irons. They thought the snow was deep enough to cover their noise. They were wrong."

Noel lowered the rifle, his dark eyes meeting Mac’s. "And the other three?"

"Under the high drift behind the blacksmith shop," Mac replied. "The foxes will find what’s left of them when the sun gets over the hill in May."

Noel let out a long, slow whistle through his teeth. "Vance is losing his temper, Mac. He’s got eighty men in the armory barracks on Acadia Street, but half of them are coughing their lungs into their blankets with the winter rot, and the other half are trading their ammunition to the townspeople for salt pork and dried apples. He needs a win before the ice goes out. If he can take your light, he controls the river from the mouth of the Petitcodiac all the way to the Moncton bend. He can tax every canoe, every scow, every net of gaspereau that comes down the water."

"He won't take the light," Maya said, her voice hard as the ash staff in her fist.

Noel looked at her, his brows lifting slightly. "He’s got eight whaleboats, girl. Old wooden double-enders from the fisheries patrol down at Saint John, hauled up across the marsh on flatcars before the rails were torn up. He’s got fifty men assigned to the oars and three heavy guns mounted on the bows. When the spring tide breaks the ice in two weeks, he’s coming up on the flood with the water under his keels. You can’t stop eighty feet of oak and iron with an ash stick."

"We aren't stopping them with a stick," Maya said, a cold, thin smile touching the corner of her lips. "We’re stopping them with the river."

Noel looked back at Mac, searching the silent woodsman’s face for confirmation. "What are you building, Mac?"

"A channel boom," Mac said simply. "Sixty-foot red spruce stringers across the narrow gut at the lower bend. Spiked with nine-inch iron teeth every eighteen inches, chained to the shale ledge on both sides with anchor link. When the flood brings his boats up through the narrows at six knots, his cedar hulls will hit those spikes before his lookouts can read the foam."

Noel stared at him for a long moment, then slowly nodded his head. "And if he beaches on the mud flats below the cliff?"

"The mud is six feet deep and cold enough to stop a man’s heart in four minutes," Mac said. "Let him beach."

Noel laid the Timberwolf back into its wool wrapper, smoothed the blanket over the barrel, and stood up. He signaled to the two young men on the handcar. "Unload the iron. Put the seed potatoes in the dry sled box where the frost won't bite them. And give them the wire."

"What wire?" Maya asked, glancing at the platform.

The Augustine brothers lifted two heavy wooden spools from the car deck. Each spool was wound with two hundred yards of heavy, galvanized steel telegraph line, thick as a slate pencil and stiff as spring steel.

"Salvaged from the high towers out on the Sackville marsh," Noel said. "The old shortwave array that fell during the ice storm three winters back. You can’t shoot it, and you can’t eat it, but if you string it three inches below the water across the landing gut, it’ll foul an eight-foot oar faster than a drift net. Consider it interest on the rifles."

"Fair trade," Mac said.

They worked in silence for twenty minutes, transferring the heavy grey iron pigs, the salt fish kegs, and the seed crates from the handcar to the cedar sled, while the two sniper rifles and the Lapua cartridges were lashed down onto the handcar’s center box. The exchange was swift, rhythmic, and wordless—the ancient etiquette of Maritime men who knew that idle talk on an open railway cut was an invitation to a bullet.

When the last lash was knotted, Noel stepped back onto the handcar platform and took the pump handle in his right hand. He looked down at Maya one more time, his dark gaze lingering on the white ash staff and the slight, telltale twitch of her woolen shirt where Scar was nestled.

"Auntie Elizabeth says to tell you the moon of the green grass will be late this year," Noel said. "The frost is five feet in the marsh clay. Keep your seed boxes inside the tower until the wild geese start flying north at night. If you plant before the peepers sing in the swamp, the seed will rot in the ground."

"Tell Auntie Elizabeth I've already got twenty flats of soil thawed in the lantern room," Maya replied. "The glass traps seventy degrees when the sun is up. We'll have green sprouts before the ice leaves the river."

Noel cracked a rare, toothy grin. "You're Mac's girl, all right. Hard-headed as a spruce knot." He looked at Mac. "Watch the south wind. When the wind backs into the southeast and brings the warm rain off the Atlantic, the river will break in twelve hours. When you hear the ice thunder at Fort Folly, get your chains taut."

"They'll be taut," Mac said.

Noel nodded once, signaled his boys, and the pump beam began its steady, rhythmic rise and fall. The iron wheels squealed against the rails, gathering speed as the car rolled south along the marsh embankment, shrinking into the vast, bleached yellow horizon of the Tantramar flats until it was nothing more than a dark speck against the grey sky.

Mac turned back to the sled, slipped the moose-hide chest harness over his shoulders, and leaned his weight forward. The steel-shod runners groaned, broke free of the freezing slush, and began their steady, hissing journey back toward the red cliffs of the light.

Maya matched his stride, four paces back, her ash staff planting firm and sure into the frozen gravel with every third step.

Inside her shirt, Scar poked his dark nose out into the cold slipstream, tasted the smell of salt fish and old iron on the sled, let out a tiny, contented sneeze, and burrowed back down against her warmth.

### III. The Anatomy of the River Boom

The smithy was the warmest room within forty miles, but it smelled of cold iron, charred sugar-maple and birch charcoal, and the thick, sulfurous reek of Cape Breton sea-coal hauled up from the old coal scow sunk off Grindstone Island.

The fire in the stone hearth was a deep, blinding white-orange cherry at the core, held in the heart of the firepot by a ring of soapstone bricks Mac had salvaged from the lining of an old wood-burning range in Memramcook. Overhead, the smoke hood—beaten from galvanized roofing tin and wired with heavy copper stays—swallowed the blue plumes and hurled them out through the stone chimney into the teeth of the north wind.

Mac stood at the eighty-pound Peter Wright anvil, his moose-hide apron tied tight across his broad chest, his forearms black with soot and scale. In his left hand, gripping with heavy round-nosed tongs, he held a length of three-quarter-inch octagonal drill steel salvaged from the Dorchester quarry. The tip of the bar was glowing translucent yellow-white, shedding tiny yellow sparks that hissed as they hit the dirt floor.

In his right hand was his four-pound cross-peen hammer.

*Clang. Tonk-tonk.*

The rhythm was as old as the maritimes: the heavy blow on the hot metal, followed by the light double-bounce of the hammer face on the hardened anvil plate to keep the wrist loose and the arm from seizing.

*Clang. Tonk-tonk. Clang. Tonk-tonk.*

Under the repeated blows, the blunt octagonal bar flattened, thinned, and drew out into a four-sided taper nine inches long. With two swift twists of the tongs, Mac turned the bar forty-five degrees and drove the cross-peen into the corners, throwing up four razor-sharp triangular barbs along the shank—teeth designed so that once they punched through two inches of cedar or oak planking, no human muscle could pull them back out without tearing the timber to splinters.

Maya stood three paces back at the wooden lever of the great double-chamber bellows. 

The bellows was built like a pair of giant accordion lungs from two-inch white pine boards and thick, oil-soaked bull-hide, weighted on the upper board with two paving bricks. Maya’s right hand held the smooth ash operating handle, her left hand planted firmly on her ash staff, using the staff as a third leg to anchor her pelvis and take the downward load off her healing fourth rib. 

With every downward stroke of her right arm, the lower chamber sucked a great lungful of cold air from the floor; on the return swing, the middle flap valve clapped shut, the upper chamber collapsed under the weight of the bricks, and a continuous, unbroken blast of oxygen roared through the cast-iron tuyere pipe straight into the heart of the coals.

*Whoosh-hiss. Whoosh-hiss.*

She didn't rush. She had learned the rhythm two days ago when her ribs still protested every sudden breath. If she leaned forward from the waist, her left side pinched like a wasp sting; but if she kept her spine vertical, locked her hips, and used the weight of her upper torso to drive the lever down, the effort flowed through her lats and shoulder blade without pulling across the fracture. The warmth from the hearth washed over her face, baking out the deep, lingering chill of the rail siding, bringing a prickle of sweat to her hairline under her wool cap.

Beneath her mackinaw, tucked into the fleece lining between her woolen sweater and her breast, Scar was dead to the world. The steady, hypnotic *clang-tonk-whoosh* of the forge seemed to act on him like an opiate; the squirrel only stirred to shift his tiny chin from one fold of flannel to the next, entirely undisturbed by the shower of sparks twenty feet away.

"Ease the air," Mac said without looking up, his tongs already thrusting the finished spike into the cooling tub.

The hot steel struck the brine with a violent, spitting scream, sending up a thick mushroom of white steam that smelled of rock salt and tempered carbon.

Maya checked her stroke, letting the bellows settle on its leather stops. She rested her forehead against the cool ash handle for three seconds, breathing through her nose, feeling the deep, solid thump of her heart. 

"That's forty-two," she said, her voice slightly husky from the coal smoke. "Forty-two spikes. At eighteen-inch centers, that covers sixty-three feet of stringer."

"We need fifty," Mac said, reaching into the coal bin with an iron shovel to bank the rear of the firepot with fresh wet slack. "The narrow gut between the red shale spit and the low-water ledge is eighty feet across on the neap, but when the spring flood piles thirty feet of red mud into the channel, the water cuts deep into the outer eddy. Vance's steersmen won't try the shallows—they don't have the draft for forty feet of oar. They'll hug the deep gut along the shale bluff where the current runs fastest. That's where the boom has to bite."

He walked over to the open double doors of the skidway at the back of the smithy.

Outside in the grey March afternoon, resting on heavy cedar cribbing five feet off the frozen mud, lay the two sixty-foot red spruce stringers they had hauled out of the high ridge timber during the bitter freeze of February. 

They were massive logs—two feet thick at the butt, tapering to fourteen inches at the crown, straight as gun barrels and peeled completely clean of bark to prevent the salt water from softening the cambium. Mac had bored spiral holes three inches deep every eighteen inches along the top third of each log using a two-inch hand auger, angling each bore forty-five degrees forward against the direction of the flood tide.

Maya picked up her staff, stepped through the smithy doors into the crisp, sub-zero air, and walked along the length of the first stringer. The shock of the cold after the furnace heat felt clean and sharp in her lungs.

"The spikes go in the bores," she said, tapping one of the auger holes with the iron ferrule of her staff. "Then we pour rendered spruce pitch around the shanks, bolt the forged collar straps over the top, and chain the two stringers together with three-foot link."

"Anchor link," Mac confirmed, pointing to a heap of massive, black-rusted iron chain coiled like a dormant python on the cribbing. "Seven-eighths inch forged wrought iron. We salvaged seventy feet of it from the old gypsum loading pier down at Hillsborough. It held four-masted barques against the bore for sixty years; it won't snap when forty tons of wooden whaleboat hit it at six knots."

"And the shore moorings?" Maya asked, looking across the frozen mud shelf to the towering, blood-red sandstone cliffs that hemmed in the river bend.

"Deadman anchors," Mac said. "I blasted two four-foot shafts into the sandstone face with black powder before the freeze. We drop two six-foot lengths of eighty-pound railway iron down those shafts, pack them with quartz boulders and wet hydraulic cement, and wrap the chain around the rail web with three turnbuckles. Once those pins drop into the rock, the cliff will have to give way before the boom moves an inch."

Maya leaned against the red spruce log, running her gloved fingers over the clean, yellow wood. It was hard as bone from the winter frost.

"When Vance hits this," she murmured, "his lead boat is going to accordion like a tin can."

"If he hits it in the dark," Mac said, "the first boat will ramp right over the spruce stringer. The stem will ride up on the log, the spikes will punch straight through the bottom boards between the second and third thwarts, and the momentum will tear the bottom out from stem to sternpost. The second boat will hit the wreckage before the steersman can throw his sweep over. In four knots of tide, with thirty feet of red water pushing behind them, forty men in heavy winter coats don't have three minutes before the bore rolls them under the ice shelf."

He didn't say it with malice or triumph; he stated it with the cold, absolute certainty of an engineer calculating the shearing load on a bridge truss. On the Petitcodiac, the river was not an ally or an enemy—it was a hydraulic engine that weighed sixty-two pounds per cubic foot, moving eighteen million tons of saltwater and mud every twelve hours, indifferent to the uniforms, the flags, or the politics of the men who tried to cross it.

"Let's finish the last eight spikes," Maya said, turning back toward the warmth of the forge doors. "I want the whole boom chained and wedged on the skids before the south wind hits the bay."

Mac looked at her, his dark eyes taking in the steady set of her shoulders and the iron grip she kept on the ash staff. "Your side holding?"

"My side is fine," Maya replied, her chin lifting. "It only hurts when I think about Amherst."

### IV. The Radar in the Cleavage and the Ash Staff

By midday, the smoke from the smithy chimney had died to a thin, grey thread of birch haze.

Inside the stone kitchen of the keeper's house, the heat was gentle, radiant, and deep. The great cast-iron cookstove—a massive Enterprise Monarch with six boiling lids, a high warming shelf, and an oval copper reservoir on the right side—hummed with a low, comfortable draft. On its front lid, a heavy cast-iron pot simmered with salted gaspereau, dried yellow split peas, and peeled winter onions, filling the room with a thick, savory steam that made Maya’s empty stomach twist with an impatient ache.

She sat at the long scrubbed-pine table, her heavy mackinaw unbuttoned, her wool cap tossed onto the bench beside her. 

Her ash staff stood propped within arm's reach against the stone chimney breast, its oiled grain reflecting the orange glow of the hearth fire. On the table before her lay a whetstone, a shallow tin saucer of light spindle oil, and three carbon-steel butchering knives with birch-slab handles that Mac had forged from old crosscut saw blades.

Maya reached inside her woolen overshirt with two fingers.

"All right, you lazy red parasite," she muttered under her breath. "Rent is due. Out you come."

Scar did not want to come out. 

When her fingers touched his warm, furry flank, he let out a tiny, indignant *whuff* and burrowed his head deeper under the curve of her collarbone. His tiny paws, equipped with curved needle claws, hooked tenaciously into the ribbed weave of her grey wool sweater, right over the valley between her breasts.

Maya sighed, a wry, self-deprecating smirk tugging at her mouth. 

*Calcium cannons,* she thought, shaking her head at the sheer ridiculousness of her life. That was what she had called them back when she was sixteen and running wild through the abandoned high school corridors in Moncton, back before the grid died, before the salt flats became a battlefield, before she had to worry about sniper optics and winter rationing. Back then, breasts were just an annoying biological development that made running hurdles miserable and required three sports bras to keep under control. 

Now? Now they were a ninety-eight-point-six-degree incubator for a three-pound tactical early-warning radar. 

The squirrel didn't care about aesthetic proportions or feminine modesty; he cared about thermal mass. Tucked into her flannel cleavage, nestled in the dark space between her woolen brassiere and her rib cage, he had the best seat in the Acadian forest. Her body heat kept him from freezing into an icicle during four-hour watches on the gallery catwalk, and his acute, twitching ears—capable of picking up the scrape of a steel crampon on wet sandstone or the metallic click of a safety catch through three hundred yards of spruce thicket—kept her head attached to her shoulders.

It was an arrangement. A totally absurd, undignified, highly effective post-apocalyptic defense treaty.

"Come on," she whispered, gently prying his little claws out of the wool one by one. "I need to check your ears, and you need to let my ribs breathe."

Scar allowed himself to be lifted out into the daylight. He sat on the scrubbed pine table, blinking his large, liquid-black eyes in the yellow lamp glow, his bushy red tail arched neatly over his back like a question mark. He shook himself vigorously, his fur puffing out until he looked like an oversized chestnut burr, then hopped twice across the table, picked up a dried pumpkin seed from a wooden bowl, and sat on his haunches, nibbling furiously with his yellow front teeth.

Maya watched him for a second, then reached down and pulled her sweater up past her rib cage.

She examined the purple-and-yellow bruise that still covered her left flank. The swelling had subsided completely, leaving behind a hard, thickened ridge of bone callus along the fourth rib where the fracture had knit together. When she pressed her thumb into the intercostal space, the sharp, sickening agony was gone, replaced by a dull, tight ache that felt like an over-tightened leather cinch.

"Bones are tougher than steel," a quiet voice said from the doorway.

Mac stood there, carrying an armload of split sugar maple logs for the woodbox. He dumped the wood with a hollow clatter, brushed the dry bark from his sleeves, and walked to the copper reservoir on the stove. He took a heavy stoneware mug, filled it with hot water from the tap, dropped in a handful of crushed dried rosehips and spruce tips from a hanging linen sack, and set it on the table in front of Maya.

"Drink that," Mac said. "Auntie Elizabeth sent the rosehips. She says the vitamin keeps the scurvy out of the gums when the salt fish gets old."

Maya took the mug, wrapping her cold hands around the hot stoneware. The steam smelled pungent, citrusy, and resinous. She took a slow sip, feeling the heat spread down her throat and settle into her chest.

"I need to work the staff," Maya said, looking over at the white ash pole leaning against the stones. "My legs are getting soft, Mac. If Vance brings fifty men up that bluff, I can't just stand behind the parapet and throw rocks."

Mac pulled out the heavy bench across from her and sat down. His face was unreadable in the firelight—the dark, weathered mask of a man who had seen three generations of war and winter pass across the red river without leaving anything behind but bones and rusted iron.

"You can't fight fifty men with an ash staff, Maya," Mac said, his voice quiet, devoid of condescension. "You can't fight one man with an ash staff if he has a nine-millimeter pistol and ten feet of daylight between you."

"I know that," Maya said, her eyes flashing. "I'm not an idiot. I don't think I'm some movie hero who can bat bullets out of the air like baseballs. Bullets move at two thousand feet per second; wood moves as fast as my shoulder can pull it. If a man has a rifle pointed at my center mass and his finger is on the trigger, I'm dead before the sound hits the wall."

"Then what is the staff for?" Mac asked.

Maya set the mug down, picked up her white ash staff, and stood up in the center of the kitchen floor. 

She stood balanced, her feet shoulder-width apart, her knees slightly bent, her weight distributed sixty-forty toward her back right heel. She held the staff at the low-ready—both hands spaced eighteen inches apart along the center taper, the iron ferrule pointing diagonally toward the floorboards, covering her lower ribs and groin.

"It’s a third leg on the ice," Maya said, her voice dropping into the cool, analytical cadence she used when thinking through a problem. "It’s a vaulting pole across the mud gutters where the clay would swallow my boots. It’s a lever to pry sixty-foot spruce logs off the cribbing. And when the visibility drops to ten yards in a tidal fog, or when a man crawls through the dark into the narrow stone passage between the tower and the oil shed..."

She stepped forward with her left foot. The staff whipped through a clean, horizontal arc—not a wild, looping swing that exposed her ribs, but a tight, piston-like thrust from the hip. The iron-shod ferrule stopped with a sharp *crack* exactly two inches from the wooden frame of the pantry door, right at the height of a man's solar plexus.

"...it’s forty ounces of cured white ash moving at sixty miles an hour," Maya finished, recovering her stance instantly, the butt of the staff tucking under her right elbow to guard her flank. "It doesn't run out of cartridges. It doesn't jam when the mud gets into the receiver. And it doesn't make a sound that can be heard three miles down the bay."

Mac watched the movement, his eyes tracking the path of her hips and the angle of her left foot.

"Your left heel was off the deck," he said calmly.

Maya paused, looking down at her boots.

"If your heel is up," Mac explained, standing up and moving behind her, "your pelvis can't lock into the sandstone. When you thrust with the ash, the reaction force pushes you backward onto your wounded side. If the man is wearing a heavy caribou coat or an iron breastplate, your ferrule won't penetrate—it'll bounce, and the shock will go straight into your fourth rib."

He reached out, his heavy, calloused hand pressing firmly against her lower back, adjusting the tilt of her pelvis by two inches. 

"Drop the heel," Mac commanded softly. "Bend the right knee another inch. Don't push the staff with your arms—drive it with the heel of your right boot through the hip socket. The earth gives you the power; the wood is just the iron nail that delivers it."

Maya adjusted her stance. She felt the difference immediately: the strain vanished from her healing ribs, transferring down through the heavy muscles of her glutes and thighs straight into the solid white cedar and spruce floorboards beneath her feet.

"Now," Mac said, stepping back. "Again."

She drove the thrust forward. 

This time, there was no wobble, no wincing catch in her breath. The iron ferrule struck the pantry frame with a dull, concussive *thud* that shook the jars of preserved blueberries on the upper shelf. The shock traveled cleanly back through the ash, into her palms, down her spine, and dissipated into the floor.

On the table, Scar didn't even look up from his pumpkin seed. He was used to the noise; as long as the vibrations didn't carry the high-frequency click of a gun mechanism or the scrape of steel crampons on stone, the world was entirely within acceptable parameters.

Maya exhaled slowly, lowering the staff to her side. A bead of sweat trickled down the side of her neck, but her breathing was deep, rhythmic, and clear.

"Better," Mac said, walking back to the woodstove and lifting the lid on the fish pot. "Much better. Eat your gaspereau, Maya. When the sun hits the top of the tower, we have thirty flats of seed to sow in the lantern room."

### V. The Seed Trays of the High Lantern

The spiral iron staircase that wound up the spine of the lighthouse was sixty-two steps of cold, pierced cast-iron tread. 

Each riser rang with a clear, bell-like ping under Mac’s heavy boots as he ascended, carrying two fifty-pound cedar seed flats balanced across his shoulders like a milkmaid’s yoke. Behind him came Maya, carrying a single twenty-pound flat of screened compost tucked firmly against her right hip, her left hand keeping a steady, unbroken cadence with her white ash staff on the outer curve of the iron rail.

Inside her mackinaw, Scar was awake, but he was pinned flat by the steady rhythm of their climb. His small front paws rested against the top buttonhole of her shirt, his whiskers twitching as each turn of the stair brought a change in the air—from the damp, cold cellar-smell of the stone foundation, through the dry cedar aroma of the middle living tier, and finally into the warm, greenhouse brightness of the upper lantern room.

When they stepped out onto the iron deck of the watch gallery, the sudden change in temperature was like stepping through a doorway into another season.

The sun had climbed past the eastern shoulder of the ridge, pouring three hundred square feet of unfiltered, radiant heat through the circular sweep of the glass cupola. While outside the wind was still gusting twenty knots and the snow on the north-facing ledges lay two feet deep, inside the lantern room the air was a drowsy, humid seventy-two degrees. It smelled of warm copper, heated linseed oil, damp earth, and the sweet, mineral tang of curing silt.

"Look at that," Maya breathed, setting her seed flat down on the wide ash staging bench Mac had built around the perimeter of the central pedestal.

The bench was three feet wide, bolted directly to the copper-clad mullions, following the curve of the glass for eighteen feet along the southern and western arcs where the sun lingered longest. On the bench, arranged in neat, orderly ranks, sat twenty-four shallow boxes split from rot-resistant white cedar.

Mac set his two flats down with a solid grunt, wiping his brow with the back of his moose-hide glove. 

"The lightkeepers used to grow geraniums up here in the old days," Mac said, looking around the bright, sun-drenched cupola. "In seventy-six, when the keeper had four daughters, they had fifty pots of red and white flowers blooming in April while the drift ice was still grinding on the rocks below. The glass traps the long-wave heat. As long as the wind doesn't blow out a pane, it won't drop below fifty degrees in here, even if the frost hits zero outside."

Maya walked to the potting tub—a halved fifty-five-gallon steel drum scrubbed clean of fuel oil and mounted on two wooden cradles. 

Inside the tub was the seed mixture they had spent three days preparing: rich, black tidal silt hauled up from the high-water line where the autumn marsh grass had rotted down into dark humus, mixed with two parts screened sugar-maple wood ash to neutralize the acid, three parts rotted eelgrass compost from the drift banks, and two shovels of coarse quartz sand from the brook bed to keep the clay from packing tight as a brick when watered.

She plunged her bare hands into the mixture. It was cool, but it felt alive—crumbly, rich, and fragrant with the deep, fermenting life of the maritime earth.

"Good soil," Maya said, her fingers working through the dark loam, breaking up the small clods until the texture was as fine as cornmeal. "Auntie Elizabeth knew what she was doing with that compost. There's no weed seed in this; the winter frost cooked it through."

She looked over at the cloth sacks sitting beside the water keg.

There were four varieties, each labeled in faded lead pencil on scraps of brown paper: *Fort Folly Flint Corn*, *Kennebec Seed Eyes*, *Jacob's Cattle Bush Beans*, and *Yellow Crookneck Squash*.

"How many hills of corn can we carry down to the shelf in May?" Maya asked, taking a handful of the flint corn from the sack. 

The kernels were not the soft, sweet yellow dent corn of the modern grain elevators; they were hard, glossy, deep-amber pebbles, shaped like rounded teardrops, dense as flint and heavy in the hand. They were an old northern variety, bred by ten generations of Mi'kmaq to sprout in the cold, wet soils of the Maritimes, capable of maturing in ninety short days between the late June frosts and the early September storms.

"Four hundred hills," Mac said, reaching into the tub with a wooden trowel to fill the cedar flats. "We clear the lower terrace below the sheep fold—the three acres where the old orchard died out. The red sandstone holds the heat from the afternoon sun, and the salt fog keeps the late frosts from burning the silk. If we get eighty bushels of ears out of that patch, we have enough meal to carry four people and two dozen laying hens through eighteen months of blockade."

Maya began setting the corn kernels into the cedar flat, pressing each seed one inch deep into the moist, dark loam with the tip of her forefinger, spacing them two inches apart in neat, parallel rows. 

Beside her, Scar finally decided that the warmth of the lantern room was too tempting to ignore. He slipped out of her mackinaw, scrambled up her woolen sleeve with a frantic scrabble of tiny claws, and perched on her left shoulder, his bushy tail curled flat against her back. He sniffed the damp earth with intense curiosity, his nose twitching at the smell of rotting eelgrass, his little black hands reaching out to pat the edge of the cedar flat before he decided that dirt was uninteresting and curled up on the sunny copper windowsill to bake his belly in the light.

Maya smiled faintly, not looking away from her work. 

"You see that, Scar?" she murmured, pressing another amber seed into the soil. "That’s life. Real life. Not Vance's militia marching in circles on the Amherst asphalt, waiting for someone to drop a can of beans off a truck. This grows out of the rock. You can't shoot a cornfield with a Timberwolf rifle; you can't starve a homestead that knows how to turn salt mud into bread."

She looked up, gazing out through the great curved panes of the lantern room.

From sixty feet above the headland, the geography of the Maritime border was laid out before her like a map drawn in red ochre, white snow, and grey salt water. 

To the south, the Cumberland Basin stretched away into the grey distance, its frozen surface scarred by pressure ridges and black, jagged leads where the tides had cracked the winter plate. Beyond the ice, barely visible against the horizon, lay the low, dark timberline of the Nova Scotia shore—the hills of Joggins, the mouth of the Maccan River, and the distant, smoking chimneys of Amherst twelve miles across the frozen marsh. 

To the west, the Petitcodiac coiled away into the heart of New Brunswick, a vast serpentine trough of blood-red mud and jagged floes, twisting between the towering sandstone bluffs toward the inland bends. 

It was vast, wild, and utterly unforgiving. A human being caught out on those mudflats when the forty-eight-foot tide came rolling in had about as much chance of survival as a moth tossed into a blacksmith’s hearth.

"Mac," Maya said softly, her fingers pausing over the soil.

"Aye?"

"Vance doesn't understand this river, does he?"

Mac leveled the soil in the second flat with a notched pine lath, his broad face shadowed against the glare of the glass. 

"He understands the highway," Mac said. "He understands the four-lane asphalt of Route Two, the rail yards at Sackville, and the concrete barracks of the armory. Men like Vance think land is something you draw a red line across on an ordnance survey map. They think if you have eighty men with rifles and three machine guns, you own the ground you stand on."

He set the lath down, looked out across the bay toward the southern horizon, and spat into the iron gutter beneath the sill.

"They don't know the red mud," Mac continued, his voice dropping into that deep, rhythmic cadence that sounded like stones rolling in the surf. "They don't know that the mud is sixty feet deep in the gutters, that it can swallow a six-ton diesel truck in twenty minutes and leave nothing behind but an oily bubble. They don't know that the bore doesn't care about rank, or orders, or who has the most ammunition. In two weeks, the river will remind them."

Maya set the last kernel of corn into the flat, smoothed the dark soil over the hole, and patted it firm with her palm. 

"Let it remind them," she said.

### VI. The Kinap's Ridge Patrol and the Deadfall Traps

The afternoon sun was sinking behind the western ridge when Mac took his snowshoes from the wall of the oil shed.

They were forty-eight-inch modified bear-paws, framed from straight-grained white ash bent over hot steam and laced with heavy, unvarnished moose-hide babiche. They were wider than the common snowshoes used by the valley farmers—wide enough to float two hundred and twenty pounds of bone, muscle, and steel over three feet of soft, granular spring sugar-snow without breaking the crust.

He didn't carry a rifle. Slung across his chest in a rawhide baldric was his heavy four-pound broadaxe, its carbon-steel bit honed to a shaving edge and protected by a greased leather sheath. In his right hand was his heavy ash tracking pole, eight feet long, tipped with a four-inch iron spear point forged from a quarry chisel.

Maya stood on the stone stoop of the kitchen, her ash staff planted in the packed snow beside her, watching him lace the moose-hide thongs over his heavy winter moccasins.

"How far north are you going?" she asked.

"To the high red cut above the narrow gut," Mac replied, not looking up as his fingers knotted the wet babiche with quick, greasy hitches. "The thaw is starting along the southern ledges. The snow is pulling back from the rock face, and the goat path between the upper cliff and the mud shelf is thawing out. If Vance sends another three-man reconnaissance patrol on foot before the boats move, that's the only path they can use without snowshoes."

"Set the birch triggers?" Maya asked.

"The birch triggers," Mac confirmed. "Two deadfalls on the switchback above the shale slide. If a man steps on the false treadle, three tons of red sandstone boulders and frozen spruce logs drop sixty feet straight down the chimney. It won't leave enough of him to make a meal for a raven."

He stood up, testing the tension of the snowshoe harnesses with two sharp stomps that sent the dry powder flying from the crust. He looked at Maya, his gaze lingering on her left side.

"Keep the door barred," he said. "The Clacks is locked on the safety pin. If Auntie Elizabeth blinks from the point, don't answer unless she gives three long pulses. Scar will hear me when I hit the lower brook. Don't come out on the stoop with the lantern."

"I'm not five years old, Mac," Maya said with a faint, wry smirk. "I have two loaded twelve-gauges behind the door, three butchering knives on the table, and sixty-two steps of iron stairs between the kitchen and the gallery. If anyone comes through that timber, they'll find out why the people down in Dorchester call this place the Fortress of the Bends."

Mac gave a single, slow nod—the highest mark of confidence he ever bestowed—turned his back on the homestead, and stepped out onto the deep drifts of the north pasture.

He did not walk. 

To an observer unfamiliar with the deep lore of the Acadian forest, the movement would have looked impossible—a trick of the fading winter light or an illusion born of the vast, white silence of the timber.

Mac did not trudge through the snow, dragging his webbed shoes through the drift like a common woodsman. When his moccasins struck the crust, his knees bent slightly, his spine straightened, and his mass seemed to settle into the earth, drawing up an immense, hydraulic momentum that bypassed the common mechanics of human stride. 

He moved in massive, low-trajectory leaping bounds.

With each spring of his thighs, he launched forward thirty, thirty-five, forty feet across the deep powder, his ash bear-paws barely skimming the frozen crust, leaving behind nothing more than a faint, diamond-shaped kiss in the snow before he launched again. He did not grow larger; his frame did not swell into the monstrous, distorted bulk of a storybook giant. He remained Mac—six feet of lean, hardened Mi'kmaw timber-faller—but the covenant of the *Kinap*, the ancient bone-deep resonance between his flesh and the red bedrock of the river, allowed his legs to unleash a frightening, explosive force that treated gravity and distance like mere suggestions.

*Crunch.* A forty-foot leap over a collapsed cedar brush pile. 

*Crunch.* A thirty-foot bound that cleared a frozen meltwater gully without breaking stride.

*Crunch.* A soaring, flat-trajectory arc that carried him from the base of a sugar maple straight to the high shale shelf forty paces up the ridge, landing with the light, elastic absorption of a panther.

In less than twenty seconds, he had closed a half-mile of tangled, second-growth spruce and broken sandstone, climbing four hundred feet above the river without a single ragged breath entering his lungs.

At the crest of the red cut, where the cliff fell sheer three hundred feet to the frozen mud of the Petitcodiac, Mac dropped to one knee behind a thick screen of scrub birch. 

The air up here was colder, smelling of the high atmosphere and the vast, empty forests that stretched north toward the Miramichi. Below him, the river lay coiled in its red canyon, the ice looking like a shattered mosaic of white bone embedded in dried blood.

He unslung his broadaxe and went to work on the deadfall.

The trap was already half-prepared—an ancient Acadian woodsman’s engine that relied on gravity, counterweights, and the utter treachery of the soft spring shale. 

High on the cliff wall, perched on a narrow ledge sixty feet above the narrow goat track that wound along the cliff base, sat a mass of red sandstone boulders, each the size of a flour barrel, held in place by three massive spruce retaining logs. The logs were pegged into the rock face with seasoned oak pins, held back by a heavy hemp trigger cable that ran down through a concealed crevice to a false step on the trail below.

Mac inspected the trigger.

The winter frost had heaved the anchor rock slightly, throwing the balance off by half an inch. With swift, silent strokes of his axe, he trimmed a fresh wedge from a dry yellow birch limb, drove it beneath the primary retaining lever with the flat poll of the axe, and adjusted the trip-wire tension.

The trigger was set so finely now that forty pounds of pressure—the weight of a man's boot stepping onto the loose shale slab—would kick the wedge out. 

Once the wedge slipped, the three spruce logs would pivot on their iron hinges, releasing eight tons of shattered sandstone and frozen clay straight down the narrow rock chimney. Anything caught on the path below would be swept into the river bed, pulverized into fragments, and buried under four feet of rubble before the sound of the crash reached the opposite bank.

Mac stood back, inspecting the camouflage. He scattered three handfuls of dry snow and dried birch leaves over the fresh axe marks, wiped his steel with his oily sleeve, and turned his gaze south toward the mouth of the bay.

The wind was shifting.

It was faint at first—barely a breath—but to a man who had spent fifty years reading the tides, the change was as distinct as a gunshot. 

The cold, dry north wind that had held the bay in iron for three months was dying. Out over the Atlantic, beyond the outer mouth of the Bay of Fundy, a massive low-pressure engine was turning, drawing warm, moist air off the Gulf Stream and hurling it north across the Nova Scotia peninsula. 

The air on his cheek was suddenly soft, greasy, and smelled of warm rain, melting ocean pack, and rotting seaweed.

"The southwester," Mac murmured to the silent trees. "Twelve hours. Noel was right."

He did not linger. He stepped to the lip of the shale terrace, bent his knees, and launched himself out into the empty air above the snow-filled ravine.

Four forty-foot bounds took him down the steep face of the ridge. Six more carried him across the frozen brook. In ninety seconds of silent, terrifying locomotion, he was back at the perimeter fence of the lighthouse, his snowshoes coming to rest against the stone wall with a soft, final hiss of powder.

### VII. The Thunder of the Red Breakup

The thaw did not arrive as a gentle spring morning; it hit the Petitcodiac like a warm, wet fist.

By midnight, the temperature had climbed from eighteen degrees below zero to forty-two degrees above. A heavy, drenching rain—the thick, greasy Atlantic downpour the old Acadians called *la grosse pluie du sud*—swept across the Cumberland Basin in blinding sheets, turning the three-foot snowpack on the ridges into a sodden, grey sponge that poured millions of gallons of boiling runoff down every brook, gully, and ravine.

Inside the lighthouse kitchen, the sound of the rain was a deafening roar against the thick slate shingles.

Maya sat by the stove, fully dressed in her heavy wool trousers, mackinaw, and oilskin cape. Her ash staff stood between her knees, her hands resting on the polished wood. 

Beneath her coat, Scar was awake, but he was completely quiet, his small body pressed tightly against her sternum. The squirrel did not like the smell of the south wind; the sudden drop in barometric pressure had set his ears ringing, and he kept his nose tucked deep under her collar, only shifting when a sudden gust of wind rattled the iron flue pipe above the stove.

Mac stood by the seaward window, peering through the rain-streaked glass with a brass night glass.

"Slack water," Mac said. His voice was tense—the only time Maya had ever heard a thread of genuine alertness in his tone. "The low tide has drained the basin. The ice is hanging on the mud shelves by its own weight. In twenty minutes, the equinox flood hits the mouth of the bay."

"How high?" Maya asked, standing up, her staff clicking against the stones.

"Forty-eight feet," Mac said. "A spring perigee tide pushed by a fifty-knot southeasterly gale. It’ll force the entire Atlantic Ocean into the funnel between Cape Chignecto and Cape Enrage. When that wall of water hits the frozen river, the ice won't just melt—it'll explode."

A low, deep vibration began to hum through the floorboards beneath their boots.

It was not a sound that could be heard through the ears; it was a subsonic tremor that traveled through sixty feet of solid red sandstone bedrock, vibrating the iron stove plates, rattling the tin mugs on the dresser, and setting the oil in the lamps dancing in concentric rings.

Scar let out a sudden, sharp *chirr* and clawed two inches upward, his whiskers trembling violently against Maya’s neck.

"He feels it," Maya whispered, her hand instinctively pressing over the squirrel’s back. "What is that?"

"The bore," Mac said, lowering the glass. "Come to the parapet. You need to see this once in your life. After tonight, the river belongs to the boats."

They climbed the spiral stairs to the watch gallery, not stopping at the lantern room, but stepping through the heavy iron storm door out onto the open stone parapet beneath the light.

The gale hit them like a solid wall of warm salt spray. The rain drove horizontally out of the black south, stinging their eyes, but the darkness was not complete. The low clouds were illuminated from below by a pale, eerie phosphorescence—the white foam of millions of tons of churned saltwater smashing into the mud banks.

Below the sixty-foot cliff, the Petitcodiac was dying and being reborn in violence.

Down in the black throat of the channel, three miles to the south, a white line stretched from the red mud of the eastern shore to the red mud of the west—a foaming, tumbling wall of chocolate-brown water eight feet high, roaring up the estuary at fifteen knots with the sound of a hundred freight trains derailing simultaneously into a stone quarry.

It struck the winter ice pack at the lower bend.

*BOOM.*

The sound was concussive—a massive, subterranean detonation that slapped against Maya’s chest, making her breath catch in her throat. 

Ten-foot-thick plates of blue winter ice, solid as granite, measuring three hundred feet across, were picked up by the hydraulic surge like playing cards. The immense upward pressure of forty-eight feet of tide lifted the frozen floor of the river, snapping it along the center channel with a series of machine-gun cracks that echoed off the Hopewell cliffs ten miles away.

*CRACK. CRACK-CRACK-BOOM.*

The ice plates rose into the air, tilted forty-five degrees against the howling wind, and smashed into one another with catastrophic force. Slabs weighing fifty tons climbed up the backs of other slabs, grinding, splintering, and piling into thirty-foot pressure ridges that shrieked with the sound of tearing metal. 

The red mud beneath them, liquefied by the sudden influx of ocean water, boiled up through the fissures like molten copper, staining the white ice sheets a dark, violent crimson. 

In less than ten minutes, the frozen highway that had connected New Brunswick to Nova Scotia for three long months was obliterated. In its place was a churning, terrifying maelstrom of jagged ice floes, floating timber, and raging red current, racing inland toward the Moncton bends with an unstoppable, murderous momentum.

Maya stood at the iron railing, her hands clamped to her ash staff, the warm rain plastering her dark hair to her temples. Her heart was hammering against her ribs, but her eyes were wide, shining with an awe that bordered on fierce exhilaration.

"Sweet mother of spruce," she breathed into the gale. "Nothing can cross that. Nothing human could survive two seconds in that water."

"Not tonight," Mac shouted above the thunder of the grinding floes. "Not while the ice is running. But in three days, the drift will clear. The floes will be swept out into the bay on the ebb, and the channel will be open water."

He pointed with his heavy arm down toward the narrow gut at the base of their bluff, where the sixty-foot red spruce stringers of their boom lay waiting on the skids, ready to be dropped into the current.

"When the water clears, Vance will look at the river from the Amherst heights," Mac said, his voice cutting through the roar of the tide. "He’ll see open water. He’ll think the river is his road."

Maya leaned on her staff, feeling the solid, unyielding iron beneath her boots, feeling the tiny, terrified heartbeat of Scar thrumming against her breast, feeling the knitted bone of her ribs holding firm against the wind.

A cold, fierce smile spread across her face in the dark.

"Let him come," Maya said. "We have the spikes. We have the wire. And we have the red river."
