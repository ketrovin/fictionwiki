# Chapter 3: The Big Chimney and the Balloon

---

### Part I: The Thermodynamics of the Granite Flue

A decommissioned stone lighthouse on an exposed maritime cliff is not a romantic sanctuary; it is a four-story vertical vacuum cleaner designed by physics to freeze a man solid in his sleep.

On the morning of Day Eight, with the summer sun climbing hot, heavy, and brass-yellow over the mudflats of Shepody Bay, I loaded my two willow pack baskets with my essential carpentry kit, two heavy Mackinaw wool blankets, a tub of rendered tallow, a spool of braided bank line, and made the steep climb up the scarp line to the Old Stone Light.

The tower had been built in 1874 by the Department of Marine and Fisheries to guide timber schooners and gypsum barques through the violent, shifting shoals of the Petitcodiac estuary. 

It was constructed from four-foot-thick blocks of hammer-dressed gray granite, quarried from the cliffs of Albert County and laid up in hydraulic lime mortar that had cured over a century into an artificial bedrock harder than the stone itself. It stood forty-five feet tall: a tapered, cylindrical monolith anchored directly into the headland, forty feet above the high-water mark, capped with a cast-iron lantern deck and an eight-sided glass cupola that looked out over forty miles of open water.

The Coast Guard had automated the light in the late 1970s, replacing the old kerosene vapor lamp with an electric beacon powered by a submarine cable from the mainland, and then abandoned the station entirely in the 1990s when satellite GPS navigation made visual coastal lights redundant. 

The brass lens had been stripped and hauled away to a maritime museum in Halifax; the paint had weathered away under decades of salt gales; and the glass panes had been left to the nesting herring gulls.

The moment you pulled open the three-inch-thick iron foundation door at the base of the tower, you felt the breath of the stone.

The air inside was eight degrees colder than the forest clearing outside, smelling of cold mineral dust, desiccated sea salt, and a hundred years of coal-soot residue trapped in the masonry pores. Because the tower was forty-five feet tall, completely hollow from threshold to lantern deck, and vented at the roof through eight copper salt louvers, the difference in barometric pressure between the sea-level base and the cliff-top cupola created a permanent, roaring **stack effect**.

It was the exact same aerodynamic physics that makes a hundred-foot factory smokestack draw fresh air through a coal furnace.

If you stood on the ground floor and struck a wooden match, the flame did not rise vertically; it was sucked flat on its side, the sulfur sputtering as the howling draft pulled the heat straight up the central iron spiral staircase and blasted it out the roof vents fifty feet above your head at twelve miles an hour. 

In July, that draft pulled damp, humid ocean air into the cold masonry, causing the interior granite walls to sweat rivers of icy condensation that dripped constantly onto the iron treads. In November, when the arctic air masses swept down from the Gulf of St. Lawrence, that same draft would pull twenty-below air across your floorboards, sucking every calorie of warmth out of your woodstove and turning your bedding into sheets of frost before midnight.

"Montgolfier rules," I muttered aloud, setting my heavy tool basket down on the rusted iron landing. The sound of my voice echoed up the stone cylinder like a voice inside a cathedral. "If you want to live inside a masonry chimney, you have to stop treating it like a house and start treating it like a hot air balloon."

---

During the long, quiet winter of 2024, when I had been working the night shift as a caretaker at the university archives in town, I had spent three months reading early 19th-century French aeronautical treatises and Victorian mechanical engineering journals.

A hot air balloon achieves flight because of a fundamental law of thermal buoyancy: when air is heated, its molecules expand, its density drops relative to the surrounding atmosphere, and it rises into the closed crown of the envelope, creating positive lift. Cold air, being dense, heavy, and sluggish, naturally sinks to the lowest open boundary—the throat.

If you leave the top of a balloon envelope open, your buoyant thermal bubble escapes into the sky in three seconds, the envelope collapses, and you plummet to the earth.

If you baffle the top of the envelope, trap the rising warm air pocket in the middle third of the structure, and place an adjustable valve at the bottom throat to regulate the intake of cold oxygen, the entire vertical cylinder becomes a self-contained, stable, and highly efficient thermal engine.

I spent three full days of methodical, sweat-soaked carpentry working my way up the iron spiral staircase of the Old Stone Light, converting the howling stone flue into an engineered, multi-tier living fortress:

```
[ THE GLASS LANTERN DECK / CLACKS ROOM ] ──▶ The Crown Parachute Damper (Vents summer heat / Seals winter freeze)
                    │
[ THIRD LANDING / OBSERVATION DECK ]      ──▶ 2-inch Pine Trapdoor (Lined with oiled wool felt gasket)
                    │
[ SECOND LANDING / THE LIVING SNUG ]      ──▶ The Thermal Envelope: Insulated timber room *inside* the granite
                    │
[ GROUND FLOOR / FOUNDATION VAULT ]       ──▶ The Throat: Adjustable floor intake louver / Iron drop-door
```

---

#### 1. The Throat (Ground Level Intake)
* At the base of the tower, where an eight-inch cast-iron ventilation port passed through the four-foot granite foundation wall, I framed an adjustable wooden louver box out of split white cedar.
* By sliding a notched rock-maple slat up or down, I could restrict the volume of cold ocean air entering the base of the tower from a raging gale down to a controlled, pencil-thin stream, completely starving the chimney of the air volume it required to maintain its howling vertical updraft.

#### 2. The Stairwell Baffles (Landing Trapdoors)
* At the second and third landings, where the cast-iron spiral stairs broke through the circular timber floorings, I framed heavy, two-inch-thick white pine drop-hatches hung on seasoned ash trunnion pivots.
* Around the entire circumference of each hatch, I tacked a three-inch strip of heavy, felted Mackinaw wool, soaked in neatsfoot oil to repel salt dampness.
* When the hatches were dropped shut, their weight compressed the wool gaskets against the pine frames, slicing the forty-five-foot vertical stone cylinder into three completely isolated, airtight horizontal chambers. 
* The roaring stack effect died instantly. The acoustic pipe-organ groan of the ocean wind in the masonry vanished, replaced by a deep, dead, peaceful silence in which you could hear the faint ticking of a wristwatch twenty feet away.

#### 3. The Living Snug (Second Floor)
* On the second level—ten feet above the damp granite foundation and twenty feet below the exposed glass cupola—I constructed an insulated timber room *within* the stone cylinder.
* I did not allow a single pine plank to touch the cold granite masonry. I framed a circular interior wall of rough-sawn white pine with a **two-inch continuous air gap** between the timber and the stone.
* I packed that two-inch gap with hundreds of pounds of dried sphagnum moss, hand-stripped sheets of paper-birch bark, and aromatic northern white cedar shavings from my workshop.
* When the small cast-iron woodstove on the second landing was fired, the heat rose off the iron plates, struck the flat timber ceiling, and rolled across the snug in a warm, trapped thermal bubble that maintained a steady eighteen degrees Celsius, while on the other side of the cedar paneling, the granite stone remained cold, damp, and completely isolated from the living space.

#### 4. The Crown Parachute Damper (Lantern Deck)
* At the very top of the tower, directly beneath the circular hatch leading into the glass lantern room, I constructed an overhead circular wooden damper, four feet in diameter, suspended from a counter-weighted pulley rope anchored to the iron roof ribs.
* *In the hot days of July:* I pulled the control line, opening the crown damper three inches. The tower acted as a massive, natural, powerless air conditioner, gently drawing cool, sixty-degree air off the shaded forest floor and venting the hot, stagnant ceiling air out through the copper salt louvers at the roof.
* *In the freezing gales of November:* I released the line. The heavy oak disc dropped into its wool-felted seat under gravity, completely sealing the top of the tower and locking the entire rising heat envelope inside the living quarters below.

By six o'clock on the evening of Day Ten, I stood in the center of the finished second-floor snug.

I struck a wooden kitchen match and touched it to the wick of a beeswax candle on the corner of the workbench. 

The flame rose straight, still, and bright: a perfect, unmoving teardrop of warm yellow light in the center of the quiet room. 

The howling chimney was gone. In its place stood a dry, warm, four-story granite citadel that smelled of cedar oil, fresh wood shavings, and bees.

"Chimney conquered," I whispered, blowing out the match. "Now we harvest the salt."

### Part II: The White Crust of the Tantramar Marshes

A man who possesses two hundred pounds of fresh fish and no salt is not a survivor; he is merely a custodian of future maggots.

On the morning of Day Eleven, with the summer neap tides exposing the vast, flat coastal salt marshes of the Tantramar basin, I strapped two large wooden pack baskets to my shoulders, picked up my broad ash scrape-shovel, and walked three miles east along the abandoned Intercolonial railbed toward the mud pans of the bay.

The Fundy salt marshes are an ancient, wild kingdom: thousands of acres of spartina cordgrass, muddy drainage gullies, and shallow, flat clay depressions known locally as *pannes*.

During the massive spring tides of May, the ocean surges over the barrier dikes, flooding the shallow clay depressions with two or three inches of dense sea brine. When the moon moves past its perigee and the tide retreats, the water is trapped in the shallow clay pans, exposed to the blistering July sun and the dry, relentless southwest winds that sweep off the bay. 

Over five days of baking heat, the water evaporates into the sky, leaving the red clay floor of the pannes encrusted in a gleaming, quarter-inch-thick sheet of pure, coarse, crystalline sea salt.

Modern preppers hoard iodized table salt in blue cardboard cylinders with little aluminum pouring spouts, foolishly believing that four three-pound containers will carry a household through an apocalypse.

In the old days, an L'nu family preparing to cure fish and game for an eight-month maritime winter measured salt by the wooden bushel basket. 

You need one pound of coarse, non-iodized sea salt for every four pounds of oily fish if you want the fillets to draw their moisture, cure down to hard leather, and remain free of clostridial mold through seven months of damp freeze and thaw.

I spent four hours on the Tantramar mud pans under the blinding summer sun.

Using the broad, flat, hand-planed edge of the ash shovel, I scraped the white crust off the dried red clay, working with a light, sweeping stroke so the shovel lifted the clean white crystals without digging into the underlying river silt. The salt came up in heavy, coarse, glittering flakes that smelled powerfully of the open Atlantic: clean iodine, sun-dried bladderwrack kelp, and concentrated ocean minerals.

By two o'clock in the afternoon, both pack baskets were loaded to the top: eighty-five pounds of pure, bone-dry, unrefined Fundy sea salt, heavy, crystalline, and dry on my collarbones as I climbed back up the gravel railway embankment.

---

Back at the Creek Camp, I went to work constructing the **Subterranean Cold Smokehouse.**

I did not build a little wooden outhouse on the surface of the ground that would pour thick white smoke into the sky like a military signal flare, advertising my location to every pair of eyes between here and the Trans-Canada highway.

I built the smokehouse inside an ancient **upturned hemlock root ball** eighty yards up the north creek bank.

When a sixty-foot eastern hemlock blows over in a November gale, its shallow, spreading root system rips out of the forest floor in a solid, circular disc of interwoven roots, granite boulders, and heavy blue clay, ten feet across and four inches thick, that stands vertically like a natural earthen wall.

Behind the shelter of that living root ball, I excavated a trench four feet deep and six feet wide into the hillside, framing the inner walls with split green alder logs that would never ignite from low heat, and laying down removable racks of hand-planed northern white cedar.

Thirty feet down the slope, at the base of the creek bank, I dug a small, stone-lined combustion pit into the clay, capped it with a heavy slab of flat granite, and connected the firebox to the bottom of the root-ball chamber with a thirty-foot subterranean flue constructed from three hollow cedar logs buried two feet beneath the forest duff.

The physics of the system were brilliant in their absolute stealth:

```
[ Fire Pit at Base of Creek Bank ] ──▶ [ 30-ft Subterranean Cedar Flue ] ──▶ [ Root-Ball Smoke Chamber ]
    (Green Sugar Maple & Birch)           (Smoke Cools to 24°C Underground)         (Dense 80-ft Canopy Disperses Smudge)
```

1. **True Cold Smoke:** By the time the sweet, aromatic smoke from the green sugar maple chips and birchbark traveled thirty feet through the cold, damp, subterranean cedar pipe, the temperature of the gas dropped from two hundred degrees down to twenty-four degrees Celsius. Cold smoke does not cook the delicate fats in the fish; it gently draws the moisture out of the cellular tissue while depositing natural creosols and phenols that act as powerful chemical preservatives, transforming the meat into hard, translucent sheets of smoked protein that will keep for two years at room temperature.
2. **Total Canopy Smoke Dispersion:** The cold smoke exited the root-ball chamber through an array of small cedar vents and rose directly into the dense, eighty-foot canopy of the mature hemlock grove above. The thick, overlapping evergreen boughs acted as a giant natural filter, breaking the smoke into microscopic, invisible wisps that dissolved completely into the forest air before they ever reached the skyline.

I split twenty fresh winter flounder down the backbone, rubbed the pink fillets with the coarse marsh salt, and hung them on the cedar racks above the cold, sweet smudge.

In forty-eight hours, those fillets would be as tough as oil-tanned leather, rich with maple smoke, and ready to be packed into the earthenware crocks in the north slope root cellar.

### Part III: The Salisbury Rumor and the Brass Spyglass

On the evening of Day Thirteen, with the Fundy tide roaring out toward the open ocean, I sat on the iron gallery railing of the Old Stone Light, watching the sunset bleed copper and crimson across the western river bends.

I had pulled my 25x brass field spyglass to its full three-draw extension, resting the heavy objective barrel against the granite parapet, sweeping the agricultural valley toward Salisbury.

Four miles to the west, where the hemlock ridges gave way to the rolling green dairy pastures of the Petitcodiac valley, my lens caught a sudden, blinding flash of geometric color.

It was not the rusted orange of an abandoned tractor, nor was it the faded blue of a plastic hardware-store tarp.

It was a massive, sprawling, breathtaking expanse of brilliant rainbow colors—crimson, electric blue, canary yellow, and emerald green—spread across two hundred feet of mowed alfalfa grass beside an abandoned red timber barn.

A hot air balloon.

I lowered the brass tube, blinked the salt wind out of my eyes, wiped the ocular lens with the corner of my wool shirt, and brought the glass back to my eye.

The **Sussex Balloon Fiesta** was held thirty miles down the valley every autumn, and commercial sightseeing aerostats flew the Petitcodiac river basin every calm weekend from May until October. 

When the power grid died on Day Zero, some pilot had been caught in the sky above the highway, cut his burner blast, and put the aircraft down in the safest, widest flat pasture he could spot before walking away into the chaos of the road.

A modern looter looking at that field through a car window would see nothing of value. A looter wants canned beans, ammunition, gasoline, and batteries; he would look at two hundred pounds of colorful nylon fabric and an awkward wicker basket and drive right past it.

An L'nu builder and timber craftsman looking at that pasture saw **the greatest logistics and materials score in the province of New Brunswick.**

"Tomorrow morning," I whispered into the brass barrel of the glass. "On the peak of the morning flood tide."

### Part IV: Launching the Canoe on the Fundy Flood

At twenty minutes past five on the morning of Day Fourteen, the great tidal bore came around the southern bend of the Petkotkweyak.

It was a magnificent, terrifying sight: a continuous, three-foot-high wall of foaming, chocolate-brown water, roaring up the dry, empty riverbed at twelve miles an hour, pushed by the immense hydraulic weight of two hundred billion tons of Atlantic Ocean forcing its way into the narrow funnel of the bay. 

Behind the wave, the river channel rose fifteen vertical feet in less than twenty minutes, filling the muddy red canyon from bank to bank with a boiling, swirling flood of water moving upstream against the land.

I slid my hand-built sixteen-foot northern white cedar canoe (*Kwitn*) out from beneath her spruce-bough shelter at the mouth of the freshwater creek.

She was an absolute masterpiece of traditional naval architecture: sixteen feet of steam-bent cedar ribs, hand-planed cedar planking, sewn at the stems with soaked black spruce roots (*wapnaqn*), and sealed along every lap joint with boiled white pine resin and rendered bear grease. She weighed fifty-four pounds, drew less than three inches of water, and floated on the surface as light and silent as a fallen leaf.

I loaded my expedition kit into the center bilge:
* My six-foot white ash bear-spear, lashed along the starboard gunwale with rawhide quick-release loops.
* The 48-pound ash hunting flatbow, unstrung inside a grease-treated deer-hide sleeve, with six bone-tipped field arrows in the clean quiver.
* My crooked knife and an eight-inch mill file on my leather belt.
* Two hundred feet of half-inch braided hemp rope and two heavy, forged-steel timber dogs in a canvas dunnage bag.
* A two-quart tin of cold spring water and six strips of dry, smoked flounder wrapped in paper-birch bark.

I stepped into the stern, settled my knees against the cedar floorboards, set the narrow, hand-carved ash paddle into the brown current, and shoved off from the clay bank.

---

Paddling a canoe on a Fundy flood tide is not an exercise in muscle; it is an exercise in **riding an aquatic freight train.**

The incoming tide was rushing upriver at a solid eight knots. I did not have to dig deep with the ash paddle; I merely used subtle, silent J-strokes and steering rudders to keep the cedar bow centered in the deep-water current, letting the brown flood sweep the canoe up the valley at twelve miles an hour.

The river was a corridor of absolute, primeval silence.

The high highway bridges of Wheeler Boulevard and the Trans-Canada crossed sixty feet above my head: massive, brooding structures of cracked concrete and rusted steel, choked solid with thousands of motionless, burned-out vehicles. 

From the center of the brown river channel, eighty yards from either shore, I was completely invisible to anything shambling along the guardrails above. Sound does not travel downward against a twenty-knot salt wind; the deep, rushing roar of the tidal current muffled every movement of the paddle.

Twice during the four-mile run, I spotted lone infected wanderers on the high red clay banks: gray, ragged figures stumbling through the cattails, their heads tilted downward, tracking the scent of dead river shad stranded on the high-water line.

Neither of them looked up.

A zombie’s sensory field is locked in a narrow, horizontal tunnel at eye level; its primitive viral brain has no concept of an open river channel moving beneath its feet. The cedar canoe slipped past them like a piece of floating driftwood, brown, silent, and gone before their dead auditory nerves could register the displacement of the current.

In forty-five minutes of effortless navigation, the river narrowed into the freshwater cattle meadows of Salisbury.

I turned the bow across the current, drove the canoe into a deep, muddy drainage ditch beside the alfalfa pasture, tied the painter line to an ancient willow root, and stepped out onto the firm green turf with the ash bear-spear in my left hand.

### Part V: The Golden Envelope in the Alfalfa

The balloon rig was resting in the center of a twenty-acre hayfield, three hundred yards from the silent secondary highway, exactly as I had seen it through the spyglass.

The envelope had deflated with clean, symmetrical precision: a vast, shimmering swath of silicone-coated ripstop nylon, eighty feet wide and a hundred and ten feet long, its bright panels of crimson, sunflower yellow, cobalt blue, and emerald green spread across the alfalfa like the wings of a fallen dragon.

The wicker gondola basket was resting upright on its white ash skid runners forty paces away, completely undamaged.

I circled the perimeter of the pasture sixty yards out in the tall grass, my ash spear held at the low ready, scanning the treeline and the abandoned dairy barn for movement.

The field was clean.

The pilot had performed a textbook emergency landing: the heavy drag-rope was neatly coiled near the load ring; the parachute vent lines were tied off to the frame; and the burner assembly was empty. The pilot had unclipped his heavy propane tanks and walked toward the highway two weeks ago, leaving the entire aeronautical rig intact.

I knelt beside the mouth of the envelope, pulled my crooked knife, and sliced a four-inch test strip from the throat panel.

The fabric was **DuPont high-tenacity ripstop nylon, reinforced with woven Kevlar grid-threads**, coated on both faces with silicone elastomer to make it 100% airtight and impervious to water.

To an average scavenger, it was colorful junk.

To an L'nu craftsman, it was **the ultimate raw material of the modern age**:
* **Tensile Strength:** Rated for fourteen thousand pounds of structural load. You could not tear it with your bare hands if you harnessed it between two draft horses.
* **Weight:** Less than 1.8 ounces per square yard.
* **Hydrostatic Rating:** Ten thousand millimeters of water column. Completely, permanently waterproof.

"Eighty thousand square feet of indestructible sailcloth," I whispered, running the smooth, silky fabric through my fingers. "That is four canoe sails, six storm tarps, four hundred square yards of ceiling insulation for the stone tower, and enough high-tensile cordage to rig a merchant ship."

---

The harvesting took five hours of intense, calculated labor.

I did not try to haul the entire eight-hundred-pound envelope in one awkward mass. Using the razor-sharp crooked knife, I carefully disassembled the rig into functional, transportable components:

1. **The Parachute Crown Panel:** Fifty square yards of heavy, heat-resistant Nomex cloth and four hundred feet of braided Spectra pull-line (the ultimate material for the lighthouse ceiling dampers).
2. **The Main Envelope Sections:** I sliced three massive, sixty-foot panels of the primary ripstop nylon, folding them with military precision into tight, three-foot bundles that I compressed and lashed with bank line.
3. **The Aircraft Rigging:** Using the file and pliers from my tool kit, I unbolted the eight main stainless steel load cables—each fifty feet long, 1/4-inch braided 7x19 aircraft wire, rated for seven thousand pounds of tension—and coiled them into clean, rust-proof loops.
4. **The Wicker Gondola (The Basket):**
   * Hand-woven from thick, solid rattan vines and split English willow, with a reinforced two-inch white oak plank floor and tubular stainless steel corner frames.
   * It weighed seventy-five pounds, but it was virtually indestructible: flexible, shock-absorbing, buoyant as a cork, and capable of carrying four hundred pounds of cargo without straining a joint.

I hauled the harvested materials down to the riverbank in four trips, dragging the wicker gondola across the smooth alfalfa grass like a sled.

I laid the folded nylon bundles flat in the bottom of the sixteen-foot cedar canoe to serve as dry, waterproof flooring, slid the coiled steel cables beneath the center thwarts, and lashed the wicker gondola upside-down across the gunwales amidships with rawhide cords.

When the tide turned at two o'clock in the afternoon, the ebb current began its violent, rushing retreat back toward the Bay of Fundy.

I pushed off into the brown water. The cedar canoe, carrying four hundred pounds of aerospace-grade materials, rode two inches lower in the current, slicing down the Petkotkweyak on the outbound ebb like an arrow leaving a bowstring.

### Part VI: The Cantilever Hoist on the Bluff

By Day Eighteen, the Old Stone Light had ceased to be an uninsulated granite ruin; it had become an engineered tactical fortress.

I spent three days on the exposed headland, using the salvaged aircraft cables, the wicker gondola, and seasoned white ash timbers from my north woodlot to construct **The Bluff Elevator Hoist.**

On the seaward gallery of the lantern deck, thirty-five feet above the rocky ground, I framed a heavy, triangular cantilever boom out of six-inch rock-maple timbers. The boom projected six feet out past the granite parapet, anchored back through the masonry floor with two of the 1/4-inch stainless steel aircraft cables bolted directly to the central iron spiral staircase column.

At the outer tip of the boom, I hung a heavy-duty stainless steel marine pulley salvaged from the balloon burner frame.

Through that pulley, I ran a hundred and ten feet of the 7x19 stainless aircraft wire, dropping the bottom forged-steel hook down the sheer, vertical granite face of the cliff to the landing platform at the head of the weir trail.

To that bottom hook, I attached the **braided wicker gondola basket.**

Inside the glass lantern room, I built a two-man hand-cranked wooden winch: a six-inch drum of seasoned white oak with four long lever-arms carved from split ash, geared with a rock-maple ratchet pawl that locked the drum every quarter-turn with a solid *clack*.

The mechanics were completely effortless:

```
[ 35-ft Granite Headland ] ──▶ [ Rock-Maple Cantilever Boom ] ──▶ [ Stainless Pulley & Cable ]
                                                                             │
[ Hand-Cranked Oak Winch in Tower ] ◄────────────────────────────────────────┼
                                                                             ▼
                                                                [ Wicker Gondola Elevator ]
                                                                (Lifts 350 lbs from the Trail)
```

You never had to haul fifty-pound sacks of coarse marsh salt, sixty-pound baskets of smoked flounder, or heavy bundles of split firewood up forty-five rusted iron spiral stairs on your shoulders.

You loaded the wicker basket at the base of the cliff, walked up the scarp trail with empty hands, stepped onto the lantern deck, and turned the oak winch.

The braided rattan basket rose up the sheer face of the granite cliff in forty seconds, silent, smooth, and suspended twenty feet out of reach of anything standing on the ground below. 

When the basket reached the parapet, a guide line pulled the boom inward, and the cargo swung safely onto the timber floorboards of the lantern room with a single pull of a wooden lever.

### Part VII: The Weight of the Silence at Twilight

At eight o'clock on the evening of Day Eighteen, I sat on the cold iron gallery railing of the Old Stone Light, forty feet above the crashing Fundy surf.

The south wind was cool, clean, and carrying the wild, resinous scent of balsam fir and salt mud across the headland.

Inside the lantern room behind me, the new ripstop nylon ceiling baffles were glowing deep gold in the last rays of the setting sun. The living snug on the second floor was dry, warm, and stocked with seventy pounds of maple-smoked flounder, eighty pounds of coarse marsh salt, and three sealed totes of physical books.

Below the cliff, the three shallow pit weirs were gleaming like dark mirrors in the receding mud, holding six live striped bass in their stone basins for tomorrow's supper.

To the southeast, the town of Petkotkweyak was entirely dark.

Not a streetlight. Not a headlight. Not a single candle flame visible through the 25x brass spyglass. 

Just a sprawling, silent silhouette of concrete, brick, and steel, rotting quietly under the rising summer stars while the ancient river continued its ten-thousand-year rhythm beneath the broken bridges.

I rested my forearms on the cold iron rail, listening to the vast, unbroken silence of Mi'kma'ki.

There was an ache in my shoulders from eighteen days of felling, dragging, sawing, and lashing—a deep, clean, honest tiredness that felt better than any day I had ever spent working for a contractor’s wage in town.

I was safe. I was warm. I was food-secure for the next two winters.

And yet, as the first white stars began to prick through the violet sky above the Tantramar marshes, a small, cold ache opened up behind my ribs.

It was the silence.

A man can build a fortress out of stone, cedar, and white ash. He can weave weirs that feed him forever and build gates that close themselves under gravity. He can read every page Terry Pratchett ever wrote by the light of a beeswax candle.

But a fortress without another human voice is just a very well-engineered tomb.

"Eighteen days," I said softly to the darkening water.

Nipn came out through the lantern hatch, his nails clicking lightly on the granite floorboards. He sat down beside my knee, leaned his warm, heavy, furry shoulder against my thigh, and looked out over the empty river.

I rested my hand on the dog's thick neck fur, feeling his slow, steady breathing.

"We keep building," I told him. "Someone is going to come down that trail eventually."

I stood up, turned the wooden latch on the lantern hatch, and climbed down into the warm, silent dark of the stone tower.
