# Chapter 2: The Girl with the Backpack

By the middle of the second week, the wind out of the south smelled like an open grave wrapped in burning plastic.

Down in the valley, the supermarket meat lockers had finally reached ambient room temperature. If you had the misfortune of being anywhere near the commercial strip on Mountain Road, you’d have needed a gas mask just to keep from choking on the methane and rotting poultry. Up here in the hemlock hollow, the air was mostly clean, smelling of damp spruce needles, river mud, and the sweet, resinous smoke of my woodstove.

I was on my knees beside the garden patch, driving split-cedar stakes into the soft earth with a heavy wooden beetle mallet I’d carved out of rock maple.

A garden in a maritime summer is basically an all-you-can-eat buffet for deer, raccoons, and anything else wandering through the brush with an appetite. With civilization canceling itself, protecting my flint corn, pole beans, and squash wasn't just gardening—it was defensive engineering. I was weaving long, flexible alder shoots between the upright posts, creating a four-foot wattle fence that would keep out rabbits and trip up anything clumsy that didn't know how to lift its knees.

Beside me, Nipn was lying in the shade of the tool lean-to, his chin resting flat on his front paws.

Suddenly, his left ear twitched backward. 

He didn't bark. He didn't growl. He just rose smoothly to all four feet, his tail held level, and pointed his black muzzle toward the ferns where the old logging spur met the creek trail.

I stopped the mallet mid-swing, lowered it to the grass without a sound, and picked up the ash bear-spear leaning against the corner post.

---

The figure came out of the ferns slowly.

She wasn't shambling, and her jaw wasn't hanging open. She was walking with that careful, heel-to-toe gait of someone who knows that dry twigs snap if you step on them with your full weight. 

She was wearing a worn canvas field jacket, heavy denim pants tucked into laced hiking boots, and carrying an internal-frame Osprey backpack that looked like it had been packed by someone who understood weight distribution. The heavy stuff was close to her spine, not dangling off the back like an oversized pendulum. In her right hand, she carried a three-foot length of galvanized iron pipe, wrapped at the grip with hockey tape.

She was tired—you could see the dark purple shadows under her eyes from ten days of sleeping with one ear open—but her eyes were clear, sharp, and constantly moving between the tree line and the cabin roof.

She stopped fifteen paces away, right where the ferns thinned out into the mossy clearing. 

She clocked Nipn. She clocked the spear in my hand. Then her gaze drifted down to the wattle fence I was weaving, lingered on the neat rows of corn shoots, and slowly came back up to my face.

"That's a wattle fence," she said. Her voice was slightly hoarse from lack of water, but steady.

"It is," I said, keeping my hands resting casually on the ash shaft. "Keeps the deer out. Trips up the slow ones."

She adjusted the chest strap on her pack with her left hand, never letting the pipe drop. "Most people I've seen in the last ten days were trying to barricade themselves inside a Circle K with a staple gun."

"Most people get their survival advice from movies," I replied. "Movies are written by people who live in apartments and think food comes from a delivery driver."

A tiny, exhausted flicker of amusement crossed her mouth. "You're Simon, aren't you? Kwitn?"

I raised an eyebrow. "Word travels fast for a dead telephone network."

"I used to do triage shifts at the clinic on Main Street," she said, nodding toward the valley. "Your cousin Chris brought in a guy who took a framing nail through his palm about six months ago. You came to pick them up. You were carrying three books under your arm and argued with the receptionist about whether the emergency room magazine selection was a psychological torture experiment."

I let out a short breath and lowered the spear tip to the dirt. "It was mostly golf magazines from 2014. It *was* torture. You're Maya, right? The one who actually knew how to irrigate a puncture wound without screaming?"

"Maya Lin," she said. She let the iron pipe rest lightly against her thigh, though her shoulders didn't drop all the way. "I've been living out of this pack since the generators died at the hospital on Day 4. The river trail seemed like the only way out that wasn't blocked by crashed Honda Civics."

"The roads are deathtraps," I agreed. "The river is open."

---

Nipn took two slow, curious steps forward, sniffed the air two feet from her boots, decided she didn't smell like rot or adrenaline panic, and sat down with a soft thump on the moss.

"He's a good judge of character," I said, gesturing toward the camp. "You look like you haven't had clean water since Tuesday."

"Monday," Maya corrected. "I boiled some creek runoff two miles back, but it tasted like mud and pine needles."

"I have cold spring water in a cedar flume behind the porch. And there's a kettle of sweetgrass and chaga on the stove that's still warm."

She looked at the cabin—solid timber frame, hand-split cedar roof, smoke drafting cleanly from the brick-lined flue—and then looked back at me with that sharp, measuring hospital look.

"What's the catch?" she asked.

"No catch," I said, picking up my mallet. "I'm framing the second bay of the garden fence, and my cousin Chris isn't due up from his shop until tomorrow. I've got two flounder sitting in clean saltwater down in the weir that need cleaning, and there's a spare bedroom on the east side of the cabin that’s currently holding two milk crates of Terry Pratchett novels and an empty bed frame with a spruce-bough mattress."

Maya stared at me for three long seconds. 

"You have a guest room," she said, flatly.

"I had a five-year off-grid transition plan," I explained, driving another cedar stake into the ground with a solid *thud*. "I was planning on having family over for hunting season in two years. The world just decided to rush the schedule."

She unbuckled her waist strap, slipped the heavy Osprey pack off her shoulders, and set it down against a tree stump. She took a deep, shuddering breath, looking at the quiet green hollow like someone who had just stepped out of a burning building into a cool rain.

"If there’s hot tea in that kettle," Maya said, rolling her shoulders, "I'll clean the fish, weed your squash patch, and I won't even make fun of your fantasy books."

"Deal," I said. "Though *Small Gods* is actually a philosophical masterpiece. I'll show you where the wash basin is."
