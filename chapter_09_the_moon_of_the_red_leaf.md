# Chapter 9: The Moon of the Red Leaf and the Linen Hearth

---

### Part I: The Morning Cramp & The Empty Pouch

The morning of the first hard frost arrived across the Petkotkweyak with the quiet, biting clarity of broken glass.

At half-past five, the temperature along the river bluffs had plunged to twenty-six degrees Fahrenheit. The thick salt fog of September was gone, replaced by a crystalline northern sky the color of a bruised plum. Every red spruce needle on the ridge was rimed in brilliant white hoarfrost, and the red mud flats thirty feet below the cabin were frozen into a brittle, crusty pavement of iron-hard shale that crunched like gravel beneath the boots of the early gulls.

In the sleeping alcove of the cedar cabin, Maya did not wake to the cold.

She woke to the cramp.

It began as a dull, heavy, rhythmic throb deep in the center of her pelvis—a familiar, leaden ache that radiated outward into her lower back and down the fronts of her thighs like liquid iron.

She lay motionless beneath the two heavy wool blankets from Dorchester, staring up at the rough-hewn spruce ceiling joists, her jaw clenched tight against a sudden, sharp spasm that tightened her abdominal wall.

She knew the arithmetic of her own body.

Twenty-eight days since the rainy night she had broken through the wire in Sussex. Her cycle was right on schedule.

Carefully, bracing her elbows on the mattress of dried marsh ferns and sweetgrass, Maya sat upright.

Her left ankle, three weeks out of its fracture, gave only a faint, stiff twinge. The bone had healed strong under Mac's Kinap setting and Elizabeth's comfrey poultices; she could walk now without the ash staff, though she still kept the wood close at hand for rough ground.

Between her breasts, nestled deep in the fleece-lined cleavage of her unbuttoned flannel shirt, Scar felt the sudden shift in her posture.

The squirrel blinked his black eyes open, let out a tiny, sleepy *chrrr*, and tucked his head back into the hollow between her breasts, seeking the warm shelter of her skin against the morning chill.

Maya ignored the rodent. Her heart was beating with a cold, hollow dread that had nothing to do with broken bones or biker raiders.

She swung her legs over the bed frame, reaching down to the floor where her faded green sixty-five-liter Osprey backpack lay propped against the corner post.

She unzipped the small internal nylon security pocket—the deep, waterproof compartment where she had guarded her most precious, irreplaceable personal survival supplies since fleeing the agricultural labs in Truro.

Her fingers reached into the empty nylon sleeve.

They searched the corners.

They touched a small brass safety pin. A stub of carpenter's pencil. An empty plastic blister pack of ibuprofen that had been empty since July.

And at the very bottom, tucked flat against the back panel, her fingertips brushed a single, crumpled rectangle of thin white plastic.

Maya pulled it out into the dim morning light.

It was a pre-collapse disposable feminine hygiene pad: *Always Ultra-Thin, Heavy Flow, with Wings.*

The outer plastic wrapper was faded, wrinkled, and worn paper-thin from six months of rubbing against the canvas seams of her pack. The adhesive backing strip was yellowed with age, and the edges of the bleached cellulose core were compressed into a flat, stiff wafer.

It was the last one.

The absolute, final, singular pad left in her possession.

Maya stared down at the crumpled white wrapper in her trembling palm, a cold sweat breaking out across her forehead despite the frost on the windowpane.

In the old world, running out of pads was a minor, ten-minute inconvenience: you walked to the corner pharmacy, swiped a debit card, and bought a box of thirty-six bleached cotton pads with blue absorbent gel and floral scent.

In the post-collapse reality of Sussex, running out of pads was a nightmare of filth and degradation.

Maya had watched the women in the refugee camps trade three days of food rations for an expired box of generic tampons salvaged from a flooded pharmacy basement. She had watched women resort to tearing up greasy, oil-stained cotton rags from the tractor repair shops—rags that introduced virulent bacterial infections, pelvic inflammatory disease, and toxic septic fever because there was no clean boiling water to sterilize the cloth.

And now, she was standing in a two-room log cabin on the edge of the Fundy wilderness, thirty miles from the nearest human settlement, sharing a hearth with a six-foot-five, two-hundred-and-sixty-pound bearded giant of a man she had known for barely two weeks.

How in the name of the salt and the shale was she supposed to walk into the kitchen and tell a Kinap woodsman that she was bleeding?

Maya squeezed her eyes shut, pressing the heels of her hands into her temples, letting out a ragged, silent groan of pure mortification.

*Think, Maya. You're an engineer. You have a bachelor of science in agricultural mechanics. You built a three-to-one compound overdrive transmission out of scrap iron. You can figure out how to stop blood from soaking through your only pair of wool trousers.*

She thought of her clothing inventory:

She had one pair of heavy green wool work pants from Dorchester. One pair of denim jeans, torn at the left knee from the culvert fight. Two pairs of wool work socks. And two pairs of cotton underwear.

If she used her spare socks as rags, she would ruin her footgear before the November freezes arrived. If she tore up her spare flannel shirt, she would freeze on the draisine deck.

And in forty-eight hours, this single, fragile, yellowed pre-collapse pad would be soaked, useless trash.

From the outer room, the heavy iron door of the cast-iron cookstove creaked open with a low, metallic squeak.

The sound of dry birch splits hitting the grate echoed through the spruce wall boards, followed by the crisp, fragrant *crackle-pop* of dry birchbark catching flame.

The scent of woodsmoke, roasted chicory, and frying salt pork began to drift beneath the wool door curtain.

Mac was awake.

Maya drew a long, shaking breath, shoved the crumpled plastic pad into her jeans pocket, took her white ash staff in her right hand, and pushed the wool curtain aside.

### Part II: The Honest Conversation at the Wash Basin

The main room of the cabin was warm, bright, and alive with the clean, unhurried rhythm of the morning hearth.

The cast-iron cooktop was radiating a steady wave of dry heat that had already melted the frost off the south-facing windowpanes. In the seasoned Griswold skillet, four thick rashers of dry-cured salt pork were sizzling gently, filling the air with the rich, mouth-watering aroma of toasted hickory and caramelized fat.

Mac was standing at the heavy birch worktable, wearing his gray wool work waistcoat over his linen shirt, his sleeves rolled past his massive, hair-dusted forearms.

He was using a razor-sharp drawknife to shape a replacement spruce thwart for his coastal dory, long, paper-thin ribbons of fragrant white wood curling off the timber and falling in a neat heap between his heavy leather boots.

Beside the stove hearth, Nipn was lying stretched out in the sunbeams, his silver coat gleaming, his great paws twitching softly in post-breakfast contentment.

Mac looked up as Maya stepped through the curtain.

"Morning, Maya," he rumbled, his voice as deep and welcoming as an oak timber. "Fire is hot, the chicory is steeping, and the salt pork has about five minutes left in the pan."

Maya didn't answer right away.

A sharp, vise-like spasm gripped her lower abdomen, so sudden and vicious that her breath hitched in her throat. Her knuckles turned white around the rock-maple knob of her ash staff, and her left hand pressed instinctively flat against the denim of her lower belly.

She tried to straighten her posture, tried to paste a casual, neutral expression across her face, but the blood had drained from her cheeks, leaving her bronze skin pale and tight around the jaw.

Mac stopped the drawknife in mid-stroke.

His pale blue eyes, trained by a decade of tracking wolves and reading the micro-movements of wild animals in the brush, narrowed with instant, laser-like focus.

He set the tool down on the shaving bench without making a sound.

He walked across the split yellow birch floorboards, wiping his hands on a clean linen tea towel, stopping three paces away. He didn't tower over her; he tilted his head, his face softening with immediate, genuine concern.

"Your ankle gave out," Mac said, his tone matter-of-fact. "You put weight on it too fast coming back from Fort Folly."

"The ankle is fine," Maya rasped, her voice tight, strained, and high-pitched.

"Then it's the throat," Mac continued, his eyes dropping to the faint yellowing bruises along her collarbones. "The swelling in the windpipe hasn't settled. Let me brew a pot of wild ginger and marsh willow—"

"Mac, stop," Maya interrupted, closing her eyes, her face burning with a sudden, overwhelming wave of heat that turned her ears crimson. "My ankle is fine. My throat is fine. That’s not... that’s not what’s wrong."

She turned away, hobbling over to the wooden wash basin in the corner, resting both hands on the cedar rim, staring down into the cold spring water.

Mac followed her, stepping up to the adjacent counter, leaning his massive shoulder against the timber wall, waiting in patient, unhurried silence.

Maya stared at her reflection in the dark water.

Her pride screamed at her to shut her mouth, to pretend she had a stomach bug, to go out to the spruce shed and tear up an old oily rag in secret like the desperate women in Sussex.

Then, she looked at Mac’s large, scarred hands resting loosely against his apron.

She remembered him carrying her through the freezing culvert without touching her inappropriately. She remembered him defending her against three raiders at Stony Creek with the fury of a falling mountain. She remembered his furious, apoplectic refusal to let her "pay rent" with her body, declaring that a traveler under his roof was sacred under the ancient laws of *Hospitium*.

If she couldn't trust this man with the basic biology of her own body, then the new commonwealth was a lie.

Maya drew a deep, jagged breath, turned her head, and looked him dead in the eye.

"Is there an abandoned pharmacy in Hillsborough?" she asked bluntly. "Or an old convenience store? A gas station? Anything along the tracks between here and Salisbury?"

Mac’s brow furrowed in mild confusion.

"Hillsborough had a small Co-op pharmacy on the main street," he answered carefully. "The roof caved in during the second winter. The inventory was looted five years ago by refugees heading for the coast. Why? Are you looking for painkillers? I have dried meadowsweet and wild willow bark in the cellar with three times the salicin concentration of synthetic aspirin."

"I don't need aspirin, Mac," Maya said, her voice dropping into a flat, raw, desperate register that held nothing back. "I need pads. Menstrual pads. Cotton, cellulose, adhesive strips, tampons—whatever in the world women used to buy in boxes before the power grid died."

She pulled the crumpled, yellowed plastic wrapper from her jeans pocket and slammed it onto the cedar counter between them with a dry *slap*.

"That is my last one," Maya said, her voice shaking with a mixture of rage, pain, and humiliation. "One single pre-collapse pad. My cycle started twenty minutes ago. In forty-eight hours, that piece of plastic is going to be soaked trash, and unless you have a secret warehouse of paper products behind the lighthouse, I am going to bleed through my only pair of wool trousers. So tell me, Mac—what does your grand, sovereign ten-year homestead do when a woman has her period?"

The silence that followed was absolute.

Maya braced herself for the reaction: the awkward, avert-your-eyes male panic; the stuttering stammer; the disgusted grimace; or the patronizing joke she had heard from dozens of men in Sussex when hygiene supplies ran out.

Mac didn't flinch.

He didn't blush. He didn't avert his eyes.

He looked at the crumpled white plastic pad on the cedar wood. He looked at Maya’s pale, furious, defiant face.

Then, he let out a long, slow breath through his nose, reached out with his broad right hand, picked up the plastic wrapper between two thick fingers, turned it over, and tossed it into the small kindling basket beside the stove.

"You brought six-year-old bleached petrochemical garbage from an industrial warehouse into my valley," Mac said, his deep baritone completely calm, steady, and entirely free of embarrassment. "And you thought I was going to send you to an abandoned convenience store to scavenge rat-chewed cellulose?"

Maya blinked, her jaw going slack in utter astonishment.

"What?"

Mac picked up a clean wooden ladle, dipped it into the kettle of boiling water on the stove, and poured it into an earthenware mug over three spoonfuls of dried green leaves he pulled from a glass jar on the shelf.

"Maya," Mac said, his tone as calm and steady as if they were discussing the weather on the bay, "you're a woman. Women bleed with the moon. It's been that way since the first campfire was lit on this river."

He set the steaming mug on the counter in front of her.

"Do you honestly think," Mac continued, an arched eyebrow rising over his pale blue eyes, "that the L'nu women who lived on this ridge, or the Acadian grandmothers who worked the salt dikes, sat in their cabins crying because nobody invented plastic wrappers with sticky wings?"

Maya stared at him, her mouth opening and closing in surprise.

"I... well... no, but—"

"Plastic pads were just another bad habit from the old cities," Mac said, leaning his hands flat against the counter. "Made to sell people something they never used to need, and make women feel ashamed of their own blood so they'd keep buying boxes every month."

He tapped the wooden counter with his thick knuckles.

"Going into an abandoned drugstore for six-year-old boxes is a waste of time, Maya," Mac said plainly. "The paper is damp, mice have nested in it, and wearing old plastic when you're working out in the cold will just chafe you raw and give you an infection. We don't need town garbage out here."

He looked at her, his pale blue eyes warm with quiet, brotherly reassurance.

"The women down at Fort Folly have gathered moss from that plateau since the glaciers retreated," Mac added, setting the cedar ladle back in its crock. "It grows in deep, golden cushions four feet thick. When you dry it over the stove, it's softer than down and holds twenty times its weight in water. Auntie Elizabeth gave us two bolts of heavy flax linen when we traded down at the ridge. We'll cut envelopes with wings that fold around your undergarments, fastened with polished bone toggles. You slide a pad of dry moss inside, wear it through the day, burn the used moss in the stove firebox, and boil the linen clean with wood-ash lye. Sustainable, sterile, and you'll never have to beg town scavengers for a moldy box again."

"Drink that tea," Mac said gently. "Red raspberry leaf, yarrow, and wild mint from Auntie Elizabeth. It'll settle the cramps in twenty minutes. Then throw on your coat and grab a basket. We're heading out to the bog."

### Part III: The Bog Harvest & Nature’s Cotton

Ten minutes later, the tea had already begun its silent, biochemical work.

The sharp, stabbing cramps in Maya’s pelvis had softened from a jagged white-hot spasm into a dull, manageable ache, the bitter-sweet tannins of the raspberry leaf relaxing the contracted smooth muscle of her uterus like a warm hand laid across her lower stomach.

She followed Mac out the back door of the cabin, carrying a light, woven ash pack-basket on her shoulder.

The frost was brilliant on the boardwalk—a narrow, two-plank walkway of split red cedar laid across the spongy black peat of the muskeg that bordered the southern ridge.

Mac walked ahead of her, carrying a clean wooden rake and a bundle of linen drying sheets over his shoulder.

Between Maya's breasts, Scar was not his usual arrogant, chattering self.

The little squirrel had felt the sudden drop in Maya’s abdominal temperature and the tight, defensive tension in her posture earlier that morning. Instead of popping his head out over her collar to play look-out, he had tucked his entire body down into the deep, warm hollow of her cleavage, his chin resting against the bridge of her bra, his black eyes peering up at her with quiet, unusually gentle concern.

*The female is heavy-bellied,* Scar transmitted, the thought soft, hesitant, and completely devoid of cheek. *She carries the moon-blood. The duplex is warm, but her pulse is slow.*

"I’m fine, Scar," Maya thought back, her gloved fingers resting over the bulge in her flannel shirt. "Just growing pains."

Mac halted at the edge of a vast, open peat bog that stretched fifty acres across the plateau between the black spruce ridges.

The bog was a breathtaking mosaic of arctic colors: mounds of deep burgundy, emerald green, and golden-bronze moss rising out of dark, tea-colored water, studded with low bushes of wild Labrador tea, sweet gale, and dwarf winterberries rimed in silver frost.

Mac stepped off the cedar planks, kneeling beside a massive, raised hummock of pale, sea-green moss that stood eighteen inches above the water table.

"Here," Mac said, plunging his bare hands into the center of the hummock.

He pulled up a massive, dripping cushion of living moss the size of a loaf of bread.

The top two inches were a delicate, star-patterned pale green; below that, the stems were pure, creamy white, fading into soft, fibrous golden strands that held together like a dense, unspun skein of silk.

"Sphagnum bog moss," Mac said, holding the clump up for her inspection. "*Sphagnum palustre*. The English settlers called it bog cotton. The Mi'kmaq called it *msi-gmu*—the blanket of the earth."

Maya leaned forward, taking a handful of the wet moss between her gloves.

As an agricultural botanist, her eyes widened as she examined the cellular structure:

"The hyaline cells," she whispered, her scientific training instantly unlocking the physics of the plant.

"Explain it to me, engineer," Mac smiled, sitting back on his heels in the frost.

"Sphagnum isn't like normal plant tissue," Maya explained, her fingers teasing apart the delicate fibrous strands. "Over ninety percent of its cellular volume consists of dead, hollow, barrel-shaped hyaline cells surrounded by a network of living photosynthetic cells. The hyaline cells have spiral structural bands and microscopic capillary pores that act like open suction pumps."

She squeezed the clump in her fist.

A clean, crystal-clear stream of water poured out between her fingers, and when she released her grip, the moss instantly expanded back to its original shape like a resilient, open-cell synthetic sponge.

"It holds twenty to twenty-five times its dry weight in liquid," Maya gasped. "Modern cotton only holds five to six times its weight! This is four times more absorbent than surgical cotton!"

"And that is only half of its virtue," Mac nodded, his voice thick with ancestral respect. "Modern disposable pads rely on bleached wood pulp and synthetic polyacrylate superabsorbent polymers that turn to chemical sludge. Sphagnum moss contains *sphagnan*—a complex pectin-like polysaccharide in its cell walls that binds metal ions and releases hydrogen ions. It makes the moss naturally acidic, with a pH between three-point-five and four-point-two."

Maya’s jaw dropped.

"That’s the exact natural pH of a healthy human vaginal canal," she whispered.

"Precisely," Mac confirmed. "Bacteria like *E. coli*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and the pathogens that cause toxic shock syndrome cannot survive in an acidic environment. Sphagnum is naturally sterile, naturally antiseptic, naturally antifungal, and completely odor-neutralizing. During the First World War, when the German U-boats cut off the cotton shipments from America, the Canadian Red Cross harvested hundreds of tons of this exact bog moss from the Maritimes to pack the wounds of soldiers in the trenches of France. The British surgeons found that wounds packed with sphagnum healed faster and had eighty percent fewer gangrene infections than wounds packed with sterile cotton gauze."

Mac reached for his ash basket, using his hands to carefully peel the clean, pale-white middle layer of the hummock away from the peat, shaking off the loose water.

"You harvest the clean white fiber below the green tips and above the dark peat," Mac instructed, laying the thick cushions of moss into the basket. "We wash it in boiled spring water to remove any wild seeds, spread it on the cheesecloth screens over the masonry heater for four hours, and it dries into a soft, fluffy, snow-white batting that feels like goose down."

Maya knelt beside him in the frost, plunging her hands into the cold, ancient hummocks, harvesting the fibrous cushions alongside him.

For the first time in her adult life, the crushing, humiliating dread of her own reproductive cycle vanished.

Nature was not a hostile wasteland that required chemical factories to survive. The earth had already built the perfect absorbent technology, refined through ten million years of evolutionary design, growing free and unpolluted in the quiet hummocks of the boreal muskeg.

Within thirty minutes, the two ash pack-baskets were filled to the brim with forty pounds of pristine, pale sphagnum fiber.

"Now," Mac smiled, hoisting the heavy basket to his shoulder, "let's go back to the hearth and show you how the Acadian women tailored the moon cloths."

### Part IV: The Tailoring of the Moon Cloths

By midday, the cabin was transformed into a sun-warmed tailor's shop.

The four screens of cheesecloth suspended from the ceiling joists directly above the masonry heater were piled high with three inches of drying sphagnum moss. The dry, thirty-degree heat rising from the soapstone bricks had driven out the moisture, transforming the damp fibers into an airy, cloud-soft batting that was pure white, completely odorless, and impossibly soft to the touch.

Mac had pulled out a heavy, iron-strapped cedar chest from beneath the worktable, setting it open on the red spruce floor.

Inside lay the textile wealth of the lower estuary:

Dozens of yards of hand-woven, unbleached Acadian flax linen—tightly spun from local flax retted in the salt marshes, washed in wood-ash lye, and sun-bleached on the grass until the fabric was as soft and supple as old silk, yet tough enough to survive fifty years of boiling water.

Beside the linen lay four three-pound batts of carded, scoured sheep's wool fleece from the flocks of Dorchester, snow-white, lanolin-scented, and crimped into springy, resilient clouds.

Mac laid the linen across the birch dining table, setting down a heavy pair of forged Sheffield tailoring shears, two bone bodkins, a spool of fine waxed linen thread, and three dozen carved flat buttons hand-turned from white bone and polished apple wood.

"The tools are yours, Maya," Mac said, pulling off his apron. "I can forge a broadaxe, split a cedar shake, and pack a bearing, but my fingers are too thick for fine needlework. The tailoring is your domain."

Maya sat at the table in the warm sunlight, picking up the heavy Sheffield shears.

Her engineer's mind, which had spent years drafting machine tolerances and tractor power-take-offs, immediately went to work on the ergonomics of feminine anatomy.

She did not cut flat, clumsy rags that would bunch between the thighs and chafe the skin during physical labor.

She took a piece of clean charcoal and drafted a paper pattern directly onto a sheet of heavy brown wrapping paper:

A contoured, hourglass-shaped pad, ten inches long, three inches wide at the center, flaring to four inches at the front and back to provide full coverage without bulk.

To the sides of the center hourglass, she drafted two curved, three-inch wings designed to wrap around the crotch of her wool underwear and secure beneath the gusset with a single flat bone button.

"Look at this design, Mac," Maya called out, her shears gliding through the heavy unbleached linen with a crisp, musical *snick-snick-snick*.

Mac leaned over the table, watching the geometry take shape.

"It’s a double-layered envelope," Maya explained, her fingers pinning the linen layers together with sharp steel needles. "The top layer—the part that touches the skin—is two layers of brushed flax linen. It is smooth, breathable, non-chafing, and hypoallergenic. The bottom layer, facing the underwear, is a dense, tightly woven canvas linen that acts as an impermeable friction barrier."

She pointed to the top end of the hourglass.

"And here, at the back of the envelope, is a clean, overlapping slit," Maya demonstrated. "Like a pillowcase. You don't sew the absorbent core inside permanently, because a thick, permanently stitched pad takes two days to dry after washing and harbors bacteria in the center."

She picked up a handful of the dried, fluffy white sphagnum moss from the drying screen.

She wrapped the cloud-soft moss in a thin, folded layer of carded sheep's wool fleece, forming a resilient, three-inch-wide absorbent core that compressed down to less than a quarter-inch of thickness.

She slid the core through the slit into the linen envelope.

The result was extraordinary:

A sleek, contoured, perfectly balanced reusable menstrual pad.

The dry sphagnum core provided twenty times its weight in sterile, odor-free, antimicrobial absorption.

The thin fleece wrap provided a hydrophobic barrier that wicked moisture away from the skin and prevented compression leakage.

The bone-buttoned wings held the pad immovably centered in her underwear, preventing slipping, bunching, or lateral leaking during hard physical work.

And when the pad was full, the process was pure, sustainable simplicity:

You unbuttoned the wings, slid the saturated sphagnum core out of the envelope, and tossed the natural moss directly into the woodstove hearth, where it burned instantly into clean wood ash.

The linen envelope was soaked in cold spring water, washed in boiling water with natural wood-ash lye soap, and hung in the sun, where the ultraviolet light sterilized the fibers, drying the linen in two hours.

"Zero waste," Maya whispered, holding the finished pad in her hands, her eyes glowing with triumphant engineering satisfaction. "Zero reliance on pre-collapse factories. Zero plastic. It breathes, it heals, and it lasts ten years."

Mac looked at the tailored linen pad, then at Maya’s radiant, empowered face.

He shook his head slowly, a deep, respectful chuckle rumbling in his chest.

"I told you, Maya," Mac said softly. "The earth never left us without answers. We just had to stop listening to the salesmen and start listening to the grain of the wood."

Maya picked up her shears, cutting out six more linen envelopes from the bolt.

By three in the afternoon, seven complete reusable winged pads lay stacked neatly on the birch table—a complete, independent, five-year menstrual management system tailored by her own hands from the plants and animals of the valley.

She had solved the problem.

She was sovereign over her own body once again.

### Part V: Scar the Living Heating Pad & The Raspberry Infusion

By late afternoon, the brief respite provided by the morning tea had begun to fade.

The second wave of the menstrual cycle—the peak uterine contractions that accompanied the onset of active endometrial shedding—descended upon Maya with a vengeance.

A deep, gripping, visceral cramp seized her lower pelvis, radiating up through her lumbar spine with that heavy, sickening ache that made her teeth grind and her breath come in short, ragged gasps.

She set down her bone needle, resting her forehead flat against the edge of the birch table, her hands clutching her lower stomach, a low, involuntary whimper escaping her lips.

Mac was at her side in two strides.

He didn't ask questions. He didn't offer empty platitudes.

He reached down, slid his massive, scarred arms beneath her shoulders and knees, and lifted her from the ash chair as gently as if she were made of spun glass.

He carried her across the room to the wide day-bed built into the alcove beside the masonry heater.

The soapstone bricks of the stove were radiating a deep, penetrating sixty-degree radiant warmth that had soaked into the heavy wool mattresses.

Mac laid her down on her side, tucking a soft, folded sheep's fleece beneath her head, drawing the heavy Dorchester wool blanket up over her shoulders.

He walked to the cooktop, returning with an earthenware bowl filled with a fresh, steaming infusion of red raspberry leaves, dried yarrow blossoms, and wild ginger, sweetened with a generous spoonful of dark summer clover honey.

He set the bowl on the bedside stool, sliding his broad hand behind her neck to help her lift her head, holding the warm rim to her lips.

"Drink deep, sister," Mac murmured, his voice as soothing as the purr of the sea. "The ginger warms the blood; the yarrow stops the inflammation; the raspberry leaf soothes the muscle. Drink it all."

Maya drank the hot, fragrant brew in slow, steady swallows, feeling the clean, medicinal heat spread down her throat and settle into her aching core like liquid comfort.

Mac set the empty bowl aside, banking the coals in the stove firepot, and stepped quietly out onto the porch to chop the evening birch splits, pulling the heavy wool curtain closed to give her darkness and peace.

Maya lay on her side in the warm dimness, her knees pulled up toward her chest, her hands pressed over her lower abdomen, waiting for the herbs to take hold.

Inside her flannel shirt, a sudden commotion began.

Scar, who had been sleeping quietly between her breasts all afternoon, poked his whiskered nose up through her unbuttoned collar.

He looked around the shadowy alcove.

He felt the tight, shivering tremors running through Maya's abdominal wall. He heard the faint, pained catching of her breath.

The squirrel didn't chitter. He didn't demand sunflower seeds.

With slow, deliberate, unhurried caution, Scar turned his body around inside her flannel shirt.

He did not stay in his established command post in the warm fleece of her shirt.

He wiggled down.

He slid down past her ribcage, past her navel, squirming beneath the waistband of her green flannel shirt until he was resting flat across her lower abdomen, right over the agonizing cramp above her pubic bone.

He curled his warm, furry body into a tight, dense, circular russet disc.

He tucked his paws beneath his chest, wrapped his bushy tail over his nose, and pressed his entire ninety-eight-point-six-degree body flat against her skin.

Then, from deep within his tiny eight-ounce chest, Scar engaged his engine.

He began to purr.

It was not a bird-like chirp or an angry chitter; it was a deep, rhythmic, ultrasonic vibration—a continuous, warm, soothing hum that felt remarkably like an electric heating pad set to high.

The intense, localized heat radiating from the squirrel’s fur soaked directly through the cotton of her underwear and into the spasming muscles of her uterus.

Maya’s breath hitched.

She let out a long, shuddering, disbelieving gasp as the iron vise in her pelvis visibly, miraculously began to loosen under the twin barrage of Auntie Elizabeth's raspberry tea and eight ounces of living, purring boreal central heating.

A sudden, wet tear slipped from her eye, tracing a path down her temple into her dark hair.

She reached down beneath the blankets, her fingertips gently resting over the warm, furry ball on her lower belly.

"You absolute little marvel," Maya whispered, a breathless, tearful chuckle catching in her throat. "The landlord has become an eight-ounce hot water bottle."

Scar didn't move. He gave a microscopic, contented twitch of his left ear, pressed his warm belly deeper into her skin, and continued to purr, broadcasting a wave of pure, simple, unwavering devotion across the mental link:

*The bed is hurting,* Scar transmitted softly. *The landlord warms the bed. Sleep, female. The nest is guarded.*

Maya closed her eyes, her hand resting over the little creature on her stomach, feeling the deep radiant heat of the masonry heater at her back and the unshakeable peace of the valley surrounding her.

Within five minutes, the pain had dissolved into a soft, distant hum.

And for the first time in six long years of collapse, Maya fell into a deep, healing, painless sleep, knowing that she was held, protected, and completely at home.

### Part VI: The Test of the Moon Cloths & The Autumn Dawn

The following morning broke over the Petkotkweyak with the blinding, knife-edge clarity of mid-October.

Maya woke in the sleeping alcove before the sun had cleared the eastern bluff, but for the first time since her arrival in the valley, her body was completely free of pain.

The heavy, dragging ache in her pelvis was gone. The tight lumbar spasms had relaxed into a warm, comfortable stillness.

She sat up on the edge of the mattress, taking a slow, deep breath of the crisp spruce air.

She reached down to check her clothing.

The winged linen moon cloth she had tailored at the birch table twenty-four hours earlier had performed with absolute, mathematical perfection:

### Part VII: The Great Autumn Harvest of the Twenty-Four Mounds

By eight in the morning, the sun had burned the hoarfrost off the southern terraces, turning the garden into a shimmering sea of crimson, gold, and deep forest green.

The time had come for the harvest of the **Twenty-Four Mounds**—the ancestral Three Sisters seed Auntie Elizabeth had entrusted to Mac eight years ago, planted by Maya’s own hands during her third morning at the hearth.

The transformation of the garden over the past four months was a masterpiece of biological engineering:

In each of the twenty-four mounds along the stone terrace, the northern flint corn stood nine feet tall, its thick, fibrous stalks as sturdy as bamboo, supporting two and three massive, heavy ears wrapped in pale, papery husks that hung downward toward the earth.

Spiraling up the corn stalks in tight, green helixes were the Wabanaki cranberry pole beans, their heavy, purple-speckled pods swollen with mature, protein-dense seeds, their root nodules fixing nitrogen from the air directly into the soil to feed the corn.

And blanketing the ground between the mounds, sprawling across forty yards of dark, kelp-composted earth, were the massive, hand-sized prickly leaves of the Acadian long-keeper crookneck squash: dense, interlocking foliage that had choked out every weed, shaded the soil to prevent summer moisture evaporation, and was now studded with dozens of massive, dark-orange, bell-shaped squashes weighing thirty to forty pounds each.

Maya walked the terraces without her ash staff.

Her boots gripped the red shale paths with absolute, springy confidence, her body moving with the rhythmic, joyful grace of a woman who had fully reclaimed her physical strength.

She carried two large, woven ash harvest baskets, while Mac walked behind her with the heavy two-wheeled garden barrow he had framed from white ash and fitted with a chilled-iron wheel.

Beside the terrace wall, Nipn lay stretched out in the sun, his amber eyes tracking their movements, his tail thumping softly whenever an ear of corn snapped from its stalk.

"We take the corn first, Maya," Mac instructed, his hands reaching into the first mound. "Grasp the ear at the base, twist ninety degrees downward against the stalk, and snap the shank clean. Leave the husks attached—we need the leaves to braid the drying ropes."

Maya reached into the dense green foliage of the first mound.

Her fingers closed around an enormous, heavy ear of flint corn—ten inches long, thick as a man’s forearm, dense and hard as a piece of seasoned timber.

She gripped the shank, twisted downward, and heaved.

*CRACK.*

The ear broke free with a loud, satisfying pop that smelled intensely of sweet corn silk, dry husk, and rich river loam.

She peeled back the outer papery leaves with her thumbs.

A soft, involuntary gasp of pure wonder escaped her lips.

The kernels revealed beneath the husks were breathtaking:

Eight straight, perfectly aligned rows of enormous, hard, glassy flint corn—not the pale, sickly yellow of modern commercial sweet corn, but a brilliant, deep, iridescent garnet-red, streaked with veins of dark plum-purple and golden amber that caught the morning sun like a tray of polished rubies.

Each kernel was covered in a hard, glassy layer of flint starch—*Zea mays indurata*—so dense and durable that no weevil could chew through it, capable of keeping in a dry pine bin for five years without losing its nutritional viability.

"Look at this, Mac," Maya whispered, holding the glowing garnet ear up into the sunlight. "It looks like jewelry."

"It is jewelry, Maya," Mac smiled, tossing three heavy ears into the barrow. "The jewelry that keeps the blood warm when the bay freezes solid in January."

For four hours, they worked the southern terrace in a continuous, hypnotic rhythm of abundance:

Maya snapping the ears and piling them into the ash baskets; Mac hauling the heavy barrows to the cabin porch; Nipn guarding the perimeter; and Scar riding in the warm fleece of Maya’s shirt, popping his head out every few minutes to inspect each ear of corn with critical, connoisseur-like precision.

By noon, the harvest of the twenty-four mounds stood completed:

Seven hundred and twenty massive, garnet-colored ears of northern flint corn piled on the cedar porch—over sixteen hundred pounds of calorie-dense, glass-hard grain that would yield eight hundred pounds of stone-ground cornmeal and nixtamalized hominy.

Next came the beans:

Maya and Mac sat on the porch bench, stripping the dry, brittle pods from the vines, shelling the Wabanaki cranberry pole beans directly into clean linen sacks.

The beans were fat, rounded, and mottled like wild quail eggs: deep crimson-red speckled with creamy white, packing twenty-four percent complete vegetable protein.

By two in the afternoon, four five-gallon linen sacks sat filled to the brim with ninety pounds of dry shelled beans—enough vegetable protein to sustain two adults for an entire winter without touching a single strip of smoked fish.

And finally, the heavy artillery:

Mac descended to the terraces with his forged billhook, severing the thick, fibrous stems of the crookneck squashes.

Together, they hauled forty-two massive, forty-pound squashes up to the sunny roof of the spruce shed, laying them in single rows across the cedar shakes to cure in the October sun. The solar heat would harden the outer rinds into a tough, impermeable armor that would allow the sweet, nutrient-dense orange flesh to store in the root cellar until the following May without rotting.

Maya sat back on the porch steps, wiping sweat from her brow with the back of her wrist, looking out across the porch:

The ceiling rafters of the cabin porch were draped in fifty-foot braids of braided garnet corn ears, glowing like red gold in the sun. The sacks of beans were tied with rawhide. The squashes were curing on the roof.

She did the mental arithmetic in her head:

Over four million calories of food. Complete proteins, complex carbohydrates, beta-carotene, vitamin C, calcium, and niacin.

All harvested from a quarter-acre of red mud, rockweed compost, and ancient heritage seed.

"We did it, Mac," Maya breathed, her dark eyes shining with immense, unshakeable pride. "We beat the winter before the first blizzard even hit the bay."

Mac leaned his large frame against the porch railing, looking at the mountain of food, then down at the fierce, brilliant woman who had brought the arithmetic to his hearth.

"The seed kept the promise, sister," Mac said softly, his voice thick with deep reverence. "And you kept the hearth."

### Part VIII: Ursus's Pre-Hibernation Feast & Scar's Manic Hoard

By four in the afternoon, the tide had turned at the mouth of the Petitcodiac, and the river was alive with the furious, churning energy of the autumn mackerel run.

The great Fundy tidal bore swept up the red canyon with a low, earth-shaking roar, lifting forty feet of cold ocean water into the estuary, carrying with it millions of fat, oily fall mackerel and sea-run brook trout heading for the spawning channels.

Down on the mud flats, Mac’s stone holding weirs did their ancient, effortless work.

As the tide receded, seventy massive, four-pound fall mackerel were trapped in the sandstone basins, their dark green-and-black tiger stripes flashing in the shallow brine, their thick bellies swollen with rich, calorie-dense ocean fat.

Mac and Maya walked down the shale zigzag trail carrying four large ash fish baskets.

Beside them trotted Nipn, his gray ears forward, his nose testing the wind that blew down the creek ravine.

The wolf halted.

He gave that low, familiar, subterranean hum from deep in his chest.

*The fat mountain walks,* Nipn projected, the thought completely relaxed. *He is as wide as a hay wagon. He can barely lift his legs.*

Through the dying, golden fern fronds at the edge of the creek thickets, a massive black shape emerged onto the red mud flats.

It was Ursus.

In the late October light, the black bear was an absolute, colossal marvel of pre-hibernation biology.

Over the past four months of summer grazing on wild blueberries, tidal gaspereau, and homestead compost, the bear had gained nearly one hundred and fifty pounds of dense, rich adipose fat. He weighed easily five hundred and fifty pounds now, his shaggy black winter coat four inches deep and glistening with natural oils.

His belly hung so low that the long fur brushed the red silt as he walked, his massive shoulders rolling with slow, heavy, ponderous power.

He stopped ten paces from the stone holding pool, sat flat on his massive rump with a wet, squelching *FWUMP* in the mud, rested his front paws on his enormous belly, and let out a long, wheezing, exhausted sigh of pure digestive contentment.

*Too much walking,* Ursus rumbled across the mental link, his thought-form slow, deep, and heavy as an iron fog bell tolling under sixty feet of water. *The belly drags on the ground. The fat is heavy. It is time to find the deep root-hole and sleep until the snow turns into rain.*

Maya laughed—a bright, fearless laugh that carried across the salt flats.

She balanced on her sound boot, reached into the stone basin with Mac’s dip net, and scooped out four of the largest, fattest fall mackerel—each fish thick as a man’s calf, gleaming with silver scales and rich omega-3 oils.

She stepped forward to the edge of the sandstone shelf, six paces from the bear’s paws.

"Here, grandfather," Maya said softly, her voice carrying the clear, sovereign authority of the hearth. "The last tax of the autumn tide. Sleep deep, and wake strong when the ice breaks."

She tossed the four mackerel onto the flat shale between his paws.

*SLAP. SLAP. SLAP. SLAP.*

Ursus didn't even stand up.

He reached out with one massive, five-inch clawed paw, hooked the first mackerel by the gills, brought it to his mouth, and swallowed the entire four-pound fish whole in two rhythmic gulps of his massive throat.

He ate all four fish in ninety seconds, licked a silver scale from his black lower lip, gave a loud, resonant belch that echoed off the sandstone bluffs, and looked up at Maya with small, dark eyes that held centuries of unhurried woodland peace.

*Good female with the wooden stick,* the bear thought, his consciousness washing over her with immense, drowsy warmth. *The silver-skins are very fat. The red tree-rat in her fur is quiet. I will sleep beneath the great red spruce on the high ridge. Do not let the bad road-crawlers wake me.*

"Nobody will wake you, Ursus," Mac rumbled gently, stepping up beside Maya. "The northern gate is shut. The valley is peaceful."

The giant bear gave a deep, coughing *WOOF* of agreement, struggled onto his four massive legs with a great heave of his five-hundred-pound bulk, turned his broad rear to the wind, and waddled slowly, majestically back into the dense spruce forest, heading for his winter den beneath the roots of the mountain.

### Part IX: The Winter Quarters of the Stone Light & The Clacks Salute

Three days after the harvest was gathered into the root cellar, the autumn gave its final gasp, and the true northern winter locked its iron jaws around the valley.

The mercury in the bronze thermometer on the cabin porch had dropped to twelve degrees Fahrenheit.

The Petitcodiac was no longer liquid red mud: the high salt tides still surged up the gorge twice a day, but along the banks and over the mud flats, massive rafts of jagged, six-inch shore ice—churned into round, frozen cakes by the tidal bore—were grinding against the sandstone bluffs with a continuous, groaning roar that sounded like crushing millstones.

The time had come to execute the annual winter migration: moving the core living quarters from the summer cedar cabin up into the warm, unassailable fortress of the **Old Stone Light**.

Mac and Maya spent two full days carrying the winter provisions up the seventy-four spiral steps of hand-cut Albert County granite:

Forty pounds of nixtamalized garnet hominy corn; two five-gallon crocks of salt-cured fall mackerel; three sacks of dried Wabanaki cranberry pole beans; four gallons of wild clover honey; and sixty pounds of seasoned salt pork.

The winter apartment inside the sixty-foot granite lighthouse was a marvel of nineteenth-century engineering and antiquarian comfort:

A circular living chamber thirty feet across, located forty feet above the bluffs, surrounded by four-foot-thick solid granite walls that absorbed the screaming Fundy gales without a single tremor.

In the center of the chamber stood a massive, circular cast-iron cylinder stove—an antique parlor heater with mica windowpanes, connected to a heavy copper flue that ran straight up through the lantern room ceiling.

Lining the curved stone walls were four thousand antiquarian volumes on hand-built white pine shelves: eighteenth-century treatises on maritime hydraulics, navigation charts, herbal pharmacopeias, botany, classical literature, and the complete works of Shakespeare and Joseph Howe, bound in blind-tooled calfskin and dark green buckram.

Maya sat in a deep, wool-upholstered wingback armchair beside the cylinder stove, holding an earthenware mug of steaming chicory and roasted dandelion root.

On the pine table beside her lay her tailored linen moon cloths—washed, boiled, sun-bleached, and neatly folded into a clean cedar box alongside two bushels of dry, fluffy sphagnum batting. Her cycle had ended cleanly on the fifth day, with zero infections, zero chafing, and zero waste.

Across her lap lay her white ash staff, though she rarely touched it now; her left ankle was healed, tight, and strong.

And curled inside the warm, flannel-lined V-neck of her green work shirt, wedged comfortably in the warm fleece of her shirt, Scar was fast asleep.

The squirrel’s private hoard had been reorganized: he had transferred three cracked butternuts from her bra into a hollow pine knot beside the library bookshelf, but he had kept two prime sunflower seeds and a single glossy garnet corn kernel tucked behind her underwire as an emergency traveling ration.

Mac walked up the spiral stairs carrying the final armload of split sugar maple logs, dumping them into the copper woodbox with a dull, satisfying clatter.

He wiped his hands on his trousers, walked to the south observation window, and looked out across the gray, ice-strewn expanse of Shepody Bay.

"The wind is swinging around to the northeast, Maya," Mac said, his deep baritone echoing off the curving stone walls. "The barometric pressure has dropped twenty millibars in four hours. The first true nor'easter of the winter is crossing Cape Chignecto."

He walked to the corner cabinet, pulling out the brass-bound signal ledger and adjusting the leather tension straps on the optical telegraph levers.

"Before the snow blinds the bay," Mac smiled, "we send the final seasonal salute to the ridge."

Maya set her mug down, standing up with smooth, effortless balance, and followed him up the last iron ladder into the seventy-foot lantern room.

The circular glass gallery was cold, crisp, and vibrated with the immense power of the gathering gale. Outside the curved plate glass, thirty miles of dark spruce hills and frozen salt marshes stretched away into a wall of dark, leaden snow clouds rolling in from the Atlantic.
