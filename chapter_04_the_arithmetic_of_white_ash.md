# Chapter 4: The Arithmetic of White Ash

### Part I: Awakening to the Hearth & The Broth

The first sensation was not pain. It was the smell of boiling venison marrow and dried summer sweetgrass.

Maya opened her eyes.

Above her head ran the massive, hand-hewn hemlock ceiling beams of the cabin, their axe-planed surfaces darkened to the rich, warm color of clover honey by years of beeswax and gentle woodsmoke. Between the beams, the split cedar roof decking sat tight, dry, and immoveable. 

There was no dripping rain. No freezing wind howling through broken highway overpasses. No smell of wet nylon, sour ditch mud, or the metallic terror of gun grease.

She lay on a thick mattress that crackled softly beneath her shoulders—clean oat straw blended with dried sheep’s wool and sweetgrass that released a faint, summery perfume whenever she breathed. Over her chest lay a heavy patchwork quilt, warm and smelling of pure tallow soap, tucked neatly beneath her chin.

She moved her toes.

Her right foot responded instantly, warm and resting against the soft wool blanket.

Her left foot, however, felt heavy as an anvil. She looked down beneath the quilt.

Her left leg, from the mid-calf down to the ball of her foot, was encased in a rigid, seamless sleeve of pale green-and-white birch bark. The bark had dried tight to the contours of her ankle, bound with neat, unbleached linen bands pinned with polished bone needles. Beneath the bark, she could feel the cool, soothing moisture of fresh sphagnum moss.

The agonizing, throbbing fire that had made her wish for a quick bullet in the spruce swamp was gone. In its place was a dull, numb, manageable ache—as if the bones in her ankle had agreed to call a temporary truce with gravity.

A soft, rhythmic sound caught her ear.

Maya turned her head toward the doorway.

Lying across the threshold of the bedroom, his massive gray-and-silver head resting flat against his front paws, was Nipn.

The wolf’s amber eyes were open. He did not growl. He did not bare his teeth. He merely blinked—a slow, ancient, measured blink of calm appraisal that held neither threat nor subservience.

Maya’s breath hitched in her throat, her knuckles instinctively clenching the quilt.

*The fire is gone from the blood,* a thought-form drifted into the room.

It did not arrive with the blare of a human voice. It was an impression: the sensation of cold, clear spring water running over hot river pebbles, accompanied by the quiet image of a deer standing motionless in the morning mist.

Maya stared at the wolf, her jaw slightly slack.

"Did you... did I just hear you?" she whispered, her voice rough as sandpaper.

Nipn did not answer with words. He merely let out a soft, wet *chuff* through his black nostrils, lifted his wedge-shaped head two inches, and glanced toward the kitchen.

Footsteps approached.

They were not the frantic, heavy stomps of scavengers. They were the slow, measured, acoustic thuds of heavy double-soled leather boots moving with absolute, balanced deliberation across two-inch hemlock planks.

Mac stepped into the doorway.

He carried a wide oval wooden tray carved from yellow birch. On the tray sat a steaming earthenware bowl of dark, golden broth glistening with droplets of rendered venison fat, a clean pewter spoon, a small wooden plate holding two toasted oat-cakes smeared with amber cloudberry honey, and a steaming mug of mint-and-willow tea.

He was wearing his clean oatmeal work shirt, his gray-streaked brown beard combed neat, his pale blue eyes clear and rested.

"Good morning, Maya," Mac said, his deep baritone filling the room like warm wool. "You've slept for thirty-six hours. The fever broke around three o'clock yesterday afternoon, and the swelling in the ankle has dropped by half."

Maya pushed herself up onto her elbows, the heavy quilt sliding down to her waist.

She looked at the food, then at the massive man standing in the doorway, and finally down at the wolf lying across her threshold.

"You really live here," she said, her voice shaking slightly with the sheer, overwhelming reality of it. "A real cabin. A stove. A wolf that sleeps in the hall like an overgrown collie."

"Nipn would take profound semantic exception to being called a collie," Mac smiled, walking across the room with the tray. "Collies have been subjected to two centuries of selective breeding for neurotic sheep-management. Nipn is an Eastern timber wolf. He has no interest in sheep unless they are slow enough to catch, and he considers neurotic behavior an inefficient expenditure of calories."

He pulled up a three-legged ash stool beside the bed, setting the tray across her lap with infinite, careful gentleness.

"Drink the broth first," Mac instructed softly. "Venison marrow, wild leeks, dried sea kelp for iodine, and cracked barley. It will rebuild the red blood cells you left in the railway ditch."

Maya didn't wait.

She picked up the pewter spoon, dipped it into the steaming liquid, and brought it to her lips.

The taste made her eyes sting with sudden, involuntary tears. It was rich, savory, and thick with natural gelatin that coated her dry throat, carrying the deep, mineral vitality of clean well water and salt cured meat. It tasted like life. It tasted like home.

She ate three spoonfuls in rapid succession, her hands trembling slightly, before pausing to look up at him.

"You said your name was Mac," she said, wiping her lower lip with the back of her thumb. "Just Mac? Or is that short for something?"

Mac paused, looking down at his large, scarred hands with a quiet, thoughtful smile.

"In the old tongue—the L'nu language of my people—my name is *Kwitn*," he said, the word rolling from his throat with that soft, guttural, rhythmic cadence of the Maritimes that carried the sound of a birch paddle cutting through cold salt water.

Maya’s lips twitched as she tried to shape the sound. "K... Kw-wheet'n? K-witten? Kwuh-t'n?"

Her tongue stumbled over the initial consonant cluster, her brow furrowing in acute linguistic frustration.

"My tongue just tried to tie itself into a clove hitch," she groaned, shaking her head. "It sounds like someone clearing their throat while swallowing a syllable. I'm going to break a tooth trying to pronounce that with an Acadian accent."

"Most people do," Mac smiled gently. "The English and the French have spent four hundred years flattening indigenous phonemes into vowels they can digest with white bread. It means the ocean canoe. The traditional birch-bark vessel with the raised, flared gunwales that can ride forty-foot Fundy breakers without taking water."

Maya looked at his broad shoulders, at the calm, unsinkable depth in his blue eyes, and nodded slowly.

"A canoe that doesn't sink. Yeah. That fits you," she admitted. "But my mouth is strictly Acadian French and bad road slang. Can I just call you Mac?"

"Mac works fine," he chuckled softly. "One syllable. Economical, functional, and reduces vocal expenditure by forty percent. Leaves more breath for sawing wood and drinking tea. The late William of Ockham would applaud the lexical parsimony."

Maya gave a dry, approving smirk.

"Good. Mac it is. You chop trees, bend iron with your fingers, and throw two-hundred-pound raiders into culvert pipes. Mac fits."

Maya paused with her spoon halfway to the bowl, giving him a sharp, sideways glance.

"Did you just quote a medieval philosopher over a bowl of deer soup?"

"I was an archivist for forty years, Maya," Mac said with complete deadpan sincerity. "Old habits die harder than modern civilization."

### Part II: The Transit & The Accidental Squish

Ten minutes later, the wooden bowl was wiped clean with the crust of the last oat-cake, and the mint tea was warm in Maya's belly.

The color had returned to her cheeks—a healthy, golden olive tone that contrasted sharply with the dark hair tumbling across her shoulders. The fever had receded entirely, leaving only the stiff, bruised ache of a body that had spent four days running for its life.

She set the empty tray on the ash stool, pushed back the heavy patchwork quilt, and swung her legs toward the edge of the bed.

"Right," Maya muttered, gripping the side of the mattress with both hands. "I need to get up. The bladder is officially filing an emergency requisition, and I want to see what this place looks like in the daylight."

"Steady now," Mac cautioned, rising from his seat and stepping to the foot of the cot. "The talus is reseated and the fibula is splinted, but the bone has only had thirty-six hours of osteoblast activity. If you put sixty percent of your body mass on that left heel, the fracture will displace and we'll be starting the carpentry all over again."

"I can hop," Maya insisted, gritting her teeth. "I've hopped across rocky creeks with a thirty-pound rucksack. Just point me toward the porch."

She planted her sound right foot on the hemlock floorboards and pushed upward with her arms.

The moment her left foot even brushed the floor, a sharp, white-hot lance of nerve warning shot up her shin. Her right knee buckled under the sudden, uneven load. She gasped, her balance evaporating, pitching forward toward the hard timber planks.

Mac didn't dive. He took half a step, extended his right arm, and caught her smoothly by the waist before she had dropped two inches.

His grip was immovable—like being caught by an iron crane wrapped in wool.

"Hopping," Mac observed mildly, holding her suspended effortlessly above the floor, "is an inefficient locomotive strategy with a ninety percent probability of secondary orthopedic trauma. Human bipedalism relies on alternating bilateral support; remove one pillar, and the center of mass immediately falls outside the base of support."

Maya hung there for two seconds, her hands resting against his massive shoulder, breathing hard through her teeth.

"You're very smug for a guy in wool socks, Mac," she muttered.

"Not smug, Maya. Merely an enthusiastic observer of Sir Isaac Newton."

Mac cleared his throat. He looked down at her with polite, dignified gravity, keeping his eyes squarely on hers.

"If you're needing to get out to the porch bench for the morning sun and the facilities, Maya," Mac said, his tone formal and gentlemanly, "I can provide the transit. But only if you don't mind being carried. You have the right of bodily agency in this house, and I will not lay hands upon an invalid without explicit consent."

Maya looked at his broad, weathered face, at the honest, unpretentious blue eyes that held not a shred of leering arrogance or predatory intent. After four months on the highway—four months of sleeping with a knife in her boot and treating every human male as a potential murderer—having this massive, terrifying titan politely request permission to carry her across a room hit her right in the chest.

Her shoulders softened.

"Yeah," she breathed, a little sheepish. "Yeah, Mac. Provide the transit. Please. Just... don't drop me."

"I have dropped two tea cups and one volume of Pliny in forty years," Mac assured her solemnly. "I have never dropped a passenger."

Mac stepped forward, bending his knees to lower his center of gravity.

He slipped his right arm behind her shoulders, cradling her upper back, and slid his left arm beneath her knees, lifting her clear of the floor in a single, fluid, velvet-smooth motion.

Then, physics happened.

Maya was not a tiny, delicate waif. She was a strong-boned, broad-shouldered woman with wide hips and an exceptionally generous, well-endowed bust that even her heavy flannel work shirt and canvas vest couldn't conceal. 

Mac, for all his gentlemanly intentions, was built like a four-hundred-pound granite lintel. His chest was a solid wall of dense, unyielding muscle and heavy bone.

As he scooped her up to tuck her against his center of mass to prevent her fractured ankle from swinging, her torso was pressed squarely and firmly against his chest.

*SQUISH.*

Maya’s dark eyes went wide as dinner plates.

The sheer, unyielding density of Mac’s ribcage compressed her exceptionally generous cleavage with the uncompromising force of an industrial hydraulic press. Her breath left her lungs in a sharp, squeaking *OOF*, her ribs flattening against a torso that felt less like human muscle and more like a dressed granite pier supporting a suspension bridge.

She winced, gasped, and tapped his collarbone sharply with two knuckles.

"Hey! Paul Bunyan! Watch the front upholstery!"

Mac froze mid-stride, his boots hovering an inch above the floorboards, his center of gravity locked like a surveyor's transit. "Pardon?"

"The upholstery!" Maya gasped, her face flushing a vivid, furious crimson that fought between acute embarrassment and indignant laughter. "You're crushing forty pounds of cleavage flat against your chest like you're trying to press cider! I know you're carved out of the continental shield, Mac, but there are actual ribs, nerve endings, and breathing apparatus under this flannel! Give a girl some shock absorption before you turn my lungs into crepes!"

Mac’s face went entirely rigid.

A faint, unmistakable wave of deep copper-pink rose beneath his gray-streaked brown beard, spreading rapidly across his cheekbones to the tips of his ears. The man who had stopped a twelve-gauge shotgun blast with an open palm and hurled a two-hundred-pound raider into a culvert pipe without blinking was suddenly reduced to acute, flustered academic confusion.

He immediately shifted his grip, extending his thick forearms three inches outward to create a polite, respectful, and geometrically rigorous buffer of negative space between her bust and his chest, holding her suspended in the air with the effortless ease of someone balancing a folded newspaper.

He cleared his throat twice—a dry, rasping sound that echoed through the quiet room—his blue eyes studiously locking onto the top molding of the doorframe.

"My sincerest and most humble apologies," Mac stammered, his voice dropping into that rapid, deadpan, overly clinical register he instinctively used whenever raw human biology collided with his scholarly composure. "Three-dimensional spatial geometry becomes somewhat fraught when attempting to distribute physical mass across a high-volume anatomical profile. I was merely attempting to establish an immovable structural fulcrum beneath your mid-thoracic vertebrae to eliminate rotational torque on the distal fibular fracture."

Maya let out a snort of laughter that turned into a full, helpless, head-shaking chuckle, resting her hands against the hard tops of his shoulders.

"Right. Archimedes," she grinned, shaking her head as her dark curls tumbled across her cheek. "Greek for 'watch where you're squishing.' You've got hands like snow shovels and a chest like an anvil, Mac. Just remember there’s soft tissue in this flannel. Don't turn me into apple butter, professor."

"Apple butter was never part of the archival curriculum," Mac murmured, recovering a shred of his dignity as he carried her with exaggerated, velvet-smooth gentleness through the common room.

From his braided wool rug beside the cool masonry heater, Nipn watched the entire awkward human negotiation with his gray chin resting flat between his paws.

The silver timber wolf let out a long, wheezing, theatrical sigh through his black nostrils, his amber eyes rolling upward toward the ceiling beams with an expression of profound, cosmic exhaustion.

*Two-legs make moving across a room look remarkably complicated,* Nipn transmitted directly into Mac’s mind, his mental tone heavy with deep canine pity. *All that noisy talking about front-meat. Four legs are better. No upholstery to complain about.*

"Quiet, Nipn," Mac muttered under his breath, pushing open the heavy tamarack door with his left shoulder. "Canine opinions on human biomechanics are entirely unhelpful and historically inaccurate."

Maya blinked, looking around the room. "Did you just talk to the dog again?"

"Wolf, Maya," Mac corrected mildly. "And no. I was merely addressing the atmospheric humidity."

### Part III: The Carving of the White Ash Crutches

The cedar porch bench was wide, warm, and smelled of sun-baked white pine.

Mac set Maya down with the gentle, balanced deceleration of a ship easing into a dry dock, making sure her splinted left leg was extended straight along the bench on a folded wool blanket.

Maya took a deep, shuddering breath of the morning air.

For the first time in five months, the air didn't taste of burnt plastic, diesel soot, or the bitter copper panic of the road. It smelled of crushed wild thyme from the courtyard, sweet resin from the coastal spruces, and the distant, clean salt perfume of forty miles of exposed Fundy mud flats steaming in the June sun.

She looked around the clearing, her jaw dropping.

"Jesus, Mac," she whispered, her dark eyes darting from the low-slung, weathered cabin to the eight-foot wall of woven green wood encircling the homestead. "Is that... is that fence *growing*?"

"Black willow, thorny hawthorn, and coastal white cedar," Mac said, walking down the three porch steps toward the workshop shed. "Braided and fused over ten seasons. Nature does not build in right angles, Maya. If you build a dead wooden fence, the Maritime damp rots the posts in seven years. If you plant a living fence, every storm makes the roots dig deeper, and every winter gale only tightens the weave."

He returned carrying a six-foot log of northern white ash (*Fraxinus americana*), four inches in diameter, split cleanly down the center into two half-rounds, along with a wooden shaving horse, a heavy drawknife, and an English spoke-shave.

He set the shaving horse on the crushed shell path four feet from the porch bench, straddling the wooden bench like a saddle.

He clamped the first half-round of white ash into the horse’s swinging dumbhead vise, resting his booted feet against the lower foot-treadle.

"Before we discuss politics or current events," Mac said, pulling a six-foot surveyor’s folding boxwood rule from his canvas pocket, "we have an urgent engineering problem to solve. You cannot live on a porch bench, and you cannot hop across crushed clam shells without breaking your neck. You need independent mobility."

He walked over to the bench, kneeling on one knee before her.

He unfolded the boxwood rule with a sharp series of wooden clicks.

"May I measure the axilla?" Mac asked politely, holding the rule ready.

Maya shook her head, a dry, self-amused smirk playing across her lips. "You're asking permission to measure my armpit after you just crushed my chest like a cider press?"

"Procedural consistency is the bedrock of civilization, Maya," Mac replied without blinking an eye. "Accidental spatial collisions during emergency medical transit are regrettable anomalies; taking anatomical measurements for an orthopedic apparatus without prior consent is an unpardonable breach of domestic decorum."

Maya threw her head back and laughed—a rich, full, throat-clearing laugh that shook the tension right out of her ribcage.

"Measure away, professor," she grinned, lifting her left arm. "Just don't tickle me. I have an involuntary right hook when I'm startled."

Mac measured with clinical precision: forty-eight inches from her axilla to the floor; twenty-eight inches from the underarm cradle down to the natural flexure of her wrist, where the horizontal hand-grip would seat; and a four-inch spread for the palm.

"Perfect," Mac said, noting the dimensions in pencil on the white wood of the rule. "Proportion is everything. A crutch that is two inches too tall jams into the brachial plexus, cutting off radial nerve circulation and numbing the fingers. A crutch that is two inches too short forces the thoracic spine to hunch forward, fatiguing the lumbar erectors within two hundred yards. Vitruvian man applies to mobility aids as strictly as it applies to cathedrals."

He sat back on the shaving horse, grabbed the two handles of the drawknife, and leaned his weight into the stroke.

*SHIIIIP. SHIIIIP. SHIIIIP.*

Long, paper-thin curls of creamy white wood peeled off the ash round under the razor edge of the carbon steel blade, curling into tight, springy spirals that piled in a fragrant heap between his boots.

"White ash," Mac explained as the steel sang against the wood. "The ancient Norse called it *Yggdrasil*—the World Tree that binds heaven, earth, and the underworld. The Romans used it for the shafts of their legionary pilum spears. Modern sporting companies made baseball bats and hockey sticks from it until carbon fiber ruined the romance of timber."

He flipped the piece, tapering the lower shaft down to a graceful, octagonal strut.

"Why ash?" Maya asked, watching the rhythmic, hypnotic stroke of the blade.

"Shock absorption and flexural modulus," Mac replied, not breaking his cadence. "Ash has long, continuous cellulose fibers bound by flexible lignin. When you plant a crutch on hard stone, the impact sends a kinetic shockwave up the shaft. Oak is too brittle—it rattles your teeth. Pine is too soft—it snaps under shear load. Ash absorbs eighty percent of the impact through axial deflection, flexing just enough to act as an organic spring before snapping back true. It is a shock absorber grown by sunlight and rain."

With an iron froe and a wooden mallet, Mac carefully split the upper two-thirds of the ash shaft vertically down the center line, stopping precisely four inches above the lower foot. 

Then, using a pair of wooden wedges and hot water from the copper kettle, he gently spread the split halves apart, bending the green wood into a classic, elegant bifurcated Y-frame.

He carved a contoured horizontal cross-strut from dense rock maple for the hand-grip, securing it through the ash arms with two dried white oak trunnels driven through mortises and wedged with walnut wedges.

At the top, spanning the two curved arms of the Y, he mortised a curved wooden cradle shaped to fit the contour of a human chest wall beneath the axilla.

He didn't leave the wood bare.

From his workshop box, Mac retrieved two thick pads of boiled wild sheep’s wool he had cleaned and carded the previous autumn. He laid the soft, springy wool across the wooden cradles, wrapping them in strips of supple, oil-tanned buckskin—brain-tanned deer leather as soft as velvet—and bound them tight with waxed linen thread using a heavy leatherworker’s harness needle.

Finally, at the bottom of the crutch, he fitted a thick, two-inch ferrule made from a slice of high-pressure hydraulic rubber hose salvaged from the abandoned rail yard, secured with a brass wood screw.

"Non-slip on wet rock," Mac said, holding up the completed left crutch. "And completely silent on crushed shell. You don't want a wooden peg clicking like a metronome when you walk through the woods."

He turned to the second piece of ash and went to work on the right crutch.

Maya watched the muscles of his broad shoulders moving beneath his work shirt—effortless, tireless, like a waterwheel turning in a steady millrace.

"You really don't get tired, do you?" she said quietly.

Mac paused, looking over his shoulder with a raised brow. "I sleep eight hours a night, Maya. I assure you, fatigue is an inescapable thermodynamic reality of biological existence."

"That's not what I mean," she said, leaning forward on the bench, her dark eyes intense. "I saw what you did at the railway culvert. I saw you crush a steel rifle with one hand like it was made of stale pastry. I saw you take a blow from a barbed-wire steel pipe on your bare arm, and the pipe bent while you didn't even flinch. And you were holding me the whole time. You didn't even drop your shoulder."

She paused, swallowing hard.

"What are you, Mac? Really?"

### Part IV: Maya's Story & The State of the Road

Mac did not answer immediately.

He pulled the drawknife across the second ash strut, letting two long ribbons of white wood flutter down to the shells. His blue eyes looked out past the living fence, toward the red waters of the river winding through the Acadian hills.

"In the old stories of this valley," Mac said slowly, his voice dropping into that low, resonant register, "the Mi'kmaq spoke of the *Kinap*. Not gods. Not monsters. Simply men upon whom the earth laid an unusual degree of weight and balance. Men who are as strong as they need to be, when the need is real. Men who carry the responsibility of maintaining the peace of the woods."

He looked back at her, his face completely free of bravado.

"I am not a superhero, Maya. I have no cape, no secret lair, and no desire to rule over anyone. I am a retired archivist who likes old books, good tea, and straight-grained timber. My strength is inborn; it cannot be taught, and it cannot be passed on. But it is not a weapon of conquest. It is simply a very heavy shovel for taking care of my hearth."

Maya stared at him for a long moment.

Her face, hardened by months of terror, softened into something approaching awe.

"A Kinap," she whispered, tasting the syllables. "Well... whatever you call it, I owe you my life. If you hadn't come down that rail spur, Cole and his bastards would have finished me in that ditch."

"Who was Cole?" Mac asked, continuing to shape the hand-grip with the spoke-shave.

Maya’s expression darkened, a cold, bitter shadow settling across her high cheekbones.

"Cole was a diesel mechanic from Sussex before the world went to hell," she said, her hands clenching into fists on her lap. "When the grid collapsed in January and the grocery stores in Moncton and Saint John emptied out in seventy-two hours, people lost their minds. Modern people had three days of food in their pantries and zero idea where water came from once the municipal electric pumps lost three-phase power. Sussex burned inside of a week. The fuel tanks at the highway truck stops went dry, and the people who didn't know how to grow a cabbage turned into pack animals."

She looked down at her bruised hands, picking at a callus on her thumb.

"I had a small holding outside Salisbury. An acre of potatoes, a garden, two milking goats, and fifty laying hens. I thought I could wait it out. I thought if I kept my head down, boiled my own well water, and split my own firewood, the government or the army would get the generators back online. But by the second month, when the late February freezes hit and the temperature dropped to twenty below zero, the city emptied out like an anthill on fire. Desperate people walking the four-lane highway in bands of ten, twenty, fifty. They had children freezing in shopping carts. They stripped every pantry, broke every root cellar, and slaughtered every animal within ten miles of the asphalt."

Her voice dropped, tight with remembered grief.

"They took my goats, Mac. They butchered them in my yard and burned the shed to keep warm for four hours. When I tried to stop them, two guys with crowbars chased me into the woods. I grabbed my pack, my skinning knife, and an ash pole I’d shaved down for a walking stick, and I started walking south. I didn't have a plan. I just knew that staying near the roads was suicide."

"Why the railway line?" Mac asked gently.

"Because the Trans-Canada Highway was a slaughterhouse," Maya said, her dark eyes looking past the living fence. "Miles of stalled-out cars, burned-out tractor-trailers, abandoned luggage, and gangs perched on the highway overpasses waiting for lone travelers like vultures on a fence line. The railway was quiet. The tracks were straight, elevated above the spring mud, and cut through the deep black spruce woods, away from the suburban subdivisions. I was heading for the coast—for Hopewell Cape, Alma, or Cape Enrage. The old folks in Salisbury used to say there were fishing villages down on the bay that still had dories, salt, and weirs."

She let out a dry, self-deprecating laugh.

"I made it as far as the Salisbury transmission towers. I was crossing the marsh culvert during the storm when Cole’s crew jumped me from the blackberry bushes. They’d set up camp in an abandoned rail siding tool shed. They wanted my pack. They wanted my boots. Cole came at me with an iron pry bar. I got his brother in the throat with my ash spear—drove the knife right through his neck—and then I jumped down the embankment into the quagmire. My ankle snapped on a submerged hemlock root. I heard the bone go *crack*, and the whole world turned white."

She shuddered, the memory making her skin prickle.

"I dragged myself on my elbows through the mud until I found that dead pine hollow. I wedged myself under the root ball, bound my leg with my flannel shirt, held my spear across my chest, and lay there in the freezing rain for two days waiting for Cole to find me, or for the sepsis to kill me."

She looked up at Mac, her dark eyes shining with raw, unvarnished gratitude.

"And then you showed up with a timber wolf, caught a shotgun blast with an open hand, broke their steel like dry twigs, and carried me out like a sack of groceries."

Mac set down his tools on the shaving horse.

He walked over to the porch bench, holding out the two completed white ash crutches.

They were works of functional art: straight, silky-smooth to the touch, their Y-frames curving with organic grace, their underarm cradles thickly padded in soft buckskin and boiled wool, their base ferrules capped with tough, non-slip rubber.

"The past is behind the living fence, Maya," Mac said gently, offering her the maple hand-grips. "In this house, under the ancient laws of *Netukulimk*, you are not a refugee. You are a guest. And as long as you are under this roof, the road cannot touch you."

Maya reached out, her fingers closing around the smooth, warm wood of the handles.

She looked up at him, her chin lifting with that stubborn, unyielding Acadian pride that no disaster could ever extinguish.

"Right," she said, her voice steadying. "Then let's see if these wooden legs of yours can actually walk."

### Part V: The First Appearance of Scar & The Language of Woods

Maya was just tucking the buckskin cradles of the crutches beneath her arms when a coppery-red streak dropped out of the balsam fir canopy like a feathered dart.

*PLOP.*

Landing squarely on the flat cedar railing three feet from her left elbow sat eight ounces of vibrating, puffed-up indignation.

It was Scar.

The Acadian red squirrel was standing bolt upright on his hind legs, his tail curved over his back like a question mark of pure flame, his tiny cream-colored paws clenched tightly against his white belly fur. His long, black whiskers were quivering at an impossible speed, and his obsidian eyes bulged as he stared at the new human occupying his porch.

*TCH-TCH-TCH-TCH-KRRRRRRRRRRRRRR!*

He let out a staccato barrage of territorial alarm chitters that sounded like someone shaking a jar of dry pebbles inside an empty kettle.

He stamped his hind feet twice against the cedar rail, leaned forward until his black nose was barely an inch from Maya's flannel knee, and unleashed a torrent of furious mental invective that slammed into Mac’s consciousness like a pocket-sized hurricane.

*TCH-KRRRR!* Scar’s thoughts screamed. *THE MUD-BUNDLE HAS EYES! IT IS SITTING ON MY SUN-WOOD! IT BREATHES MY FIR-AIR! TELL IT TO VACATE THE PROPERTY, MOUNTAIN-WALKER! I HAVE NOT ISSUED A FORAGE PERMIT! IT IS LARGE! IT HAS BOOTS! IT WILL EAT MY SEED CACHES!*

Mac, who was leaning against the porch post with his arms folded across his chest, let out a soft, dry sigh.

"Good morning, Scarlet," Mac spoke aloud, his gravelly baritone completely calm. "Your legal grievances have been duly noted and summarily dismissed under the ancient statutes of the commons. She is a guest of the house. You will treat her with diplomatic courtesy, or I will personally relocate your pine-cone drying racks to the north swamp."

Scar froze mid-chitter. His head cocked ninety degrees to the left, his whiskers twitching frantically as he glared from Mac to Maya.

Maya, meanwhile, had frozen completely still.

Her hands remained locked around the maple grips of the crutches, her eyes wide as she looked between the tiny squirrel on the rail and the enormous man leaning against the post.

"Mac..." she whispered slowly, her voice laced with utter bewilderment. "Did you... were you just having an actual argument with a squirrel?"

"I was," Mac nodded. "Though 'argument' is perhaps too generous a term. It was more of an administrative injunction against a resident rodent who suffers from an acute case of territorial megalomania."

Maya stared at him, her eyebrows climbing toward her hairline. "You can understand him. Not just... like, reading his tail. You actually understand what he's saying."

"I do," Mac said. "And he understands me."

"How?" she asked, her voice dropping into a breathless hush. "Is that part of... the Kinap thing?"

"Partly," Mac replied, stepping closer to the rail. "My physical strength—the ability to move granite and bend iron—is an inborn trait of my blood. It cannot be taught, and it cannot be learned. An ordinary human attempting to lift an eight-hundred-pound hemlock log will simply snap their lumbar spine."

He paused, looking down at her with gentle, serious blue eyes.

"But the tongue of the wild, Maya? The communication with the furred and feathered kin? That is not magic. That is an ancient human discipline. It is the art of shedding deceit, quieting the noisy chatter of the ego, and learning the grammar of breath, posture, scent, and intent. It can be taught. And if you have the patience to sit still and listen to the woods, I will teach it to you."

Maya tilted her head, her skepticism fighting with the impossible things she had already witnessed. "Teach it? How does someone teach you to talk to a squirrel?"

"First, by understanding how animals think," Mac explained, pulling up his ash stool. "Animals do not process the world through English nouns, verb tenses, or Latin taxonomic classifications. They think in composite sensory holograms. When Scar speaks, he projects a simultaneous braid of sensory data: the physical scent of drying resin, the thermal state of the air, the vibration of footsteps on the porch planks, and an intense, laser-focused intention regarding territory and calories. To hear them, you do not listen with your ears. You listen with the quiet center behind your breastbone."

He leaned toward her, his voice softening into a low, instructional cadence.

"Close your eyes, Maya."

She hesitated, then slowly lowered her eyelids.

"Take a slow breath through your nose," Mac guided her. "Fill your belly, not your chest. Let your shoulders drop. Now... stop the internal monologue. Stop asking yourself what you are going to do tomorrow, stop replaying the fight at the culvert, stop labeling the objects around you. Just feel the physical presence of the life sitting four feet from your right knee."

Maya drew a long, deep breath.

The silence of the courtyard settled over her. At first, there was only the hum of her own pulse and the faint ringing in her ears.

Then, gradually, the background noise began to separate into layers: the cool rustle of the hawthorn leaves in the west breeze; the deep, mineral warmth radiating from the stone chimney behind her; the sharp, resinous perfume of the balsam fir above... and then, sitting on the crushed shells four feet away, a distinct, warm, rhythmic pressure.

It was Nipn.

She didn't see the wolf with her eyes, but in the dark behind her eyelids, she felt a presence like a heavy, polished boulder resting in a cool mountain stream—calm, watchful, completely devoid of nervousness, radiating an aura of steady, unshakeable guardianship.

Beside her knee, Nipn let out a soft, warm breath through his nostrils.

Maya felt a sudden, clear impression wash across her mind: the physical image of a dry cave, a warm hearth fire, and a quiet, approving nod.

Maya’s eyes snapped open.

She looked down at Nipn. The silver wolf was looking directly up into her face, his amber eyes clear, bright, and calm.

Her breath caught in her throat. Her hand flew to her collarbone.

"I felt him," she breathed, her voice trembling with awe. "I didn't hear words... but I felt him say that the cave was dry. That I was safe."

Mac smiled—a slow, deep, proud smile that crinkled the corners of his weathered eyes.

"You have good ears, Maya," Mac whispered. "You listened with the bone, not with the tongue. That is the first lesson of *Netukulimk*. The wild kin are always speaking; humanity simply forgot how to turn down the volume of its own machines."

On the porch rail, Scar had watched this entire exchange with intense, fidgety suspicion.

Seeing that he was no longer the center of attention, the tiny red menace hopped twelve inches closer along the cedar rail, stopping directly beside Maya’s left shoulder.

His black eyes swept over her clothing: the thick red-and-black wool lumberjack jacket, the soft green flannel shirt, and the fleece-lined winter vest she wore unzipped over her chest.

Scar’s tail gave three slow, rhythmic twitches.

A sudden, overwhelming sensation of architectural covetousness blossomed in his mind, radiating outward with hilarious, uninhibited clarity.

*Soft,* Scar’s inner voice projected, the mental image glowing with pure luxury. *Deep gray wool. Warm like the under-belly of a snow goose. Pockets. Cavities. Heated hollows between the flannel. A ten-star winter drey. Better than dry hemlock feathers. Better than hollow birch bark.*

He tilted his head, staring with laser-like focus directly at the spacious, fleece-lined expanse beneath Maya’s flannel collar—measuring the cubic volume of her bust with the clinical eye of a master carpenter inspecting a heated penthouse suite.

Maya caught the squirrel’s gaze, looking down at her own chest, then back at the squirrel.

"Why is he looking at my shirt like that?" Maya asked, narrowing her eyes with sudden suspicion. "He looks like an eviction lawyer planning a condominium development."

Mac pressed his lips together, suppressing a powerful urge to laugh.

"He’s admiring your textile inventory, Maya," Mac said with complete deadpan solemnity. "Acadian red squirrels are connoisseurs of high-thermal nesting materials. I would advise keeping your jacket zipped when he is in the immediate vicinity, unless you wish to find three dried butternuts and an ounce of cedar bark tucked into your brassiere."

Maya gave a sharp, incredulous bark of laughter, swatting the air two inches from the squirrel’s nose.

"Get lost, you little red pervert!" she grinned. "This flannel is occupied!"

Scar gave a haughty, indignant *chip!*, flicked his tail violently, and scampered up the corner post to the eaves in three vertical bounds, turning around to peer down from the roof shingles with his paws folded over his chest like a disgruntled landlord waiting for the rent.

### Part VI: First Steps on Ash & The Perimeter Tour

"Right," Maya said, her smile lingering as she gripped the maple handles of her new crutches. "Enough rodent diplomacy. Let's see if I can stand without humiliating myself."

"Take your time," Mac said, stepping off the porch to stand on the crushed-shell path below, his arms loose at his sides, ready to support her without crowding her space.

Maya adjusted the buckskin cradles beneath her axillae.

The fit was uncanny. The curved wooden cradles, cushioned by the boiled sheep’s wool and soft deer hide, pressed against her thoracic ribs without pinching the delicate nerves in her armpits. The maple hand-grips sat at the exact natural angle of her palms, allowing her forearms to bear eighty percent of the load with her wrists straight and locked.

She pushed down with her palms.

Her strong shoulders engaged. She lifted herself off the cedar bench, bringing her sound right foot down to the porch planks, keeping her splinted left leg swinging free four inches above the floor.

She paused, testing her balance.

There was no wobble. The white ash shafts flexed beneath her weight—absorbing the initial kinetic surge like a pair of high-tensile organic leaf springs—and then held firm, solid as iron pillars.

"Well," Maya breathed, her face lighting up with fierce, radiant satisfaction. "Look at that. I'm vertical."

"The center of gravity is precisely over the right calcaneus," Mac noted, nodding with quiet approval. "Now, advance the rubber tips eighteen inches forward, plant them simultaneously, and swing the right foot through the gap. Treat the crutches as a pair of supplementary forelegs. You are temporarily a quadruped, Maya. Emulate the wolf, not the flamingo."

From the bottom of the steps, Nipn looked up, his tail giving a single, approving *thump* against the crushed clam shells.

Maya took her first step.

The rubber-capped tips of the crutches touched the cedar porch planks with a soft, muted *thud*. She leaned her weight onto her palms, pushed downward, and swung her hips forward in a smooth, controlled arc, planting her right boot squarely on the top step.

*Thump-swing-step.*

She navigated the three shallow porch steps with the fluid, determined athleticism of a woman who refused to let an injury keep her grounded.

When her boots hit the crushed-shell path of the courtyard, she stopped.

The white shells crunched softly beneath her rubber ferrules. The sun was warm on the back of her neck, the air sweet with wild thyme.

Beside her right knee, Nipn fell into step.

The wolf didn't crowd her; he matched his stride to her swinging, three-legged cadence, his broad silver shoulder maintaining an exact twelve inches of clearance from her right hip, his amber eyes scanning the tree line ahead like an escort frigate guarding a flagship.

"He's walking with me," Maya murmured, watching the wolf’s silent, liquid pace.

"He respects resilience," Mac said, walking at her left shoulder. "In a wolf pack, an injured hunter who whines and crawls is a liability; an injured hunter who stands on three legs and keeps moving forward is an asset. Nipn has already registered you as a member of the hunting party."

Maya looked down at the wolf, her eyes softening. "Good dog."

Nipn gave a soft, dry snort that clearly communicated: *Wolf, not dog. But you walk well on the wooden sticks.*

They walked the courtyard in a wide, slow circle:

Past the rainwater cistern—a colossal four-hundred-gallon oak vat bound with iron hoops that captured the pristine runoff from the cabin roof, fitted with a gravity-fed sand-and-charcoal filtration barrel.

Past the raised garden beds—terraced into the south-facing sandstone shelf, rich with black composted seaweed and river silt, where emerald rows of heritage sweet peas, climbing pole beans, winter squash, and dark green flint corn were thrusting up through the mulch.

Past the outdoor stone forge—where the Peter Wright anvil stood dark, polished, and ready, smelling of old charcoal and tempered steel.

"Come around to the north slope," Mac suggested, gently matching her swinging pace. "Before you see the perimeter, you should understand the pantry. A fortress without food is merely an expensive tomb."

He guided her along a smooth, packed earth path that skirted the leeward side of the cabin, passing beneath the deep, cool shade of virgin hemlocks and towering yellow birches. The air cooled noticeably, dropping ten degrees into that humid, emerald quiet where the sun never directly touched the forest floor.

Set squarely into the natural sandstone terrace of the hill stood a low, arched portal that looked like the entrance to an ancient Celtic barrow.

Maya stopped, leaning on her crutches, her eyes widening.

"What is this?" she asked. "A bomb shelter?"

"A root cellar," Mac replied, stepping to the heavy tamarack door. "Though the distinction is largely academic. Both are designed to preserve organic life against external thermodynamic collapse. Modern preppers spent fifty thousand dollars burying corrugated steel shipping containers that rusted into toxic tetanus traps in five years. This was built of native granite, split cedar, and glacial till. It will be here when the next ice sheet retreats."

Mac lifted the counterweighted forged iron latch. The thick, four-inch door—insulated with sheep's wool and cattail down—swung outward with a soft, hydraulic sigh of displacing air.

A wave of subterranean cool washed over Maya’s face: clean, forty-one-degree air smelling of limestone, river sand, cured birch, and the faint, sweet trace of wild apples.

"Step inside," Mac said, holding the door steady. "Watch the stone sill. Three shallow steps."

Maya swung forward on her crutches, the rubber ferrules gripping the dry granite steps with sure, silent traction.

When she reached the floor of crushed beach gravel, she looked around the eighteen-foot vaulted chamber in stunned silence.

The walls, recently coated in fresh, brilliant white lime-wash, glowed with soft, diffused light from the open door. Along the north wall, bins of rough-sawn white pine held bushels of firm, purple-and-brown seed potatoes buried in clean river sand. On the center benches stood two dozen glazed stoneware crocks sealed with heavy stone weights, holding fermented sauerkraut, salt pork packed in leaf lard, and brined herring. And on the upper cedar shelves, rows of glass jars caught the light—golden cloudberry preserves, wild blueberry compote, and clover honey.

Maya, who had grown up on an Acadian farm and spent five months starving on the road, ran her eyes across the bins with the ferocious, calculating assessment of a veteran quartermaster.

"Kennebec and Chieftain potatoes," she murmured, her voice hushed with reverence. "Dry sand storage... zero sprouting. Two-pipe passive thermal siphon in the ceiling... the air is completely circulating without a fan. And the crocks are sealed with lard caps."

She turned to Mac, her face pale with pure, unadulterated awe.

"Mac... do you have any idea what this room represents?"

"Approximately four million calories of shelf-stable carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins," Mac replied with mild precision. "Plus adequate ascorbic acid to prevent scorbutic degeneration of the vascular endothelium."

"It's life," Maya corrected, her voice cracking slightly with emotion. "This isn't survival, Mac. This is security. Two people could live out of this vault through two solid years of crop failure and never lose an ounce of body fat. Out on the highway, people were murdering each other over half a can of dog food... and you've got twenty bushels of seed potatoes sleeping in clean sand."

"The earth provides the dividend, Maya, if you don't default on the principal," Mac said softly. "The Acadians farmed this valley for a hundred and fifty years without electricity or chemical fertilizers. They understood that the pantry is built in July, not in November."

He guided her back out into the hemlock shade, letting the heavy tamarack door swing shut with that solid, airtight *thunk*.

As they rounded the northern ridge, Mac pointed up through the spruce canopy toward the high headland half a mile to the south.

There, rising sixty feet above the coastal forest, stood the white granite octagon of the Belliveau Head lighthouse, its cast-iron lantern gallery gleaming in the afternoon sun against the sapphire bowl of Shepody Bay.

Maya shielded her eyes with one hand, looking up at the tower.

"You own that too?" she asked.

"Deeded under the Crown in 1884," Mac said. "Bought it when the Coast Guard decommissioned it. The first tier has a fully appointed keeper's apartment—four-foot-thick granite blocks, a 1912 Enterprise Monarch cookstove, and a soapstone thermal mass that laughs at seventy-knot gales. And the spiral staircase inside has seventy-four stone steps lined with custom timber shelves holding six thousand volumes of human history, science, and literature."

Maya stared at the tower, then back at the low cabin in the clearing.

"Why have the cabin if you have a stone fortress?"

"Rhythm, Maya," Mac explained, his voice warm and steady. "The cabin is our summer harbor—close to the garden beds, the bog boardwalk, the forge, and the fish weirs. But when November arrives on the Bay of Fundy, the weather ceases to be a climate and becomes an artillery barrage. Sleet, freezing spray, and river ice jams that can tear the banks clean. When the Great Freeze comes, we close the cabin, pack our winter stores, and retreat to the lighthouse apartment. Up there, winter is merely an intellectual spectator sport."

Maya let out a long, slow breath, looking from the tower down to the sturdy ash crutches beneath her hands.

"You planned for everything," she whispered. "Every season. Every storm."

"Not everything," Mac murmured with a faint, self-amused smile, looking down at her. "I hadn't planned on an injured Acadian with a sharp tongue and a stubborn refusal to stay in bed. But the house adapts."

They walked back to the front courtyard.

And finally, to the living-wood palisade.

Maya stopped six feet from the eight-foot woven barrier, leaning on her ash crutches, staring in pure, breathless astonishment.

Up close, the living fence was an architectural wonder.

The braided trunks of black willow, coastal cedar, and hawthorn had fused into an unbroken, diamond-lattice wall four inches thick. Thorny spurs bristled through every gap like a forest of bayonets. At the base, the trunks were anchored into the sandstone bedrock with roots as thick as a man's thigh, while the green canopy overhead was alive with hundreds of glossy leaves drinking the sunlight and photosynthesizing fresh defensive bark.

"They're... they're completely grafted," Maya whispered, reaching out with one hand to touch the smooth, pale green bark where a willow trunk had fused seamlessly into a cedar stem. "They share the same sap."

"They do," Mac said, resting his hand beside hers. "The willow provides the rapid growth, deep moisture-seeking roots, and flexible tensile strength. The cedar provides the natural insect-repelling tannins and rot resistance. And the hawthorn provides the perimeter deterrence. No raider with a chainsaw could cut through two hundred yards of living, water-saturated green wood before the chain jammed with pitch. And no fire could burn it; the sap pressure is too high."

He walked two paces to the heavy tamarack gate.

He showed her the secret knothole in the outer plank, the counterweighted hornbeam rocker latch, and the balanced stone ball-and-socket pivot.

With the pressure of a single finger, Mac pushed the six-hundred-pound timber gate open three inches, then let it swing shut. It closed with a quiet, solid, satisfying *thunk* that echoed through the forest.

Maya leaned against her ash crutches, looking out through the narrow gap in the gate toward the vast, empty wilderness beyond.

"Out there," she said softly, her voice heavy with the memory of the road, "it's wolves eating wolves. People stealing canned corn and killing each other for half a tank of stale gasoline."

She turned back to face the courtyard, looking at the low cedar cabin, the smoking chimney mass, the garden beds, the silent wolf, and the quiet giant who had built it all with his bare hands.

"And in here... it's a hundred years ago. It’s an honest life."

"It is an old life, Maya," Mac corrected gently. "Not a hundred years ago—ten thousand years ago. The modern world was a temporary detour into petroleum-fueled arrogance. The earth was always going to reclaim the ledger. All we did here was make sure the house was ready when the bill came due."

### Part VII: The Evening Skillet & The New Rhythm

By twilight, the wind had veered into the northwest, carrying the cool, clean chill of the Acadian highlands.

Inside the cabin, the central hearth was humming.

The four-ton masonry thermal mass had absorbed the modest heat of the afternoon maple coals, radiating a steady, unshakeable seventy-two degrees of mineral warmth that filled the room with the dry comfort of sun-baked stone.

Maya sat at the heavy yellow-birch table in a wide ash armchair, her splinted left leg propped comfortably on a low wooden footstool padded with an old wool sweater.

She was not idle.

Her hands—brown, capable, scarred at the knuckles from five months of frontier survival—were moving with rhythmic, practiced speed, shelling fresh green garden peas from an ash basket into a shallow wooden bowl.

*SNAP. ZIP. PLINK-PLINK-PLINK.*

Beside the bowl lay a clean maple cutting board where she had already sliced four ounces of dry, salt-cured pork belly into paper-thin lardoons and minced a handful of wild garlic bulbs gathered from the spring house.

"Don't waste the pea pods, Maya," Mac called from the cooktop, his broad back turned as he stoked the secondary firebox with split sticks of dry birch. "They go into the iron stockpot for tomorrow’s vegetable broth. High in soluble fiber and sweet sugars."

"I grew up on an Acadian farm near Shediac, Mac," Maya retorted with a dry, affectionate smirk, flicking a hollow green pod into the stockpot with flick-knife accuracy. "I was shelling peas and gutting mackerel before I had my adult teeth. You don't need to lecture me on agricultural parsimony."

Mac chuckled softly, reaching for his Griswold No. 8 cast-iron skillet hanging on the wall.

"A woman of classical rural virtues," Mac said, setting the skillet on the hot iron plate. "Columella would have written a third volume of *De Re Rustica* in your honor."

"Stop quoting dead Romans while you're cooking," she laughed, shaking her head. "It's unnatural. Just get that salt pork sizzling."

Mac dropped the lardoons into the skillet.

*SHHHHHHHHH.*

The rich, mouth-watering perfume of melting pork lard and cured sea salt billowed into the cabin, instantly setting Nipn’s tail thumping softly against his braided rug in the corner.

Working with the unhurried economy of a man who loved his kitchen, Mac added the shelled peas and minced wild garlic to the bubbling fat, tossing them with a curved maple spatula until the peas turned a brilliant, translucent jade-green, blistered at the edges with savory pork cracklings.

Then, he slid four immaculate fillets of winter flounder into the skillet—dusted lightly with cattail starch, coarse gray Fundy salt, and crushed dried juniper berries.

The fish seared to a golden, flaky crisp in two minutes.

Mac plated the entire meal onto two broad pewter trench-plates: steaming, golden-crusted flounder fillets resting atop mounds of sweet, sizzling peas and crispy pork cracklings, garnished with a spoonful of cold, tart cloudberry jam and two warm slices of toasted sourdough bread baked in the brick oven.

He set one plate before Maya, pulling up his three-legged stool opposite her.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

The single beeswax candle on the table flickered with a tall, steady golden flame. Outside, the twilight was deepening into a rich, velvety indigo, the stars beginning to puncture the clear Fundy sky.

Maya picked up her bone-handled fork, looking down at the steaming plate.

Her eyes were suspiciously bright in the candlelight.

"I haven't eaten a hot meal at a real table with another human being," she said quietly, her voice catching slightly in her throat, "in one hundred and forty-two days."

Mac bowed his head slightly.

"*Netukulimk*," he whispered. "May the harvest nourish the bone, and may the hearth welcome the traveler."

They ate in that deep, comfortable silence that only comes when the food is honest and the company is true.

The flounder was extraordinary: the delicate, sweet white meat flaked beneath the fork, rich with the piney snap of juniper and the mineral depth of the gray salt, balanced perfectly by the sweet, popping crunch of the garden peas and the tart citrus burst of the amber cloudberries.

Maya closed her eyes on the third bite, letting out a long, quiet breath that sounded like tension unwinding from an overtightened guitar string.

"My grandmother used to make flounder like this," she said softly, looking down at the blistered peas. "In Barachois. She’d fry the fillets in salt pork grease, but she didn't have cloudberries. She’d use wild gooseberries from the salt marsh ditch. She used to say that if you knew how to read the tide, you’d never owe a nickel to the grocery store in Shediac."

"Your grandmother was an epistemological realist, Maya," Mac said, chewing slowly. "The Acadians understood that the salt marshes were not wastelands to be drained and paved with asphalt. They were tidal breadbaskets. Did she ever speak of the *aboiteaux*?"

Maya looked up, surprised. "The wooden clapper gates in the dykes? Of course. My great-uncle helped maintain the old aboiteau at Memramcook before the province built the modern concrete causeway that choked the river with silt. Why?"

"Because up in the lighthouse tower, on the thirty-sixth stone step, I have a calfskin journal written in 1734 by a man named Jean-Baptiste Belliveau," Mac smiled. "He was the master-dyker who engineered the first tidal gates on this very ridge. He spent three pages detailing the exact angle to hang the wooden clapper on a tamarack hinge so the forty-foot Fundy tide would slam it shut, while the fresh hillside brook would push it open when the brine retreated."

Maya stared at him across the candle flame, her fork resting against the rim of her plate.

"You have his actual journal? In his own handwriting?"

"In iron-gall ink on rag-cotton paper," Mac said. "Written while King Louis the Fifteenth was sitting on the throne of France. Three hundred years old, and you can still read every stroke. That's why I spent forty years rescuing books from institutional recycling bins, Maya. When the power went out, three billion people lost their entire culture because they’d uploaded it to a digital cloud that required sixty thousand coal-fired power plants to stay visible. The Acadians didn't have a cloud. They had ink, paper, tamarack trunks, and the memory of their elders."

Maya looked down at the sturdy hemlock table, then out the window toward the dark silhouette of the headland where the lighthouse stood against the stars.

"People in the city used to say that books were obsolete," she murmured, a faint trace of bitterness in her voice. "They laughed at people who kept physical libraries. Said it was dead tree waste. Then the transformers blew, the cell towers died, and within a week, nobody knew how to purify water without a plastic filter, or how to identify wild leeks from false hellebore. We were a species of geniuses who couldn't survive forty-eight hours without an electrical socket."

"Arrogance is always penalized by thermodynamics, Maya," Mac said gently. "The universe does not negotiate with hubris."

Nipn did not beg. He waited until Mac finished his first fillet, at which point Mac stood up, walked to the corner, and set a clean earthenware bowl on the floor containing two crispy flounder tails and a generous dollop of rendered pork fat.

The wolf lapped the offering clean with three rhythmic sweeps of his tongue, gave Mac a slow, dignified nod of canine gratitude, and settled back onto his rug to sleep.

When the trench-plates were clean, Mac cleared the table, wiping the birch wood with a damp linen cloth. He returned from the shelves with a small, leather-bound volume: an 1894 edition of Thomas Hardy’s *Far from the Madding Crowd*.

He set the book beside Maya’s elbow, along with a fresh mug of steaming chamomile tea.

"Your evening assignment, invalid," Mac said with a gentle, self-amused smile. "Chapter one through four. It will remind you that human foolishness was thoroughly established long before the electrical grid failed."

Maya ran her rough, calloused fingers across the embossed gold lettering of the calfskin spine.

She looked up at the quiet giant standing before the hearth, at the wolf sleeping peacefully across her threshold, and at the solid hemlock beams holding the quiet night above their heads.

The road was gone. The terror was behind the living wall.

She opened the book, took a sip of hot tea, and smiled.

"Goodnight, Mac," she whispered.

"Goodnight, Maya," he replied softly. "Sleep deep. The house is keeping watch."

Mac stepped softly across the room, pulling the heavy cedar bedroom door to within two inches of the jamb, leaving just enough gap for the warm air from the masonry heater to circulate across her cot.

Outside in the common room, he banked the firebox with three splits of dense, slow-burning yellow birch, adjusting the air draft until the flue whispered with a low, contented murmur. 

He walked to the window and looked out into the moonlit courtyard.

High above on the porch rafter, curled into an immaculate ball of copper fur tucked between two dry cedar shingles, Scar the red squirrel was sound asleep, his twitching nose buried under his flaming tail, dreaming of warm flannel, wool pockets, and mountains of cracked butternuts.

At the threshold of the bedroom door, Nipn had already settled into his night watch, his massive gray paws crossed, his breath steady and deep.

Mac walked to his workbench, picking up a piece of fine sharkskin to buff the smooth curve of the spare ash hand-grip.

The work was quiet. The night was vast. Across the red river, thirty miles of dark salt water were rising with the midnight tide, washing clean the mud flats of the Petkotkweyak.

For ten years, this homestead had been a sanctuary of silence—a place where a lone Kinap could listen to the earth and read his books in monastic isolation.

Now, there were two trench-plates on the table. There was laughter in the kitchen. There was a wounded traveler healing on clean oat straw, and a new voice answering the ancient rhythm of the land.

Mac laid down the polishing skin, blew out the remaining beeswax candle, and smiled into the warm, fragrant dark.

The hearth was alight. The timber was sound. And tomorrow, there would be work to do.
