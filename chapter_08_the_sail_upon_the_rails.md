# Chapter 8: The Sail upon the Rails and the Ridge of Fort Folly

---

### Part I: Dawn on the Red Siding

The morning smelled of drying blood, ozone, and cold ocean silt.

At first light, the fog clinging to the Petitcodiac was not a gentle mist; it was a freezing, damp nor'easter draft that bit through wool like needles, carrying the harsh, iron stench of twenty square miles of exposed red mud flats.

Up on the gravel embankment of the railway spur, Maya stood beside the draisine handcar, her hands wrapped in fingerless moose-hide work gloves, leaning her weight firmly on her white ash staff.

Her left ankle, wrapped tight in clean linen and braced with split-cedar splints beneath her heavy work boot, throbbed with a dull, steady ache from yesterday's collision against the steel boxcar. Her throat was ringed with mottled dark-purple contusions where the dead raider's calloused thumbs had tried to crush her windpipe.

She didn't complain. She didn't ask for a seat.

She stood in the cold wind, watching a pair of northern ravens circle over the black spruce canopy a mile up the track, where Cody’s blood still stained the sharp granite ballast at Stony Creek.

In a brutal world, survival was not granted by pity; it was seized with iron, calluses, and an absolute refusal to break.

Between her breasts, nestled deep inside the unbuttoned V-neck of her green flannel work shirt, Scar was wide awake.

The red squirrel had spent twenty minutes methodically grooming his whiskers, cleaning the dried grease and shotgunner ear-wax from his claws, and inspecting his command bridge. He sat upright on the fleece bridge of her brassiere, his cream-colored front paws resting against the warm bronze skin of her collarbone, his black eyes surveying the railway tracks with haughty, predatory satisfaction.

Every time Maya breathed in the sharp, salty air, the squirrel rose two inches on the swell of her chest; every time she shifted her grip on the ash staff, he dug his tiny hind claws comfortably into her bra cup to maintain his balance.

*The road is quiet,* Scar projected, his telepathic voice sharp, crisp, and devoid of fear. *The cold-smoke apes are gone. The crows are eating their leavings. Proceed to high speed!*

"Hold your horses, fuzzball," Maya muttered aloud, her voice raspy from her bruised vocal cords. "Mac is tying down the freight."

At the front of the handcar, Mac was securing the cargo with braided hemp lashings, cinching heavy oiled canvas tarpaulins over the wooden firkins and seed crates.

He was wearing his heavy gray wool mackinaw coat over his homespun linen shirt, his thick leather boots planted wide across the tamarack deck. His knuckles were raw, the skin over his right fist bruised dark yellow from where he had shattered Cody's forearm and pulverized the stocky raider's carpal bones twenty-four hours earlier.

He pulled the final trucker’s hitch tight, testing the hemp line with a single, massive tug that twanged like a bowstring.

"Seventy pounds of cold-smoked gaspereau," Mac rumbled, ticking off the manifest in his deep, gravelly baritone. "Two thirty-pound tubs of coarse solar salt from the sandstone pans. Four gallons of high-test gasoline siphoned from the raider's Honda. And Auntie Elizabeth’s heritage squash and bean seeds."

He looked back across the tamarack deck at Maya, his pale blue eyes taking in her battered throat, her steady grip on the ash staff, and the twitching red head perched in the warm fleece of her shirt.

There was zero patronizing sympathy in his face. There was only the calm, unshakeable respect of one veteran survivor looking at another.

"How does the bone feel, sister?" Mac asked bluntly.

"It feels like it got kicked by a mule," Maya answered, her jaw setting hard. "And it feels like it can hold my weight for sixty miles of iron track. Let’s ride."

Mac nodded once—a sharp, approving jerk of his bearded chin.

"Climb aboard," he said.

Maya stepped up onto the low tamarack platform.

She didn't need to be carried like an invalid. Planting her sound right boot on the iron step, she hauled her body weight upward, using her white ash cane to stabilize her balance as she swung onto the deck.

She took her station at the rear of the handcar, right beside the vertical boiled-maple brake lever that controlled the four friction shoes clamped above the chilled-iron wheels.

Mac reached down to the ballast, whistling a low, two-note call through his teeth.

From the edge of the red spruce thicket, Nipn emerged in a silent, silver blur.

The wolf didn't bound clumsily onto the handcar. He leaped six feet through the air in a single, effortless arc, his broad paws landing on the forward cargo tarpaulins without making a sound louder than a dropped glove. He turned twice on the canvas, curled his long, muscular body around the mast thwart, and rested his chin on his front paws, his amber eyes fixed intently down the long, straight iron corridor to the south.

Mac stepped onto the center deck, standing before the heavy white ash walking beam of the 3:1 compound overdrive transmission.

He reached up, untying the braided halyard that held the furled ripstop canvas sail along the fourteen-foot white spruce spar.

"Ready on the brakes, Maya?" Mac called out, his hands gripping the yardarm.

Maya gripped the polished rock-maple lever with both gloved hands, pulling it backward to hold the car steady on the slight three-percent siding grade.

"Brakes locked!" she called back.

"Loose the sheet!"

Mac hauled on the halyard with a single, powerful surge of his shoulders.

The white canvas sail—twenty square yards of military-grade ripstop nylon salvaged from the fallen weather balloon—slid up the spruce mast with a loud, whistling *SHRRRRRP*.

For two seconds, the canvas hung limp in the morning mist.

Then, rolling off the open expanse of Shepody Bay, the morning Fundy gale hit the promontory.

### Part II: The Sail Unfurled & The 25-mph Flight

*CRACK!*

The nylon canvas filled with the sudden, violent force of a cannon shot.

The sail rounded into a taut, rigid white belly, straining against the braided hemp sheets with five hundred pounds of raw aerodynamic thrust.

The heavy white ash spar groaned in its iron thwart socket. The four-hundred-pound handcar shuddered on its chilled-iron axles, the wheels straining against the wooden brake shoes with a high-pitched, complaining squeak.

"DROP THE BRAKES!" Mac roared over the rising wind.

Maya threw her weight forward, slamming the maple lever into the forward notch.

The friction shoes released their grip on the twenty-inch wheel treads.

Instantly, the draisine lunged.

There was no gradual, sluggish acceleration, no slow, grinding heave of heavy machinery. With forty pounds of fresh EP-2 lithium-moly grease packed into the bronze sleeve bearings and a twenty-knot sea gale driving the sail, the handcar shot forward along the spur siding like an arrow released from a longbow.

*CLACK-CLACK. CLACK-CLACK.*

The chilled-iron wheel flanges cleared the frog of the turnout switch, slamming onto the heavy, ninety-pound continuously welded steel rails of the main CN line.

The sound of the machine changed instantly.

Off the rough siding, on the glass-smooth, uninterrupted surface of the main track, the rolling resistance dropped to near zero. The wheels did not rumble; they hissed.

A high-pitched, metallic, silver whine rose from the chilled-iron treads as they met the polished crown of the rail—a terrifying, whispering song of pure mechanical velocity that vibrated directly through the tamarack deck planks and into the soles of Maya's boots.

Ten miles an hour.

Fifteen miles an hour.

Twenty miles an hour.

The cold salt air whipped across the deck with gale force, tearing the last remnants of river mist into flying white ribbons.

Maya braced her boots wide against the deck cleats, her right hand clamped around the brake lever, her left hand gripping the structural ash stanchion. The wind caught her dark hair, pulling loose strands from her braid and whipping them across her flushed, bronze cheeks.

Between her breasts, Scar felt the sudden, violent onset of aerodynamic shear.

The squirrel didn't just tuck down; he flattened himself into a low-drag, aerodynamic wedge in the hollow of her cleavage, pressing his chin against her breastbone, his black whiskers swept back ninety degrees against his cheeks by the twenty-five-mile-an-hour headwind. His tufted red ears were pinned flat against his skull, but his black eyes were wide, bulging with frantic, uninhibited, adrenaline-soaked ecstasy.

*THE WIND-SHIP FLIES!* Scar’s voice shrieked through Maya's consciousness, vibrating her ribs like an electric current. *NO LEGS! NO RUNNING! THE IRON SNAKE CARRIES THE DUPLEX THROUGH THE SKY! SPEED! MORE SPEED!*

"He's loving it!" Maya yelled to Mac over the roaring wind.

At the center of the deck, Mac stood tall, his heavy mackinaw billowing behind him like a sea-captain's cloak, holding the mainsheet in his left hand while his right hand rested on the ash rocker handle of the walking beam.

"WE HAVEN'T EVEN ENGAGED THE GEARS YET!" Mac bellowed back, his white teeth flashing in his gray-streaked beard. "TAKE HER UP TO CRUISE!"

Mac bent his knees, gripped the broad ash cross-bar with both massive hands, and pushed downward.

*CLIIIIICK-SNAP.*

The sprag freewheel clutch on the countershaft locked home with a solid, metallic bite.

The motorcycle roller chain flashed around the sprockets in a greasy, black blur. The 3:1 compound overdrive engaged, multiplying Mac’s rhythmic downward strokes into terrifying rotational torque at the 5160 rear axle.

The draisine didn't just maintain speed; it accelerated.

Twenty-five miles an hour. Twenty-eight miles an hour.

The Acadian forest on either side of the track blurred into a continuous, streaking wall of dark green black spruce, pale yellow birch, and gray alder thickets.

Beside the track, Nipn was running.

The great silver wolf was not lagging behind. He had leaped down from the cargo deck as they hit the main line, his long, muscular legs opening up into that effortless, ground-eating predatory lope that wolves had used to chase caribou across the tundra for twenty thousand years.

His silver-tipped paws skimmed over the crushed-granite ballast; his tail was level with his spine; his tongue was out, tasting the wind. Whenever the track crossed an open gravel cut, he matched the handcar stride for stride, his golden eyes locked on Maya, running with that wild, joyful, terrifying economy of motion that made thirty miles an hour look like play.

Maya looked down at the track ahead.

The railway followed the high sandstone bluff fifty feet above the western shore of Shepody Bay.

Below them to the east, the world opened up in a staggering, breathtaking vista of raw maritime wilderness:

Thirty square miles of chocolate-red mud flats stretched out toward the distant blue ridges of Cumberland County, Nova Scotia. The tide was turning at the mouth of the bay, sending a long, foaming white line of Atlantic salt water roaring up the deep channels, flooding the marsh creeks, lifting the red silt into boiling froth.

Above the mud, flocks of thousands of semipalmated sandpipers and black-bellied plovers wheeled through the air like clouds of swirling silver dust, dipping and banking in perfect synchronization against the morning sun.

Maya threw her head back, letting out a loud, wild, ragged whoop of pure frontier joy that was swallowed instantly by the roaring wind.

For five months, she had crawled through ditches, hid in rotting culverts, slept in freezing mud, and fled like a hunted beast from men who wanted to turn her into cattle.

Now, she was flying across the earth at thirty miles an hour on a machine she had helped forge from salvage scrap, backed by an ancient Kinap giant, guarded by a silver wolf, with an eight-ounce squirrel riding her bosom like a warrior king.

She was not a victim. She was not a refugee.

She was sovereign.

### Part III: The Desolation of the Lower Estuary

Five miles south of the homestead, the railway line dipped into the low-lying marshes of the Memramcook estuary, and the grim, unvarnished reality of the fallen world closed in around them.

The speeder cleared a long, sweeping curve through a cutting of red shale, and Mac ease off the walking beam, letting the freewheel ratchet hum with that steady, rhythmic *click-click-click-click* as they coasted onto the long iron trestle spanning the **Belliveau Creek marsh**.

"Drop the sail half-mast, Maya," Mac commanded quietly, his tone dropping from the exhilaration of speed to the hard, vigilant cadence of a scout entering hostile terrain. "Check the friction shoes. We take the flats at ten miles an hour."

Maya hauled back on the brake lever, the boiled-maple blocks groaning as they kissed the chilled-iron wheels, bleeding off momentum until the handcar was rolling at a steady, cautious twelve miles an hour.

Nipn bounded back up onto the forward deck, his silver fur bristling along his spine, his ears swiveling to track the silence of the reed beds.

Between her breasts, Scar felt the sudden tension in Maya’s posture.

The squirrel’s head sank lower between her cleavage, his whiskers twitching as his black nose sampled the foul, stagnant air drifting off the lowlands.

*Old death,* Scar transmitted, the thought cold, sharp, and sour as copper. *The bad two-legs died here. Their iron is rotting.*

The scene that spread out on either side of the railway causeway was a graveyard of twentieth-century industrial fragility.

When the global power grid failed and the diesel pipelines across the Tantramar marsh went dry six years earlier, this low-lying coastal corridor had become a bottleneck of panic and starvation for thousands of people fleeing the southern cities of Moncton and Dieppe.

Along the abandoned four-lane highway parallel to the tracks, hundreds of rusted, stripped automobile chassis sat rotting in the salt marsh hay, their tires rotted away to bare steel rims, their windshields shattered by scavengers, their interiors gutted for upholstery foam and copper wiring.

Near the mouth of the creek sat the skeletal remains of an old fish processing plant: its corrugated steel roof had collapsed inward under the weight of an unplowed winter snow, its concrete holding tanks filled with green, stagnant rainwater and rotting timber.

Resting on a dead-end spur siding twenty yards from their track sat the ruined carcass of a CN diesel-electric locomotive—a six-axle, three-thousand-horsepower beast of American iron that had run out of fuel forty miles from the Halifax shipyards.

Scavengers had stripped it like piranhas: the side panels had been torched away with oxy-acetylene rigs to harvest the copper traction motor windings; the massive twelve-cylinder engine block sat rusted into a solid mass of orange scale; and bleached human bones—the remnants of refugees who had tried to shelter inside the cab during the first sub-zero freeze of the collapse—lay scattered in the rusted gravel beneath the trucks, picked clean years ago by coyotes and crows.

Maya looked at the bones, her jaw tightening, her hand gripping the rock-maple brake lever until her knuckles turned white.

"Every highway in the province looks like this," she murmured, her voice flat, cold, and stripped of sentiment. "People think the collapse was a war. They think soldiers with rifles came through and destroyed everything. It wasn't war, Mac. It was just arithmetic."

Mac stood beside the mast, his hand resting on the ash yardarm, his pale blue eyes sweeping the marsh reeds for any sign of ambush or sniper smoke.

"Arithmetic is the only law that never forgives, Maya," Mac said softly. "The cities were built on the assumption that forty thousand diesel trucks would cross the New Brunswick border every single week, carrying food that someone else grew, fuel that someone else refined, and medicine that someone else manufactured. When the banking servers crashed and the electronic bills of lading stopped clearing, the trucks stopped rolling. Inside of ninety-six hours, a city of one hundred thousand people became a cage of starving animals."

He pointed to a group of three crude wooden grave markers—weathered driftwood crosses with no names, their hand-carved letters worn smooth by six winters of salt wind—standing on a dry gravel knoll above the marsh wrack line.

"The people who survived the first winter were not the people with the most gold in their bank accounts," Mac continued, his voice heavy with ancient, unhurried truth. "They were the people who knew that a turnip takes ninety days to mature. They were the people who knew how to split a cedar shake with a froe, how to salt a gaspereau so the botulism doesn't rot the belly, and how to hold an axe without cutting their own shins. The rest... the rest are part of the marsh now."

Maya looked down at the grease-stained deck beneath her boots.

She remembered the Sussex truck stops. She remembered the women who had traded their bodies for a half-gallon of kerosene, the men who had murdered their neighbors for a box of moldy cereal, and the warlord Thorne who had treated human beings like scrap iron to be used, broken, and discarded.

She reached down, her gloved fingers gently resting over the warm, furry head of the squirrel wedged between her breasts.

Scar raised his little face, his black nose touching her leather-gloved thumb, letting out a soft, purring whistle that vibrated directly against her heart.

*We are not the marsh-bones,* the squirrel projected, his thought fierce, bright, and stubborn as pine resin. *We have the iron speeder. We have the big biped with the sledgehammer. And the landlord has twenty-four butternuts in reserve. We live.*

Maya let out a long, hard breath, the cold knot in her stomach loosening.

"The landlord is right, Mac," Maya said, looking up at him with clear, unblinking dark eyes. "The past is dead, and the marsh can keep the bones. What lies ahead?"

Mac smiled—a slow, grim, predatory grin that held zero fear of the road.

He pointed south across the red waters of the bay toward a towering, wave-battered headland of dark red sandstone that jutted three miles out into the deep channel, crowned with a dense, ancient forest of virgin white pine and red spruce.

"Ahead is Fort Folly, sister," Mac said, hauling the white sail back up to the masthead. "High ground. Deep wells. Working forges. And people who remember how to live without machines."

He grabbed the walking beam, his massive shoulders leaning into the stroke.

*CLANK-CLANK. CLANK-CLACK.*

The 3:1 overdrive engaged with a singing roar, the chilled-iron wheels bit into the rails, and the draisine accelerated out of the dead marshes, flying south toward the sandstone bastion of the ridge.

### Part IV: The Approach to Fort Folly & The Redoubt

Seven miles south of Belliveau Creek, the railway line climbed out of the salt marshes, hugging the sheer sandstone bluffs that marked the gateway to the outer bay.

The wind here blew clean and violent off the cold expanse of Chignecto Basin, tearing at the white canvas sail, whistling through the spruce mast stays with a high-pitched, singing hum.

Ahead of them, rising three hundred feet above the crashing red surf, loomed the high promontory of **Fort Folly**—known in the ancient tongue as *Kwunsook*, the rocky point that splits the waters.

The natural geography of the headland was an engineer’s dream of defensive unassailability: vertical sandstone cliffs dropped sheer into thirty fathoms of boiling tidal current on the northern, eastern, and southern sides, while the western approach was a narrow, razor-backed ridge choked with dense virgin red spruce and glacial granite boulders.

The only land access from the northern valleys was this single line of CN railway iron, cutting through a narrow rock trench blasted sixty feet deep into the solid sandstone bedrock eighty years before the collapse.

As the draisine approached the rock cut, Mac hauled down the sail halyard, letting the canvas collapse onto the boom in a soft flutter of nylon.

"Brakes, Maya," Mac murmured, his pale blue eyes narrowing as he scanned the timber crest of the rock trench. "Dead stop before the blind corner."

Maya pulled the boiled-maple brake lever backward with a smooth, firm heave.

The oiled rock-maple blocks clamped down against the chilled-iron wheels with progressive, biting friction, bringing the four-hundred-pound handcar to a dead halt thirty yards before the entrance to the cut.

Between her breasts, Scar poked his head up from the fleece of her bra, his black eyes darting frantically toward the high cliff rims.

*Iron pointed sticks above,* the squirrel transmitted, his little body trembling with intense, feral suspicion. *Two-legs hiding in the rocks. They have the long sticks that spit lead.*

"Scar’s right," Maya whispered, leaning her hand on her white ash staff, peering up into the shadows of the overhanging spruce boughs. "There are people on the rims."

Mac didn't reach for a rifle. He stepped out onto the open tamarack deck, standing tall in the sunlight, completely exposed, his massive hands resting empty and loose at his belt.

He drew a deep, cold lungful of the salt gale.

Then, from the center of his barrel chest, Mac let out a loud, resonant, three-tone maritime fog-horn bellow that reverberated through the stone canyon like an iron ship entering harbor:

*"KWUNSOOK! KWITN AT THE GATE! COMING IN WITH TRADE AND THE GUEST OF THE HEARTH!"*

The sound rolled through the rock cut, echoing off the sandstone walls.

For ten seconds, the silence of the woods hung heavy and unbroken.

Then, sixty feet above them on the northern cliff rim, the red spruce branches parted.

A man stepped out onto a projecting sandstone ledge. He was tall, lean, and weathered dark as walnut from fifty years of sea wind, wearing an oil-tanned seal-skin vest over heavy wool, holding a pre-collapse Winchester Model 94 lever-action .30-30 rifle cradled loosely across his forearm.

The sentry peered down at the handcar, taking in the heavy ash frame, the four-hundred-pound cargo tarpaulins, the silver wolf sitting calmly beside the mast, and the dark-haired woman leaning on her white ash cane with an eight-ounce squirrel peeking out from her flannel shirt.

A wide, weathered grin split the sentry's leathery face, revealing a missing canine tooth.

"BY THE TIDE!" the sentry roared down the cut, his voice carrying the broad, rolling cadence of the Fundy shore. "LOOK AT THE RIG ON THE BIG FELLA! HE'S GOT WHEELS ON HIS BOAT NOW! CLEAR THE LINE, BOYS! KWITN HAS BROUGHT THE CARGO!"

High up the canyon, a heavy iron clatter echoed across the rock.

A massive defensive gate—constructed from eight-inch squared tamarack timbers studded with three-foot forged iron spikes and weighted with counterbalancing stone cribs—swung inward on massive, hand-forged strap hinges, opening a twelve-foot breach in the palisade.

"Release the brakes, Maya," Mac smiled, stepping back to the walking beam. "Let's roll into harbor."

Maya pushed the maple lever forward.

With three rhythmic, effortless strokes of the pump handles, the 3:1 overdrive engaged, and the draisine rolled smoothly through the rock cut, emerging onto the high plateau of Fort Folly.

Maya looked around, her jaw slack with genuine, professional admiration.

This was not a refugee camp of starving, desperate scavengers shivering in wet tents.

This was an autonomous, fortified mountain redoubt of four hundred people who had spent six years turning an ancient Mi'kmaw bluff into an unbreachable bastion of survival.

To the south, climbing the sunny slopes of the headland, lay five acres of terraced gardens: thousands of mounds of northern flint corn standing shoulder-to-shoulder with climbing pole beans and sprawling winter squash, nourished by dark compost made from fermented rockweed, herring offal, and crushed mussel shells.

Along the western ridge stood twenty hand-hewn log longhouses and cedar-shingled workshops, their stone chimneys breathing sweet blue ribbons of birch and maple smoke.

Below the cliffs, protected by a massive dry-stone breakwater built from three-ton sandstone boulders, lay the boat basin: four heavy, double-ended wooden dories and two thirty-foot coastal schooners were moored in the deep water, their tarred hemp rigging creaking gently in the swell.

And dominating the center of the settlement, built around the roaring draft of a massive double stone chimney, stood the blacksmith shop.

The air here smelled richly of burning sea-coal, hot sulfur, hammered iron, and the clean, sweet brine of drying codfish hanging from long timber flakes along the cliff edge.

As the draisine rolled to a stop on the terminal siding beside the forge, a crowd of two dozen people gathered around the track:

Hardened, lean men and women in homespun wool, oil-tanned leather, and seal-skin moccasins. Children with bright, fearless eyes and cheeks flushed red from the salt wind. Every adult carried a working tool or a weapon: forged drawknives, hunting bows, heavy billhooks, and lever-action rifles kept immaculately clean of rust.

They looked at Mac with the warm, easy respect of family welcoming an honored cousin.

And then, their eyes drifted to Maya.

They took in her bruised throat, the stiff white ash staff gripped in her work-scarred hand, the sharp, intelligent fire in her dark eyes—and the small, fierce red head resting between her prominent, flannel-draped breasts.

A low murmur of appreciation and amused surprise rippled through the gathering.

"Look at the girl," an older woman murmured, nudging her neighbor. "She's got the grease of the track on her knuckles, and she’s carrying a wild squirrel in her bosom like a jewel."

Scar, feeling forty pairs of human eyes locked squarely onto his command post, puffed out his chest until his white vest fur stood on end, raised his black nose into the sea breeze, and gave three sharp, vibrating clicks of his teeth that warned the entire population of Fort Folly that he was armed, dangerous, and in full legal possession of the high duplex.

A chorus of hearty, rolling laughter erupted from the crowd.

"The squirrel has more spit than a Sussex sergeant!" a fisherman shouted.

The crowd parted.

Out of the smoke-filled doorway of the blacksmith shop strode the master of the forge.

### Part V: The Blacksmith of the Ridge & Noel's Anvil

Noel was a mountain of a man who looked like he had been chiseled directly out of the dark Albert County shale and tempered in salt brine.

He was fifty-eight years old, with broad, square shoulders that spanned four feet across, and forearms as thick as spruce fence-posts, corded with knotty muscle and hair bleached white by forty years of hot forge cinders. His face was dark, heavily lined, with a thick black mustache streaked with iron-gray, and his right eye squinted slightly from a lifetime of reading the incandescent colors of high-carbon steel.

He was wearing a heavy, split-bullhide blacksmith’s apron that fell from his throat to his ankles, stained black with coal soot, fish oil, and powdered graphite.

In his left hand, he carried a heavy, polished three-pound cross-peen hammer; in his right, he carried a two-foot wooden rule.

Noel stopped beside the draisine, his dark eyes sweeping over the machine with the sharp, merciless scrutiny of a master craftsman.

He tapped the chilled-iron rail wheels with his hammer head: *TIIIING.*

The iron rang with a clear, flaw-free silver note.

He inspected the babbitted pillow blocks, the welded structural iron corner gussets, and the two-stage compound chain transmission Maya had designed.

He bent down, touching his blackened thumb to the fresh EP-2 moly grease glistening on the rear axle journals.

He brought his thumb to his nose, sniffing the gray compound.

Noel’s dark eyes widened.

He looked up at Mac, then shifted his gaze directly to Maya, who was standing on the deck with her sound boot planted firm, her hands resting on the rock-maple knob of her ash staff.

"Molybdenum disulfide," Noel said, his voice a deep, gravelly bass that sounded like heavy stones rolling in a surf. "Clean, unoxidized industrial lithium grease. And a compound overdrive with an external freewheel sprag clutch."

He stepped up to the edge of the deck, looking Maya dead in the eye.

"Who engineered this drive?" Noel demanded.

"I did," Maya said flatly, meeting his piercing gaze without blinking. "Three-to-one final ratio. Keyed fifty-one-sixty spring-steel axle, turned down bearing journals, and a twenty-four-tooth countershaft sprocket driving a sixteen-tooth pinion. It cruises at twenty-five miles an hour with thirty strokes a minute, and you can push the whole four-hundred-pound rig with your pinky finger."

Noel stared at her for three long seconds.

The hard, critical scowl on his weathered face slowly dissolved, replaced by a deep, rumbling belly-laugh that rattled his leather apron.

"By the salt and the shale!" Noel roared, slapping his thigh with a palm that sounded like an oak plank hitting the floor. "Kwitn, you told me over the Clacks you found a guest who knew how to turn a wrench! You didn't tell me you found a master of the mechanical arts!"

He extended his massive, soot-blackened right hand toward Maya.

Maya didn't offer a limp, timid handshake. She reached out with her own calloused, work-hardened hand, gripping his palm with a firm, bone-to-bone pressure that drew an approving nod from the veteran smith.

"I’m Noel," he said simply. "I keep the fire for the ridge. What you've built on this deck is the cleanest piece of mechanical arithmetic I’ve seen since the power died."

"Maya," she replied, smiling for the first time since they had left the siding. "Mechanical engineer from the Truro agricultural campus. And former chief mechanic of the Sussex rail yards."

At the mention of Sussex, Noel’s smile vanished, his dark eyes hardening into dark flint.

He looked at the dark-purple bruises on her neck.

"Thorne’s scavengers?" Noel asked quietly, his voice dropping into a dangerous, low register.

"Three of them," Maya nodded, her jaw tightening. "They ambushed me at the Stony Creek siding yesterday morning."

Noel looked at Mac. "And what happened to the three riders, Kwitn?"

Mac stepped forward, untying the tarpaulins over the cargo bed, his face calm and unreadable.

"One is tied to an oak bench in the maintenance shed with his own zip-ties," Mac said evenly. "One has a shattered wrist and zero transport. And their leader, Cody, is walking the forty miles back to Sussex on his bare, bleeding feet through the black-fly bogs, carrying my warning to Thorne."

A collective gasp of fierce, savage satisfaction swept through the gathered crowd.

Noel let out a low, whistling breath, shaking his head.

"A bare-foot walk through the Albert County briars," Noel muttered, a cold grin touching his mustache. "That will teach the dog what the red mud feels like. Now show me what you brought from the carcass."

Maya stepped to the front of the draisine, pulling back the oilcloth to reveal their salvage:

The four gallons of clear, high-test gasoline sealed in clean metal tins. The twenty-four feet of heavy 520 O-ring motorcycle roller chain. The two clean NGK spark plugs, the Mikuni slide carburetor, and the compact lead-calcium AGM battery.

Beside the machinery lay the homestead bounty: seventy pounds of golden-brown birch-smoked gaspereau fillets and two thirty-pound firkins of coarse white solar sea-salt.

When Noel laid his eyes on the gasoline and the drive chain, his chest heaved with excitement.

"High-octane unmixed gasoline!" Noel breathed, lifting one of the cans, shaking it gently to hear the slosh. "Do you have any idea what this means for the machine shop? We have two portable Honda generators we haven't been able to fire since last autumn because our wood-gasifier tarred the intake valves! With four gallons of pure high-test, we can run the metal lathe, turn new bronze bushings for the dory rudders, and clean the injector nozzles!"

He slammed his heavy hand onto the deck.

"Unload the trade!" Noel bellowed to his apprentices. "Bring out the iron for the Bends!"

Four young men stepped forward, hoisting the heavy fish tubs and salt firkins from the draisine with practiced ease, carrying the gasoline cans into the locked stone storehouse behind the forge.

In return, Noel hauled a heavy wooden chest out of his workshop, setting it on the tamarack deck before Mac and Maya:

Inside lay the finest ironwork in the Maritime provinces:

Two massive high-carbon broadaxes forged from five-inch pre-collapse railway leaf springs, their two-pound heads ground to a razor-sharp chisel edge that could peel a ribbon of spruce bark thin as cigarette paper, fitted with straight-grained white ash helves rubbed with beeswax and walnut oil.

Three heavy, curved drawknives with hand-turned maple handles, forged from high-speed tool steel.

A dozen heavy, barbed three-prong fish tridents for the tidal weirs, forged from solid three-quarter-inch rod iron.

And a complete set of heavy, forged manganese-bronze pintles and gudgeons to re-hang the heavy oak rudder on Mac’s twenty-foot coastal dory.

Maya picked up one of the broadaxes, testing the balance.

The tool felt alive in her hands: the weight of the head pulled the edge straight through the stroke, the ash handle curved perfectly into her palm, and the steel rang like crystal when she flicked it with her fingernail.

"Sheffield quality, Noel," Maya said, her eyes shining with admiration. "Tempered to five hundred degrees in salt tallow?"

"Five hundred and ten," Noel grinned proudly. "Quenched in seal oil and drawn over birch charcoal. That edge will cut through red spruce knots all winter and shave the hair off your forearm at supper time."

He wiped his hands on his bullhide apron, looking at Maya with deep, paternal warmth.

"Now leave the iron to the boys, Maya," Noel said gently. "Auntie Elizabeth has been sitting on the high porch since the Clacks signal flashed at sunrise. She has the pipe lit, the tea brewed, and she’s waiting to meet the woman who made the iron speeder fly."

### Part VI: Auntie Elizabeth & The Seed Vault

The council house of Fort Folly sat on the highest sandstone crown of the promontory, two hundred paces above the blacksmith shop, surrounded by a low dry-stone windbreak and a fragrant hedge of wild sweetgrass, bayberry, and beach roses.

On the covered cedar porch, sheltered from the southwest gale, sat Auntie Elizabeth.

She was eighty-two years old, small, compact, and as tough and unyielding as a piece of gnarled swamp tamarack that had survived a century of salt tides. Her face was a masterwork of Maritime history: skin the color of well-oiled copper, deeply carved with thousands of fine, weathered wrinkles that held both ancient sorrow and fierce, razor-sharp humor. Her thick silver hair was parted in the center, braided into two heavy plaits that fell over her shoulders, tied with red rawhide thongs.

Her left eye was milky-white and blind—the result of a flying cinder from an open hearth fire during the cold winter of 1972—but her right eye was dark, glossy, and sharp as a northern goshawk’s, missing nothing that moved between the porch and the horizon.

She was wearing a heavy, hand-woven gray wool sweater over a faded red cotton work dress, her feet tucked into moose-hide moccasins lined with soft shearling sheepskin.

In her right hand, she held a short-stemmed pipe carved from wild cherry burl, puffing small, sweet-smelling clouds of dried coltsfoot, sweet fern, and wild tobacco that drifted lazily into the spruce air.

Beside her rocking chair sat a heavy, braided sweetgrass basket, and on the small pine table at her elbow rested an earthenware teapot steaming with wild mint and dried ginger root.

As Mac helped Maya up the stone steps—Maya leaning her weight comfortably on her white ash staff, her sound boot clicking softly on the flagstones—Elizabeth lowered her pipe.

The old woman’s single dark eye tracked Maya’s approach with intense, laser-like focus:

She took in the bruised, purple fingerprints on Maya’s throat. She took in the stiff, disciplined movement of her splinted left ankle. She took in the grease stains ground deep into the pores of her hands, the clean, steady gaze of her dark eyes—

And then, Elizabeth’s eye dropped to the unbuttoned V-neck of Maya’s green flannel shirt.

Perched between the generous, flannel-draped swell of Maya’s breasts, his front paws hooked over her fourth button like a little red king resting on a velvet parapet, sat Scar.

The squirrel’s black whiskers twitched. His nose sampled the sweet fern smoke drifting from the elder’s pipe.

Elizabeth froze.

Her single dark eye widened.

Then, the corners of her wrinkled mouth began to twitch.

A deep, wheezing chuckle started in the bottom of her belly, rising up through her throat until she threw her head back against the cedar siding and let out a wild, cackling, knee-slapping roar of pure ancient mirth that echoed clean across the high ridge.

"BY THE TIDE AND THE MUD!" Elizabeth wheezed, wiping tears of laughter from her weathered cheeks with the back of her apron. "LOOK AT THE SQUIRREL! LOOK AT THE LITTLE BASTARD!"

She leaned forward in her rocking chair, pointing the stem of her cherry-burl pipe straight at Maya’s cleavage.

"Girl," Elizabeth cackled, her voice full of gravel and joy, "I have lived eighty-two years on this red earth. I saw the British leave, I saw the provincial government try to pave the marshes, and I saw the whole modern world choke to death on its own plastic garbage. But in all my days, I have never seen an Acadian red squirrel find a nesting drey with that kind of luxury insulation!"

Maya’s cheeks flushed a bright, healthy bronze beneath her work-smudges, but she couldn't help a wide, rueful grin.

"He moved in two days ago, Auntie," Maya explained, leaning on her ash staff. "He claimed squatter's rights under the boreal statutes of seventeen-thirteen, declared my anatomy to be a two-room condominium with radiant central heating, and refused to vacate."

"And why would he leave?" Elizabeth laughed, slapping her knee again. "He’s got fleece cushions, body heat at ninety-eight degrees, free transit on an iron cart, and a woman with an engineer's brain to defend the door! That squirrel has better real estate than the Premier had in Fredericton!"

Scar, realizing that he was the center of conversation, puffed out his chest, gave a dignified, imperial flick of his tail, and let out an arrogant, soft *chrrr* from between her breasts.

Elizabeth reached into her deep apron pocket, pulling out a small handful of dried, roasted sunflower kernels.

She held her open palm out toward Maya’s chest.

"Come here, you little emperor," the elder rumbled softly. "Pay your respects to the seed keeper."

Scar didn't bolt. He didn't flinch.

He sniffed the old woman’s hand. He smelled the ancient, clean scent of cedar bark, sweetgrass, dried soil, and eighty years of woodsmoke.

Slowly, delicately, Scar stepped forward, placing his tiny cream-colored front paws onto Elizabeth’s gnarled, calloused fingers. He took a sunflower kernel between his teeth, sat back on his haunches between Maya’s breasts, and began gnawing happily, spraying tiny black seed hulls across her collar.

Elizabeth’s single eye softened, a deep, reverent warmth replacing the laughter.

She looked up at Maya.

"Sit down, daughter," Elizabeth said, her voice dropping into that quiet, sacred cadence of an elder speaking to the future. "Sit on the cedar bench and drink the mint. We have work to do."

Maya sat down on the wide bench beside the rocking chair, resting her ash staff against the wall, stretching her splinted left leg straight to ease the joint. Mac poured three earthenware mugs of the steaming mint tea, handing one to Maya and one to the elder, before stepping back to lean his massive frame against the porch post, listening in quiet respect.

Elizabeth took a slow puff of her cherry pipe, looking out across the vast, sparkling blue waters of Shepody Bay.

"Kwitn told me what happened at the Stony Creek siding," Elizabeth said quietly, the smoke curling around her silver braids. "Thorne’s dogs tried to strangle you against the iron."

"They tried," Maya replied, her voice steady and hard as cold steel. "Mac broke them. We threw their machines into the gorge, took their fuel and chains, and sent their leader walking barefoot back to Sussex."

Elizabeth nodded once—a slow, grim nod that carried centuries of ancestral memory.

"Good," the elder said flatly. "Men like Thorne are not human beings anymore, Maya. They are parasites that fed on the rotting corpse of the old cities. They think because they have an engine and a gun, they can turn the world into a plantation and treat women like draft horses. But the machines are dying. Their gas is burning out. And the winter will finish what Kwitn started."

She reached down, lifting the heavy, braided sweetgrass basket from the floor, setting it on Maya’s lap.

The basket was heavy, tightly woven, and sealed at the lid with dark, aromatic spruce pitch.

"Here is why you came, daughter," Elizabeth murmured, laying her wrinkled, spotted hand over Maya's knuckles.

Maya carefully lifted the lid.

Inside lay the living soul of the Acadian and Mi'kmaw shore:

Dozens of small birchbark canisters and dried deer-bladder pouches, each tied with rawhide string, labeled in careful, faded ink written by four generations of elder women:

*Wabanaki Cranberry Pole Beans.*

*True Northern Flint Corn — Eight-Row Garnet.*

*Acadian Long-Keeper Winter Squash.*

*Seneca Snakeroot.*

*Wild Coastal Sea-Beet.*

*High-Oil Russian Mammoth Sunflower.*

Maya ran her fingertips over the smooth birchbark containers, her breath catching in her throat.

As an agricultural botanist, she knew precisely what she was looking at:

This was not hybrid commercial seed bred in chemical laboratories to require synthetic fertilizers and patented pesticides. These were ancient, open-pollinated landrace varieties that had been selected, adapted, and preserved through four hundred years of Maritime frosts, droughts, nor'easters, and colonial expulsions. They could survive twenty-below freezes in a dry bin; they could grow in sandy, acidic river loam with nothing more than kelp compost; and they produced four thousand calories of dense, complete protein and complex carbohydrates per mound.

"Auntie Elizabeth..." Maya breathed, her dark eyes shining with profound reverence. "This is... this is an entire agricultural civilization in a basket."

"It is our survival, Maya," Elizabeth said softly, her single eye locking onto Maya’s with fierce, unyielding intensity. "When the English soldiers came in seventeen-fifty-five to burn the Acadian dykes and steal the cattle, my great-great-grandmother hid these bean seeds inside the hem of her woolen petticoat. When the federal agents came in the nineteen-thirties to take the children to the residential schools, my mother buried these corn jars beneath the stones of the root cellar. Governments fall, currencies turn into toilet paper, and empires rot into rust. But as long as the women keep the seed, the people live."

She pointed a gnarled finger at Maya’s chest.

"You have the engineer's mind, Maya," Elizabeth said, her voice rich with prophetic certainty. "You know how to make iron turn and gears mesh. But a machine is only as good as the belly of the person who pumps the handle. Take these seeds back to the Bends. Plant the beans along the terrace. Grind the flint corn for the winter bread. And when the snow lies four feet deep across the Petitcodiac, you teach the children how to keep the promise."

Maya bowed her head, her hands pressing the sweetgrass basket against her lap, feeling the ancient, living weight of the seeds vibrating through her fingers.

"I will protect them, Auntie," Maya whispered, her voice thick with fierce, sacred resolve. "I swear to you by the salt and the shale, the seeds will never die."

Inside her shirt, Scar poked his whiskered nose up, looked at the birchbark canisters of seed, gave a tiny, reverent sniff of the heritage corn, and pressed his cheek flat against Maya’s collarbone in quiet, solemn witness.

Elizabeth smiled—a slow, beautiful, ancient smile that erased eighty years of hardship from her face.

"I know you will, daughter," the elder said softly. "Now let's go down to the longhouse. Noel has boiled thirty pounds of salt cod with new marsh potatoes, the spruce beer is cold, and the people of the ridge are hungry for a song."

### Part VII: The Council of the Bends & The Feast of Salt Cod

Night fell across Fort Folly in a magnificent, velvet wash of indigo and starlight.

Inside the great cedar-timbered longhouse, the central hearth fire roared with dry birch logs and seasoned driftwood, throwing dancing golden light across sixty feet of hand-hewn red spruce beams.

The scent inside the hall was intoxicating: the rich, salty steam of fresh-boiled Atlantic cod; the earthy sweetness of marsh potatoes boiled in their skins; the sharp, resinous perfume of cold spruce beer brewed from spring tips and wild molasses; and the nutty aroma of parched cornmeal mush drizzled with dark honey.

Fifty people sat along the heavy pine trestle tables, their laughter, conversation, and wooden trenchers clattering in a joyful, boisterous celebration of community.

Maya sat at the high table between Mac and Noel, her plate piled high with tender, flaky white salt cod, boiled potatoes, and steamed fiddleheads.

Her white ash staff leaned against the bench beside her, her splinted leg rested comfortably on a folded sheepskin fleece, and her appetite was clean, fierce, and unhurried.

And nestled in his undisputed place of honor in the warm hollow of Maya's shirt, Scar was living like an emperor.

The squirrel had been provided with his own small wooden trencher plate set on the table directly in front of Maya’s chest: heaped with cracked butternuts, parched corn kernels, and two sweet dried blueberries Noel’s wife had brought from the pantry. Scar was methodically stuffing his cheek pouches, his little tail twitching with transcendental bliss, occasionally pausing to reach up and groom Maya’s flannel collar with his tiny front paws, completely unbothered by the sixty humans celebrating around him.

Beside the hearth, Nipn lay stretched out on a bed of fresh spruce boughs, gnawing contentedly on a boiled cod backbone, his amber eyes blinking lazily in the firelight.

At the head of the table, Noel set down his wooden mug of spruce beer with a heavy *thud*, looking across at Mac and Maya.

"Word came in from Salisbury an hour before sundown, Kwitn," Noel said, his deep voice carrying over the chatter of the hall.

The table went quiet. Men and women set down their forks, turning their faces toward the blacksmith.

Mac looked up from his plate, his pale blue eyes sharp and calm. "What do the tracks say, Noel?"

Noel leaned forward, a grim, savage smile spreading beneath his gray-streaked mustache.

"A fur trapper named Gabriel came down the Memramcook river in a cedar canoe this afternoon," Noel said, his voice ringing through the longhouse. "He was at the Salisbury rail bridge yesterday evening when Cody came limping out of the bogs."

Maya’s fork paused in mid-air. "He made it back to Salisbury?"

"Barely," Noel chuckled, a low, hard sound. "Gabriel said the man’s boots were gone, his socks were shredded, and his bare feet were bleeding into the gravel like stuck pigs. He had leeches hanging from his calves, his broken arm was swollen up like a black squash, and he was weeping like a whipped child."

The longhouse erupted in a burst of harsh, satisfied laughter from the fishermen and hunters.

"What did he tell Thorne’s people?" Mac asked quietly.

"He told them the southern valley is cursed," Noel replied, his dark eyes glowing with fierce pride. "He told them that the girl they were hunting is guarded by a six-foot-five *Kinap* giant who catches nine-inch knives in mid-air with his bare fingers and throws two-stroke motorcycles sixty feet into the river gorge like tin cans. He told them the woods are full of wolves and psychotic biting squirrels that tear men’s ears off, and that if any machine comes south of Stony Creek, the giant will walk to Sussex and tear their compound down to the dirt."

Noel slammed his fist onto the table, making the wooden trenchers jump.

"Gabriel said Thorne called an emergency council at the truck stop," Noel continued, his voice ringing with absolute triumph. "Half his riders refused to mount up. They said they signed on to scavenge abandoned grocery stores and shoot deer, not to fight forest spirits and giants who break iron rails over their knees. Thorne had to back down. They’ve pulled their outposts back to the Sussex town limits, and they’ve barricaded the highway bridges to the south."

A deafening cheer broke out across the longhouse, men banging their wooden mugs against the pine tables, shouting toasts to the red river and the Old Stone Light.

Maya sat in the warm glow of the fire, feeling the profound, breathtaking reality of what they had accomplished.

The terror that had haunted her for five months—the nightmare of engines whining in the dark, of hands grabbing her through the brush, of being dragged back to a concrete cage in chains—was gone.

Shattered. Annihilated by twenty-two seconds of Kinap iron and the unyielding strength of an honest hearth.

She looked to her left at Mac.

The giant sat with his hands resting quietly on the pine boards, his weathered face carved from shadow and amber firelight, completely unmoved by the praise and the cheers. He didn't boast. He didn't puff his chest. He merely smiled—a slow, gentle, sovereign smile that honored the peace they had won.

Maya reached out beneath the table, laying her hand over his massive, calloused knuckles.

"Thank you, Kwitn," she whispered, her voice rich with deep, eternal loyalty.

Mac turned his pale blue eyes to hers, squeezing her fingers with that warm, unbreakable grip.

"We did it together, sister," Mac rumbled softly. "The machine, the iron, the staff, and the hearth. We are the commonwealth of the living shore now. And nobody will ever take this ground from us."

At the head of the table, Auntie Elizabeth raised her wooden mug of spruce tea, her single hawk-like eye sweeping over Mac, Maya, Noel, and the laughing people of Fort Folly.

"To the seed that was kept!" Elizabeth proclaimed, her voice cutting through the hall like a silver bell. "To the iron that was forged! To the red river that cannot be conquered! And to the children of the new dawn!"

"TO THE HEARTH!" the sixty voices of Fort Folly roared back in unison, raising their mugs to the rafters.

Outside the heavy cedar walls of the longhouse, forty feet of cold Atlantic salt water was roaring up Shepody Bay in the dark, the great Fundy tide sweeping past the sandstone bluffs with the ancient, unshakeable power of the sea.

Above the headland, the northern lights began to dance—long, shimmering curtains of emerald-green and violet fire rippling across the diamond-studded vault of the summer sky.

Inside the hall, the fire burned tall and bright. The cod was sweet. The iron axes rested in the chest. The seeds slept safe in their sweetgrass basket.

And nestled securely in the warm hollow of Maya's shirt, full of roasted butternuts and warm tea, the little red king closed his eyes, curled his bushy tail over his nose, and fell into a deep, victorious sleep, knowing that his kingdom was sovereign, his human was safe, and the road belonged to the free.

### Part VIII: The Nixtamalization Lore & The Dawn Flight to the Bends

Before the longhouse fire died down to glowing birch embers, Auntie Elizabeth took Maya by the elbow, leading her back to the warm hearth corner where three heavy cast-iron cauldrons hung from the masonry crane.

"You have the seed, daughter," Elizabeth said, her single dark eye gleaming in the firelight. "Now you must learn the alchemy that keeps the children alive through the dark months."

Maya leaned on her white ash staff, watching with intense, professional curiosity.

"Nixtamalization," Maya murmured, her botanist's training immediately recognizing the process.

"The white settlers in the nineteenth century called it *hominy*," Elizabeth nodded, stirring a bubbling pot of boiling river water with a flat maple paddle. "They saw our grandmothers doing it, but they thought it was just a silly Indian superstition. So when the colonial schools and the company stores started selling raw degermed cornmeal without the ash, whole villages died of the black tongue—pellagra. They starved with their bellies full of corn, because their doctors were too proud to ask an old woman why she threw wood ash into her cooking pot."

The elder reached into an earthenware crock, scooping up two cups of clean, white ash sifted from the heart of the sugar maple firepot.

She dusted the fine white ash into the boiling water.

*SSSSSSSSSSSS.*

The boiling water instantly turned a cloudy, golden-yellow, releasing a clean, mineral smell like clean limestone in a summer rain. The pH of the pot spiked upward into a mild, natural alkaline solution of potassium hydroxide and calcium oxide.

Elizabeth took a wooden bowl filled with four pounds of the hard, garnet-colored northern flint kernels they had brought from the Bends.

She poured the hard corn into the simmering lye bath.

"Four hours of gentle simmer," Elizabeth explained, her voice steady and instructional. "The alkaline water dissolves the tough, glassy outer pericarp of the kernel. It frees the bound niacin—vitamin B3—so human intestines can absorb it. It releases the tryptophan, balances the amino acids into complete, bio-available protein, and infuses the grain with calcium from the wood ash so the bones stay hard as iron through twenty-below winters. You do not eat flint corn raw, Maya. You respect the fire, you use the ash of the maple, and the corn gives you life."

Maya nodded slowly, her fingers tracing the edge of the iron pot, her mind synthesizing the chemistry:

"It transforms an incomplete, indigestible starch into a complete protein that equals eggs or venison," Maya said with deep reverence. "The wood ash doesn't just clean the grain; it completes the biology."

"The wood feeds the corn, and the corn feeds the people," Elizabeth smiled, tapping Maya’s temple with a gnarled knuckle. "Never forget the ash, engineer. Without the ash, the iron in your head is useless."

Three hours before sunrise, the settlement of Fort Folly was quiet beneath a dome of cold, crystal stars.

The northern lights had settled into a faint, pale-green arch that spanned the southern horizon over the Bay of Fundy, reflecting in the black, glass-smooth water of the receding tide.

Mac and Maya walked down the stone trail from the high ridge to the railway siding, their boots crunching softly on the crushed shells.

Maya walked with smooth, confident balance, her white ash staff clicking in clean rhythm with her sound right boot, her left ankle bearing more weight with every passing hour. The cold night air had reduced the swelling completely, and the comfrey poultice had done its silent, cellular work.

Between her breasts, nestled deep in the fleece lining of her bra, Scar was fast asleep, his little stomach swollen with roasted butternuts and sweet dried blueberries, snoring softly against her sternum.

At the railway terminal, Noel was already waiting beside the draisine.

The blacksmith had spent the night working by the light of a seal-oil lamp, and the handcar was transformed:

Strapped securely to the forward deck were the two high-carbon 5160 broadaxes, their polished ash helves gleaming with fresh walnut oil; the three timber drawknives; the heavy iron fish tridents; the manganese-bronze dory pintles; and three heavy burlap sacks containing four bushels of nixtamalized hominy corn, dried squash rings, and salt cod.

Beside the cargo sat the sweetgrass basket holding Auntie Elizabeth’s sacred heritage seed vault, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed directly behind the spruce mast.

And mounted to the forward cross-beam of the draisine, Noel had bolted a masterpiece of defensive engineering:

A heavy, curved plow-deflector forged from a three-foot section of high-carbon railway rail, heated, split, and pointed like the prow of an icebreaker.

"If Cody or Thorne ever try to drag a fallen red spruce across the track," Noel rumbled, wiping his greasy hands on his leather apron, "this iron wedge will split an eight-inch spruce log like kindling and throw it into the ditch at thirty miles an hour without shaking the deck."

Maya ran her gloved fingers over the polished, blue-black steel of the rail plow, her eyes glowing with admiration.

"A cowcatcher built from eighty-pound rail," she smiled. "Noel, you're an artist."

"I’m a smith who likes his friends to stay on the track," Noel grinned back.

Mac stepped onto the deck, checking the tension on the halyard and testing the boiled-maple brake lever. Everything was greased, tight, and true.

Nipn leaped lightly onto the tamarack deck, settling his silver bulk beside the seed basket, his nose testing the wind that was beginning to shift around to the south.

The tide had turned at the mouth of the bay.

Forty feet of cold Atlantic ocean water was gathering its gravitational weight, preparing to surge northward up the great red gorge of the Petitcodiac. And with the turning tide came the southwest sea breeze—warm, steady, and blowing directly up the railway line toward the Bends.

Maya stepped onto the rear deck, taking her station beside the brake lever.

She looked at Noel, who stood on the siding with his heavy hammer resting on his shoulder, his dark eyes filled with fierce, brotherly loyalty.

"Tell Auntie Elizabeth the seeds are safe," Maya said softly. "And tell her the ash will be in every pot."

"She knows, daughter," Noel nodded, raising his right hand in a closed fist against his chest—the ancient L'nu salute of sovereign brotherhood. "Ride the iron home. The red river is yours."

Mac hauled on the halyard.

*SHRRRRRP-CRACK!*

The white nylon sail caught the morning sea wind, snapping taut with five hundred pounds of aerodynamic thrust.

"DROP THE LEVER, MAYA!" Mac roared into the starlight.

Maya threw the boiled-maple brake lever forward.

The chilled-iron wheels bit into the welded rails.

With zero friction, zero hesitation, and the terrifying, silent grace of a hawk diving through a canyon, the draisine lunged forward through the rock cut of Fort Folly, bursting out onto the open coastal bluffs.

*CLACK-CLACK. CLACK-CLACK.*

The speed climbed instantly: fifteen miles an hour, twenty miles an hour, twenty-eight miles an hour.

The cold salt wind whipped across Maya’s face, carrying the rich, clean scent of the open Atlantic.

Between her breasts, Scar felt the roaring acceleration, popped his whiskered snout up through her collar, blinked his black eyes at the rushing stars, and let out a wild, victorious *chip* of supreme, imperial joy.

To the east, the first pale violet fingers of dawn were breaking over the distant ridges of Albert County, painting the chocolate mud flats in liquid rose and molten gold.

To the north, the two straight steel rails cut through the wilderness like an iron road leading home to the Old Stone Light.

The raiders were broken. The seeds were in the basket. The hearth was sovereign.

And flying across the red earth at the speed of the wind, the people of the bends were free.

### Part IX: The Return to the Old Stone Light

The return flight up the rails took less than twenty-five minutes.

Driven by the southwest Fundy sea gale and the turning tide, the draisine hissed across the Belliveau Creek trestle, surged through the high shale cuts, and glided onto the private spur siding of the homestead with the effortless grace of a homing osprey.

Maya hauled back on the boiled-maple brake lever, bringing the four-hundred-pound speeder to a smooth, silent halt right beside the spruce shed.

The morning sun had cleared the eastern bluffs, bathing the crushed clam-shell courtyard in brilliant, golden warmth. The living willow fence was shimmering with morning dew, the Three Sisters garden was rustling in the breeze, and from the stone chimney of the red spruce cabin, a thin, fragrant wisp of blue birch smoke rose straight into the blue sky.

Nipn leaped lightly from the deck, trotting over to the rain barrel to take a long, deep drink of the sweet mountain water, his tail giving three slow, contented thumps against the flagstones.

Mac stepped down onto the gravel, rolling up his homespun wool sleeves.

Together, they unloaded the trade cargo that would secure the future of the homestead for the winter ahead:

Maya carried the sacred sweetgrass basket containing Auntie Elizabeth’s heritage seed vault, holding the ancient birchbark canisters close against her chest as she walked up the stone path with her white ash staff.

Her left ankle took her full weight with barely a whisper of stiffness. The nine miles of rail transit, the cold sea air, and the sheer, intoxicating rush of autonomous freedom had burned away the last remnants of her invalid confinement. She walked with the long, confident, balanced stride of an engineer who belonged to the earth beneath her boots.

Mac hauled the two heavy 5160 broadaxes and the burlap sacks of nixtamalized hominy into the north slope root cellar.

Inside the four-ton dry-stone vault, the air was cool, dry, and clean, sitting at a steady five degrees Celsius. Mac laid the broadaxes on the heavy timber tool rack beside his drawknives, and poured the golden, lime-treated corn into the dry white pine grain bins.

Maya set the sweetgrass basket in the center of the cedar shelving, surrounded by jars of dried wild mint, smoked fish fillets, and solar sea-salt.

"There," Maya murmured, her hand resting on the woven lid. "Safe from frost. Safe from weevils. Safe from raiders."

Mac turned to her in the cool gloom of the cellar, his pale blue eyes reflecting the lantern light, his weathered face carved with quiet, profound pride.

"A week ago, Maya," Mac said softly, his deep baritone vibrating through the heavy stone walls, "this homestead was an isolated fortress held by one man, a wolf, and an angry squirrel. Today, we are bound to Fort Folly by thirty miles of steel track, an unbreachable defense pact, four gallons of high-test fuel, two broadaxes, and four hundred years of living seed."

He reached out, his massive, scarred hand resting gently on her shoulder.

"You brought the arithmetic, sister. You brought the transmission. And you brought the heart of the new commonwealth."

Maya looked up into his honest, clear eyes.

She didn't look down. She didn't flinch. She laid her own hand over his thick fingers, feeling the unbreakable, granite strength of the man who had pulled her out of the mud and given her back her sovereignty.

"We built it together, Kwitn," Maya replied softly, her voice rich with unwavering loyalty. "And together, we will hold the red river until the ice breaks."

Inside her flannel shirt, Scar poked his whiskered snout up from between her breasts.

The little squirrel looked at Mac, looked at the seed bins, gave a haughty, dignified flick of his whiskers, and reached up with one tiny cream-colored front paw to pat Maya’s collarbone twice in solemn executive confirmation.

*The pantry is full,* the squirrel projected, his little chest swelling with imperial satisfaction. *The high duplex is stocked for the winter. The landlord will now take his morning nap. Maintain silence in the hall.*

Maya burst into a bright, musical laugh that echoed off the four-ton stone lintels of the vault.

Mac threw his head back, his booming laughter shaking the ceiling joists, and together, they stepped out of the cellar into the brilliant, sun-drenched morning of the Petitcodiac.

Down in the great gorge below, forty feet of churning Atlantic salt water was roaring past the sandstone bluffs, the great Fundy tidal bore sweeping past the Belliveau promontory with the ancient, unshakeable power of the sea.

The gate was closed. The road was open. And the fortress of the bends was home.
