# Chapter 18: The Shattered Crossing

### Part I: The Arithmetic of the Sparring Floor

The morning over the high bluffs of Belliveau Village smelled of frost-cured sweet-fern, dry spruce needles, and the cold, alkaline tang of sixteen billion tons of Fundy mud turning inside out on the low ebb.

In the packed-shale courtyard between the stone lighthouse and the living-wood palisade, the only sound was the rhythmic, dry scuff of double-soled caribou boots against river cobbles and the clean, whip-like hiss of seasoned white ash cleaving the morning mist.

"Again," Mac said.

He stood six paces out from the granite lip of the rainwater cistern, his boots planted shoulder-width on the red stone, his broad shoulders relaxed under the dark oiled caribou vest. His hands were empty. He held no staff, no iron, no cut hardwood. His long arms hung loose at his flanks, his heavy shovel-blade palms open, his scarred knuckles curled slightly inward like weathered roots resting in loam. He looked less like an opponent and more like an inconvenient piece of geological strata that had wandered into the yard and decided to take root.

Maya drew a single, sharp breath through her nose. 

She stood in a low, coiled sprinter’s stance, her feet offset by twenty-two inches—the exact stride-length of her second acceleration phase from her varsity track days at Dalhousie. Her weight was balanced sixty-forty over the balls of her feet, her center of gravity dropped four inches below her navel. Across her palms, horizontal and level with her solar plexus, lay the five-foot-two-inch white ash staff.

The wood was cool against her calloused skin. Over three weeks of evening scraping by the hearth, Mac had shown her how to draw the bastard file along the straight growth rings of the *Fraxinus americana*, taking off shavings no thicker than an onion skin until the white ash had stopped behaving like a dead branch and begun behaving like a living tendon. It weighed exactly three pounds and four ounces. The balance point sat precisely twenty-six inches from the cold-forged iron ferrule cap that enclosed the forward tip—a sleeve of soft charcoal iron held fast by two counter-sunk copper pins that Mac had peened flush with the wood.

"You're watching my eyes, Maya," Mac murmured, his voice a quiet, gravelly baritone that barely stirred the salt air. "Eyes lie. A man can look to the left while his balance is already falling to the right. A man can squint like he's going for a high cross while his right hip is already cocked to kick your knee backward through the joint. Watch the clavicles. The collarbones don't know how to tell an untruth. If the left shoulder dips three-sixteenths of an inch, the femur has to follow to keep him from tipping over."

"I'm not watching your eyes," Maya said, her teeth grit behind tight lips. A thin sheen of perspiration glistened along her high Acadian cheekbones, catching the cold autumn sunlight. "I'm calculating your moment of inertia. You have two hundred and thirty-five pounds of mass concentrated within an eighteen-inch radius of your central spine. The equation for rotational inertia is $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$. Sweeping you at the ankles with a three-pound lever arm is like trying to knock over a bridge pier with a broom handle."

"Then don't sweep the pier," Mac said mildly, shifting his weight by half an inch so the frost-cured shale under his right boot gave a faint, solid *crunch*. "Sweep the water that runs under it. If the river can't push the rock, the river flows around it until the rock forgets which way was up. Come."

She didn't hesitate.

Maya explosive-stepped off her rear right cleat. Her left foot drove forward, the rubber-and-leather sole biting into the crushed shale with a sharp, percussive snap. She brought the ash staff up in a rising diagonal arc, her left hand acting as the sliding fulcrum while her right hand levered the butt downward, driving the iron-shod tip straight toward the nerve cluster where his neck met the thick trapezoid of his left shoulder.

It was a textbook strike: two hundred and ten foot-pounds of kinetic energy delivered along a four-millimeter impact ridge. Against an ordinary man in a heavy winter coat, it would have cracked the clavicle like dry kindling and dropped his arm dead to his flank.

Mac didn't jump. He didn't duck.

As the iron tip hissed within two inches of his collar, his left hand drifted upward with the casual, languid grace of an old man catching a falling autumn leaf. His broad, scarred palm met the side of the staff just behind the iron ferrule.

He didn't swat it. He simply matched its velocity, closed his calloused fingers around the straight-grain ash, and absorbed the momentum.

There was no crack. There was only a dull, muffled *thump*—the sound of an iron axe head embedding in three hundred pounds of green spruce heartwood.

The kinetic shock traveled straight down the white ash shaft like an electric current. Maya felt the vibration punch through her palms, shudder up her radius and ulna, and ring in her shoulder sockets. The staff came to a dead, absolute halt in mid-air. Her forward momentum slammed into the sudden immobility, threatening to pitch her over her own front knee.

"Angular momentum," Mac said softly, his thumb resting lightly on the wood. "Mass times velocity times radius. When the radius stops, the mass has to go somewhere. If you fight the wood, you break your own wrist."

With a flick of his wrist—barely two inches of travel—he rotated the staff clockwise. The leverage took Maya's wrists, twisting them past their anatomical comfort angle. Her grip broke on the lead hand. Before she could stumble, his right palm pressed flat against her sternum, right over the leather-sheathed obsidian neck scalpel beneath her flannel.

The touch was completely gentle, carrying no malice, but it possessed the unyielding mass of an iron brake shoe pressing against a rolling train wheel. Her forward motion died instantly. She was held suspended, breathless, balanced on the balls of her feet with her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped grouse.

For a second, neither of them moved. 

His face was eight inches from hers. She could smell the clean, bitter scent of wood smoke, pine resin, and cold river water that lived permanently in the weave of his wool shirt. In his weathered, gray eyes, deep behind the calm pupils, she caught the faint, electric flicker of cold azure—not the blinding flare of lethal combat, but the quiet, subterranean hum of a titan who held the weight of mountains without straining a tendon. His gaze was steady, searching, and completely devoid of judgment.

Maya’s pulse spiked—not from fear, but from the sudden, overwhelming heat of his chest pressing against her palms. A flush rose beneath the olive skin of her neck, burning hot against the morning chill. She could feel every contour of his fingers through the flannel, the sheer, crushing masculine gravity of him that felt less like a partner and more like a continent.

"You're not breathing, Maya," Mac whispered, his baritone vibrating in the hollow of her collarbone.

"I'm... analyzing," she managed, her breath coming in a short, ragged gasp that ruffled the gray strands at his collar.

"Analyze with oxygen in your lungs," he said, the corner of his mouth curving into that slow, affectionate, self-amused smile that always dismantled her defenses faster than any sweep. "A brain without hemoglobin is just three pounds of wet grey sponge waiting to make a mistake."

He released her, stepping back half a pace. The cold morning wind swept between them like a bucket of salt water, breaking the circuit.

"Second exchange," Mac commanded, his posture resetting instantly. "A man with an axe doesn't grab your wood; he cuts it. Take the low line."

Maya stepped back, shook the numbness from her lead fingers, and dropped into an altered stance. She widened her base to twenty-eight inches, lowering her center of gravity to lower the center of mass. In mechanical engineering, stability was a function of the base of support relative to the vertical line of gravity: $S = \frac{W}{2h}$, where $W$ was the stance width and $h$ was the height of the center of mass. By widening her feet and bending her knees, she nearly doubled her resistance to lateral toppling forces.

"Better," Mac noted, nodding once. "Now watch the hips."

Mac took a half-step forward, his right shoulder dropping as his right arm swept forward in a simulated diagonal axe stroke—a heavy, downward cleave aimed straight at her rib cage.

Maya didn't block. In wood mechanics, a static beam subjected to a dynamic shock load experiences localized bending stress: $\sigma = \frac{M y}{I}$. If she met a thirty-pound wood-chopping axe head with a perpendicular block, even seasoned white ash with an ultimate tensile strength of fifteen thousand pounds per square inch would suffer catastrophic fiber fracture along the grain.

Instead, she executed an oblique parry. 

As his arm descended, she slid the ash staff forward at an angle of thirty-five degrees to the line of impact. The smooth, oiled wood met his wrist at a glancing tangent. The kinetic energy was redirected along the hypotenuse of the triangle rather than absorbed as shear stress. His arm slid harmlessly down the length of the staff, the iron ferrule catching on the sleeve of his caribou vest.

Using the rebound force, Maya stepped through with her rear foot. She pivoted on her left heel, brought the butt of the staff around in a tight, waist-high circle, and hooked the seasoned wood directly behind Mac’s right knee joint—the popliteal fossa, where the hamstrings insert into the tibia.

It was an explosive, textbook sweep. She pulled with twelve foot-pounds of torque, intending to break the lock of his knee and bring him down to the red shale.

The ash wood struck his tendon.

It was like hooking an anchor chain wrapped in hemp rope. 

Mac’s leg didn't yield an eighth of an inch. His boot remained glued to the shale as if he had driven eight-inch drift pins through his soles into the granite bedrock beneath. The staff flexed into a shallow arc, its seasoned grain groaning under the tension, transmitting the unyielding rigidity of his skeleton straight back into her shoulders.

"The vector is correct," Mac said calmly, looking down at his hooked knee without looking perturbed. "The geometry is sound. But you forgot the anchor. If a tree has roots down to the granite, you can't pull it over by grabbing a branch. You have to take the earth away from the root."

He shifted his hip by two inches. 

The movement was effortless, but the shift in his pelvic arch broke her mechanical advantage. The staff popped free from behind his knee. Before she could recover her guard, Mac stepped inside her arc, his chest closing within four inches of hers, his left forearm resting gently across both her wrists, immobilizing her weapons.

"Third exchange," he murmured, his breath warm against her temple. "You're in close quarters. The staff is too long. The lever arm is working against you. What is your weapon now?"

Maya didn't hesitate. She abandoned the staff completely, letting her lead hand open so the wood fell toward the shale.

With her right hand, her fingers dropped like striking hornets to her right hip. Her palm hit the honey-colored applewood scales of the 320-layer Damascus fighter knife. Her thumb found the rounded copper rivet at the bolster, releasing the friction welt of the *cuir bouilli* sheath with a sharp, oily *snick*.

She cleared the six-and-a-half-inch blade in a quarter-second flat.

The differential temper line—the milky, cloud-like *hamon* where sixty-three HRC martensite met fifty-two HRC pearlite—flashed in the autumn sun. She did not swing. She did not slash. She drove the drop-point tip straight upward in an inverted thumb-forward grip, halting the micro-serrated Damascus edge exactly one millimeter below Mac’s carotid artery.

The cold steel hovered against his throat. A single stray hair from his trimmed frost-gray beard touched the microscopic grain of the 1095 carbon steel edge and sliced cleanly in half, drifting away on the salt breeze.

Mac didn't flinch. He didn't pull back.

His gray eyes locked onto hers. And then, slowly, deep in the dark pupil of his left eye, the azure ignition flickered—a spark of cold, ancient, primordial *Mntu* that acknowledged the lethal perfection of her strike.

"Now you're dangerous," Mac said softly.

His voice was thick with something that wasn't pride, wasn't mentorship, and wasn't teacherly praise. It was the raw, quiet recognition of a sovereign equal—a woman who had taken his fire, his iron, and his teaching, and transformed herself into a living weapon capable of defending the headland.

The physical closeness was suffocating. She was pressed against the front of his caribou vest, her hips resting against his thigh, her wrist steady, the cold Damascus steel holding the line between them. She could feel the steady, slow beat of his heart—barely forty beats a minute, the rhythm of a sleeping bear or a granite ledge.

Her own heart was thumping at one hundred and forty. Her lips parted slightly, her breath catching in her throat.

For three seconds, the entire world consisted of sixty-pound rails, forty-foot Fundy tides, and the two of them breathing the same cold air.

From the top rail of the living tamarack gate, fifteen feet away, a frantic, high-pitched screech shattered the quiet.

*TCH-TCH-KRRRRRR-CHIK!*

Scar was sitting bolt upright on his hind legs on the cross-brace of the gate, his bushy russet tail flicking in violent, spasmodic arcs against the split bark. His tiny black eyes were locked onto Mac, his whiskers twitching with intense, theatrical outrage.

*THE RED-LEAF RUNNER WAS ROBBED!* the squirrel chittered into Maya’s skull, the mental voice popping with static and frantic, rapid-fire indignant fury. *THE BIG BEAST CHEATED! HE PUT HIS MEAT-SLAB ON THE SUN-STICK! HE STOPPED THE WOOD-SWING! I SAW IT! I WAS WATCHING FROM THE CEDAR POST! THAT WAS AN ILLEGAL GRAB! DEDUCT THREE NUTS FROM HIS TERRITORY! ISSUE A FORMAL SQUIRREL REPRIMAND!*

Maya lowered the knife, her fingers trembling slightly as she sheared the blade back into its hot-waxed leather sheath. The copper welt clicked shut. She caught the ash staff with her left boot before it hit the shale, flipping it back into her palm with an athlete's casual coordination.

Her chest heaved beneath her denim overshirt, the heavy wool sports bra underneath straining against the deep, rhythmic expansion of her lungs. Despite the ache in her forearms and the lingering heat on her neck, a tight, crooked smile tugged at the corner of her mouth.

"Hear that, Mac?" she panted, rolling her shoulders to loosen the tight muscles between her scapulae. "The umpire called a foul. You used an illegal meat-slab."

"The umpire has a brain the size of a dried navy bean and an economic philosophy rooted entirely in larceny," Mac remarked without looking at the gate. He reached into his vest pocket, his thick fingers emerging with a smooth, dried butternut that had spent two weeks curing near the flue stones. He held it out on his open palm. "Though I admit his vocal projection is impressive for an organism weighing less than a can of condensed milk."

Scar froze. The frantic tail-flicking stopped dead. His little head tilted ninety degrees to the left, his black nostrils flaring as the rich, fatty scent of cured butternut oil drifted across the morning breeze.

*FAT-MEAT,* Scar broadcasted instantly, all righteous legal indignation vanishing like morning mist over the mudflats. *HIGH-DENSITY RIVER NUT. THE MOUNTAIN-WALKER HAS PRODUCED A SETTLEMENT CONCESSION. THE DISPUTE IS ADJOURNED SINE DIE.*

The squirrel launched himself off the tamarack rail in a low, flat trajectory, hit the shale four feet out without a sound, scuttled across the courtyard in a russet blur, and scurried up Maya’s canvas trouser leg like a clawed beetle ascending a pine trunk. He scrambled over her leather hip holster, bypassed the applewood handle of her Damascus knife, dug his sharp little toes into the denim of her shoulder, and planted himself on her collarbone, his cold nose pressed right under her ear.

From that secure vantage point, half-tucked into the warm fleece lining of her collar, he leaned forward and extended both front paws toward Mac’s hand, chittering a rapid, transactional command.

*TRANSFER THE GOODS TO THE AUTHORIZED TENANT.*

"Not for free," Maya murmured, reaching up with two fingers to stroke the soft, downy fur between the squirrel's tufted ears. She looked at Mac, her dark chicory-brown eyes sharp and analytical. "He called your grab before my brain even registered that my wrists were locked. He watched your weight shift before I did."

"Aye," Mac agreed, his gray eyes crinkling at the corners with quiet warmth. "Rodents live in a world where everything larger than a teacup is trying to turn them into protein. Their optic nerves process visual stimuli at roughly one hundred and twenty frames a second, compared to our sixty. To Scar, you and I move like we're wading through cold molasses."

Mac flipped the butternut into the air. 

Maya caught it cleanly with her left hand, turned it over in her palm, and pulled a small steel nutpick from her utility pouch. With a practiced twist of the tempered point, she cracked the hard outer shell along its suture line, splitting the nut into two neat, oily halves. She handed the larger half directly to the squirrel nestled against her neck.

Scar grabbed the kernel with both front paws, hugging it against his chest like an orphaned child, his sharp orange incisors instantly rasping against the rich, sweet meat with a high-speed *scritch-scritch-scritch-scritch*.

*EXCELLENT WAGE-PAYMENT,* Scar projected, muffled around a mouthful of high-calorie fat. *THE RUNNER PAYS ON TIME. THE RUNNER DOES NOT DEFAULT. I WILL CONTINUE TO PROVIDE LOW-ALTITUDE AIR SURVEILLANCE. IF THE MOUNTAIN-WALKER MOVES HIS PAWS AGAIN, I WILL BITE HIS THUMB.*

"Don't make promises you can't finance, little squatter," Mac said dryly. 

He stepped over to the low cedar bench near the forge, where a gallon jug of cold spring water sat beside a clean linen cloth. He lifted the heavy stoneware jug with one hand, unstopped the wooden peg with his teeth, and drank deeply before passing it to Maya.

She took the jug with both hands, feeling the cold condensation bite her palms, and drank until the chill hit her stomach, soothing the fire in her throat. When she lowered it, she wiped her chin with the back of her wrist, her eyes traveling past the living palisade to the northern horizon where the tree-covered ridges rolled away toward Salisbury and Moncton.

The air was clear, but far to the northwest, five miles beyond the great iron rail line, a thin, dirty smudge of brownish haze hung motionless above the spruce canopy. It wasn't the clean, blue-white smoke of dry birch cordwood burning in an efficient masonry heater. It was the greasy, sulfurous smudge of raw pit coal and green wood burning in a forced-draft firebox.

"Thorne's crews," Maya said, her voice dropping all trace of humor, flattening into the cold, level register of a woman looking at her old chains. "They're firing the stationary boiler at the Salisbury gravel pit again. They're trying to clear the slide off the main line."

"Aye," Mac said. He stood beside her, his gaze resting on the smudge with the calm, patient detachment of an owl watching a vole in the tall grass. "They've been at it three days. Nipn smelled the grease on the wind last night. Coal oil, rancid lard, and bad coal from the Springhill seam."

"If they get the gravel pit cleared, they have three miles of open track straight down to the river crossing," Maya said. Her right hand dropped unconsciously to her hip, her thumb brushing the cold copper rivets of the Damascus knife's applewood scales. "They have two flatbed maintenance cars and the small diesel switcher they dragged out of Sussex. If Thorne gets that engine down to the Salisbury trestle, he controls the entire western approach to the bends."

"A machine without a track is just twenty tons of rusted iron waiting for the rain, Maya," Mac said softly. "The Salisbury trestle is four hundred feet of rotting red spruce timber that hasn't seen a creosote spray or an inspection crew in twelve years. The frost heaved the center bents three winters ago. If he runs twenty tons of steel onto that deck, the river will take the engine, the cars, and whatever foolish men are riding them."

"He knows that," Maya countered, her engineer’s mind working the numbers. "He's not stupid, Mac. Thorne was a roadmaster's foreman before he started buying human beings in the logging pens. He won't run the heavy switcher across. He'll use the handcars. He'll push a reconnaissance squad across on foot, seize the high siding at Boundary Creek, and set up a fortified picket line."

She turned to face him, the staff resting upright against her shoulder like a spear.

"We need the hardware from that siding, Mac. The high-pressure relief valve for the forge accumulator. The bronze sleeve bushings from the maintenance shed. And the four coils of twelve-gauge galvanized fencing wire sitting in the locked signal box. If Thorne takes Boundary Creek, he'll scrap every pound of non-ferrous metal for his boiler patches. We'll never get another chance at those bushings before winter locks the river."

Mac looked down at her. His weathered, granite-gray eyes held no condescension, no fatherly reluctance, and no dismissive hesitation. He looked at her as an archivist looks at a rare, perfectly preserved folio—with deep, respectful gravity and complete comprehension of its tensile strength.

"Eighteen miles out," Mac said quietly. "Eighteen miles back. Through spruce swamp, shattered ballast, and abandoned roadbed. The tide turns at noon. You have six hours of dry mud along the low channel before the bore comes up and drowns the shore."

"With the rail speeder, I can run the eight miles of intact track to Salisbury in forty minutes," Maya said, her chin lifting. "I leave the car hidden in the alder cut near the culvert. I take the marsh trail along the creek, slip into the siding from the blind side, pull the bronze bushings and the relief valve, and secure the wire coils. I'm back on the car before Thorne's picket even finishes their mid-day boil-up."

"And if you meet raiders on the iron?"

"One or two, I take the ground with the ash staff and the Damascus blade," Maya said, her voice dropping into a hard, rhythmic cadence. "Three or more, or if iron speaks from the brush, I drop pride, refuse to die for gravel, and run. The Retreat Covenant."

Mac stared at her for a long, silent moment. The wind ruffled the gray hair at his temples. Then, slowly, he gave a single, firm nod.

"Then we outfit the runner," he said.

### Part II: The Siding at Salisbury & The Recon Kit

The outfitting took forty minutes, conducted with the quiet, ritualistic precision of two people who knew that out past the living palisade, an unsharpened edge or a missing lace was a death sentence.

In the workshop beneath the cabin eaves, Mac laid out the gear on the maple workbench.

"Water," Mac said, setting down an unbleached linen canteen lined with melted beeswax and filled with boiled spring water steeped with four drops of pine-needle rosin. "Light, silent, won't clank against the staff."

Next came the rations. He didn't pack soft bread or wet salt-pork. He packed five hard cakes of *pemmican*—rendered bear tallow pounded with dried chokeberries, powdered smoked gaspereau, and parched flint-corn meal. Each cake was the size of a tobacco tin, wrapped in oiled parchment, and contained twelve hundred calories of pure, slow-burning fuel that required no fire to digest.

Beside the pemmican, Mac set down a separate, small buckskin pouch tied with a red rawhide cord.

*CHIK?* Scar poked his nose out from beneath Maya’s collar, his nostrils quivering as he caught the rich, oily scent through the buckskin. *WHAT IS IN THE SPECIAL SACK? IS THAT RUNNER PROPERTY OR SQUIRREL ENTITLEMENT?*

"Hazard pay," Mac said, tapping the pouch. "One dozen dried butternut meats, four tablespoons of roasted sunflower seeds, and six dried cranberries rolled in maple sugar. If the tenant is going to serve as your forward lookout, the tenant deserves to be paid in coin of the realm."

Maya smiled, tying the small buckskin sack securely to the left breast loop of her denim shirt, exactly two inches below Scar’s whiskers. 

"Hear that, Scar?" she murmured, tapping the pouch. "You get one nut for every two miles, and a sunflower seed every time you spot something before I do. If you scream at a blue jay, I deduct two cranberries."

*A MONSTROUS CONTRACTUAL PROVISION,* Scar grumbled, though he immediately shoved his snout against the pouch to verify the seal. *BLUE JAYS ARE ARMED TRESPASSERS. THEY STEAL CONES. THEY HAVE NO REGARD FOR TITLE DEEDS. NEVERTHELESS, I ACCEPT THE TERMS UNDER DURESS.*

Next came the tools of the trade.

Into her light canvas haversack went a six-inch tapered cold chisel forged from an old coil spring, a four-pound ball-peen hammer with the hickory handle cut down to ten inches, two spare leather boot-laces, a small tin of spruce pitch and tallow salve, and three eight-inch wedges of seasoned rock maple for securing or jamming door frames.

At her hip, seated in its form-fitted *cuir bouilli* sheath, rode the 320-layer Damascus drop-point fighter. The honey-colored applewood scales were clean, the copper rivets peened smooth as river pearls, the 63 HRC edge stropped on horsehide until it could shave the down off a moth’s wing without waking it. 

Flat against her sternum, suspended beneath her wool shirt on a braided cord of boiled caribou sinew, rested the three-inch leaf of ancient obsidian. It weighed less than an ounce, sealed in its white cedar guard with spruce pitch and balsam fir rosin. It was her invisible final line—a blade that severed on a cellular level without encountering resistance.

In her right hand, she took the white ash staff.

Before they stepped out to the handcar, Mac led her to the work table where the maintenance drawings for the rail speeder lay unrolled.

"The speeder runs on four babbit sleeve bearings," Mac said, pointing with a calloused finger. "Eighty-nine percent tin, seven percent antimony, four percent copper. I repoured them in May and line-bored them true to within three thousandths of an inch. But babbit is soft metal, Maya. If you hit fifty miles an hour on a downgrade, the frictional shear will heat the bearing cups past three hundred degrees Fahrenheit, the tallow will boil off, and you'll seize the journals to the axle."

"Twenty-two miles an hour is my maximum cadence on the walking beam," Maya replied smoothly, tapping her temple. "At twenty-two miles an hour, the journal friction generates less than forty watts of thermal dissipation per bearing cap. The ambient airflow along the rail cut will keep the babbit below eighty-five degrees Fahrenheit. The hydrodynamic oil wedge will hold."

Mac gave a short, quiet chuckle, his eyes crinkling. "By the tide, By the salt and the shale, save the engineer. Even her grease has an equation."

"Equations don't get seized axles in raider territory," Maya said, grinning.

Mac walked her to the tamarack gate. As they reached the counterweighted stone pivot, Nipn rose from his place by the hearth-stone, padding silently over the red shale to stand between them.

The great wolf looked up at Maya. His golden-amber eyes held that clear, ancient intelligence that stripped away pretense. He didn't whine or nuzzle. He simply stepped forward, pressed the hard crown of his silver-gray skull firmly against her hip, and projected a single, clean thought-form into her mind.

*The iron path is cold today,* the wolf transmitted. *The smells on the wind are wrong. Men with dirty boots walk near the water. And in the dark hollows where the sun does not reach, there are things that walk without breathing.*

Maya paused, her fingers sinking into the thick, coarse fur of Nipn's neck ruff. She frowned slightly.

"Things that walk without breathing?" she asked, glancing at Mac. "Raiders?"

Mac’s expression did not change, but the quiet gravity in his face deepened. 

"When the great cities broke four months ago, Maya, people didn't just die of starvation and rifle fire. In the hospital wards, in the quarantine sidings where the refugee trains were shunted off the main line, people died of the rot. The old fever. The black blood."

He looked out over the salt marsh toward the western rail cut.

"Most of the dead stayed dead, as the earth intended. But some of them... the nervous system doesn't turn off all at once. The marrow stays cold. The brainstem keeps firing old, broken impulses. They are the unburied. The walking dead. They don't run. They don't build. They don't use tools. But they don't sleep, they don't feel frostbite, and they don't stop walking toward the sound of living meat."

Maya felt a cold shiver trace the line of her spine, but her engineer's mind seized on the functional mechanics.

"Brainstem," she said, her voice steady. "If the brainstem controls locomotion, then destroying the cranium or severing the second cervical vertebra breaks the circuit."

"Aye," Mac said. "A thrust to the belly or a slice to the forearm means nothing to a corpse whose blood has already congealed into black lard. You don't fence with them. You don't try to make them yield. If you must strike, you deliver maximum kinetic force directly to the occipital plate or the temporal seam. The iron tip of your ash staff will punch through bone with two hundred foot-pounds. Use it."

He stepped closer, his large, warm hand resting briefly on her shoulder. His touch was solid, anchoring, filled with that calm, immovable Kinap presence that made the entire world feel stable.

"Trust your eyes," Mac said softly. "Trust your feet. And remember: the lighthouse is always burning."

"I'll be back before the bore turns," Maya said.

She stepped through the tamarack gate, broke into a smooth, effortless runner's lope, and vanished down the red bluff path toward the rail line.

### Part III: The Walking Dead of the Rock Cut

The rail speeder rolled down the slight two-percent grade toward Salisbury with a low, rhythmic *click-clack, click-clack* of chilled steel wheels against sixty-pound iron rail.

Maya pumped the walking beam with a smooth, metronomic cadence. Her track training dictated the pace: twenty-four strokes a minute, engaging the lats, glutes, and hamstrings without allowing her heart rate to spike above one hundred and thirty beats. The speeder was a masterpiece of Mac's salvage—its maple frame seasoned to bone-hardness, its babbit-metal bearings greased with rendered bear lard, rolling with so little friction that a ten-pound push sent it gliding fifty yards along the level track.

Beside her, perched on the wooden tool box between the wheel housings, Scar rode with his front paws propped on the forward rail, his tail tucked tight against the cedar grain. Every two minutes, his head swiveled like a radar dish, his whiskers tasting the damp air that rushed down the rail corridor.

*ONE MILE,* Scar chittered mentally, his teeth vibrating with excitement. *TWO MILES. THE CORN-SEEDS ARE DUE. THE TENANT HAS DETECTED THREE BLUE JAYS, ONE DEAD HARE, AND NO GROUND-CRAWLERS. ISSUE PAYMENT.*

Maya didn't break her pumping rhythm. With her left hand, she reached into the buckskin pouch at her chest, pinched a single roasted sunflower seed, and held it out. 

Scar snatched it with the lightning speed of a striking viper, stuffed it into his right cheek pouch, and returned to his forward scan without missing a beat.

At mile seven, the rail line curved sharply into the deep Salisbury rock cut—a half-mile canyon blasted through solid red sandstone eighty years ago to carry the Canadian National main line through the ridge. The cut was damp, shadowed, and cold. Even in early autumn, the sunlight only reached the gravel ballast for an hour at midday. Heavy dripping moss clung to the vertical stone faces, and the sweet, sickly perfume of rotting elderberries hung thick in the dead air.

Maya eased back on the wooden handbrake lever. The iron brake shoes rubbed gently against the wheel rims with a dull, muffled hiss, bringing the speeder to a silent halt fifty yards before the mouth of the cut.

She stepped off the platform, her boots making zero sound on the wet ballast. She reached down, lifted the front axle of the hundred-pound speeder with her legs, and dragged it smoothly off the iron ties, tucking it deep into a thick copse of speckled alder and wild raspberry canes that choked the drainage ditch. She draped a length of wet burlap over the wheels, masking the dull gleam of the iron.

### Part IV: The Treachery of the Trestle

The Salisbury maintenance siding was an iron graveyard.

Three hundred yards beyond the western mouth of the rock cut, the rail line split into four overgrown spur tracks. Rusted boxcars, overturned gravel hoppers, and a burned-out diesel switcher sat half-swallowed by thirty years of wild sumac and red alder. 

The air here smelled different. It did not smell of the cold, clean marsh or the ancient peat bogs of the bends. It smelled of coal soot, cheap whiskey, and wood smoke.

Maya slipped through the tall reed grass, moving with the fluid, silent grace of a coastal heron stalking shad. She kept the white ash staff low, trailing it through the grass so it wouldn't silhouette against the gray sky. Scar was dead silent in her collar, his little body pressed flat against her chest, only his eyes and the tips of his tufted ears showing over her denim neckline.

Ahead stood the brick-and-timber maintenance shed. 

Built in 1914 by the Intercolonial Railway and later absorbed by Canadian National, the structure was a cathedral of heavy industrial utility: red wire-cut brick walls fourteen inches thick, arched clerestory windows furred with thirty years of coal soot and spider silk, and massive hand-hewn red spruce roof trusses spanning sixty feet across the bays without a center post. The air inside smelled of cold machine oil, damp masonry, powdered sulfur, and the rich, petroleum ghost of fifty thousand gallons of diesel fuel that had soaked into the concrete floor over half a century of roundhouse maintenance.

The main track doors—two massive leaves of four-inch tongue-and-groove white pine clad in galvanized iron sheet—were chained shut with heavy three-eighths-inch transport chain and secured by a rusted, case-hardened Corbin padlock the size of a saucer. But Maya was not interested in the doors. A door was a funnel, a choke-point designed to draw an intruder into a crossfire.

Six feet above the gravel on the blind western wall, a broken glass transom hung half-open in an iron frame. 

Setting the white ash staff against the brickwork where the shadow of a wild sumac bush masked the wood, Maya checked her footing. She bent her knees, dropped her hips, and drove upward in a single, explosive vertical leap. Her fingertips hooked over the cold brick sill. Her latissimus dorsi engaged, her sculpted shoulders bunching beneath the denim overshirt as she hauled her athletic frame smoothly over the iron sill with the fluid, effortless grace of a gymnast clearing a vault.

She lowered herself down the interior iron ladder, her double-soled boots touching the oil-blackened concrete with a faint, velvet *scuff*.

The interior was a cavern of silent machinery. 

To her left stood an eighteen-foot belt-driven Lodge & Shipley engine lathe, its heavy cast-iron bed four inches deep in congealed tallow grease and fine red dust. On the wooden tool board behind the headstock hung rows of morse-taper drill bits, Armstrong tool holders, and high-speed cobalt steel tool bits ground for square-thread lead screws. To an ordinary scavenger looking for canned beans, the lathe was just an immovable lump of rusted iron; to Maya, it was civilizational capital—a machine capable of cutting the screw threads and piston rings that could rebuild a steam plant or a sawmill.

She made a mental note of its serial plate: *Lodge & Shipley, Cincinnati, 1922. Patent May 14.* If they held the river, Mac could haul the four-ton casting to the headland on winter timber sleds over the frozen mud.

Moving deeper into the shadows, she located the parts crib behind the wire-mesh tool cage.

The padlock on the tool crib had been snapped five years ago with a cold chisel, the door hanging open on dry hinges. Inside, resting on heavy cedar shelving, sat the treasure vault of pre-collapse heavy industry.

The shed was a treasure vault of pre-collapse heavy industry.

In the tool racks along the back wall, she found them: six heavy bronze sleeve bushings, machined for two-inch shafts, preserved under a thick coat of hardened amber Cosmoline grease. Beside them sat the high-pressure relief valve—eight pounds of solid, cast phosphor-bronze with a heavy stainless-steel spring and a calibrated adjustment screw, rated for eight hundred pounds per square inch.

"Mac's accumulator," she whispered, her fingers tracing the clean, uncorroded threads.

She slipped the bushings and the valve into her canvas haversack, wrapping them in oily rags so they wouldn't clank. From the signal locker in the corner, she secured two fifty-pound rolls of twelve-gauge galvanized fencing wire—clean, zinc-coated steel that would reinforce the living-wood palisade against any axe Thorne could swing.

She slung the haversack over her shoulders, settled the weight across her hips, and slipped back out through the transom into the tall grass.

The run had been clean. It had taken twenty minutes. She had the bronze, the valve, and the wire. All that remained was to reach the speeder, pump the eight miles of track back to the bluff, and be home by the time the Fundy tide filled the tidal weirs.

She reached the mouth of the high timber trestle that crossed the Salisbury creek.

The trestle was an ancient, sprawling spiderweb of squared twelve-by-twelve red spruce timbers spanning a seventy-foot ravine where the red mud of the creek churned thirty feet below. The iron rails ran across the open timber stringers without a walkway. To cross, a runner had to step from tie to tie across sixty yards of empty air.

Maya stepped onto the first oak tie, balancing her weight with the ash staff.

Then Scar went berserk.

*TCH-TCH-KRRRRRRRRR!*

The squirrel’s mental voice shrieked inside her skull like a siren. He didn't just chitter; he bit her earlobe hard enough to draw a tiny drop of blood, his tiny body trembling violently against her chest.

*FIRE-SMELL! IRON-SMELL! RUN! RUN! RUNNER, RUN!*

Fifty yards ahead, at the far end of the trestle where the rail bed disappeared into the high spruce timber, three men stepped out of the brush.

They weren't walking dead. They were living, hard-bitten men in heavy mackinaw wool coats and grease-stained canvas pants. Across their chests were leather bandoliers of rifle cartridges. In the hands of the lead man was a heavy double-barreled ten-gauge shotgun; the other two carried military-surplus Lee-Enfield bolt-action rifles.

And twenty feet down the trestle, lashed directly to the central red spruce king-post with heavy baling wire, was a wooden crate marked with faded red stenciling: **CIL FORCITE 40% DYNAMITE.**

A thin, smoking gray trail of burning fuse was hissing straight toward the detonator cap.

"There's the runner!" the lead raider barked, his voice carrying flat and harsh across the ravine. "Thorne's girl! Don't let her reach the switch!"

Maya didn't scream. She didn't freeze.

She saw the six inches of burning fuse. She saw the three rifles coming to the shoulders of the Sussex men. Her brain processed the arithmetic in three tenths of a second: *Dynamite detonation velocity: six thousand feet per second. Blast radius of forty pounds: eighty feet. Time to detonation: approximately three seconds.*

"Back!" she gasped, throwing her entire body weight backward in a violent, desperate reverse sprint.

She leaped backward across the open ties, her boots hitting the timber with rapid, frantic precision. One tie. Two ties. Three ties.

*BOOM!*

The world tore apart in an orange sheet of flame and thunder.

The shockwave hit Maya like an iron slab. The four-hundred-foot spruce trestle didn't just collapse; the dynamite shattered the central bents into ten thousand flying wooden spears. The iron rails snapped with a sound like artillery fire, whipping into the air like severed guitar strings.

The overpressure slammed into Maya’s chest, blowing the air out of her lungs in a choked gasp. She was lifted entirely off her feet, flung backward thirty feet through the air, and smashed violently into the shale embankment.

She hit the red sandstone slope shoulder-first, rolling over sharp river rocks, flying timber splinters, and raining gravel. The heavy haversack of bronze bushings took the brunt of the kinetic impact against her spine, but the force drove her breath out completely. Her head struck a jagged shelf of Fundy shale.

A white flash of blinding light exploded behind her eyes, followed by a roaring, suffocating dark.

### Part V: Blood on the Shale, Steel in the Hand

The world came back slowly, tasting of copper, wood smoke, and red river silt.

Maya lay on her side in the drainage ditch forty feet from where the trestle had stood. Above her, a towering column of black, greasy smoke was boiling into the cold turquoise sky, carrying the stench of burned dynamite and charred spruce timber. The trestle was gone—shattered into a smoking crater of splintered bents and twisted sixty-pound rail that lay half-submerged in the sucking red mud of the creek bed thirty feet below.

Her left shoulder was screaming with fire. Her ribs on the right side ached with every shallow, agonizing breath—bruised, if not cracked, by the blast wave. Warm blood was trickling down her left cheek from a jagged two-inch laceration along her hairline, dripping off her Acadian jawline onto the torn denim of her collar. Her trousers were shredded at both knees, her skin raw and weeping where the shale gravel had flayed the flesh.

She was beaten. She was battered. Her vision swam in gray, nauseous waves.

Then, against her throat, something warm and frantic stirred.

*RUNNER?*

A tiny, wet black nose poked out from inside her fleece-lined shirt. Scar’s russet fur was covered in gray wood ash and fine stone dust, his tail trembling like an aspen leaf in a gale. He crawled out onto her chest, his little paws patting frantically against her cheek, his mental voice trembling with a desperate, terrified squeak.

*RUNNER HAS EYES CLOSED. RUNNER DOES NOT BREATHE. GET UP, RUNNER! THE SICK BAD-MEN ARE CROSSING THE WATER! GET UP! I DO NOT HAVE KEYS TO THE CABIN! WHO WILL CRACK THE HEAVY NUTS?*

Maya closed her teeth against the agony in her ribs and forced her lungs to expand.

Air rushed in—cold, sharp with pine smoke, and real.

"I'm... I'm up, Scar," she rasped, her voice a dry, gravelly croak.

She pushed herself up onto one elbow. The world spun violently, then settled into a hard, crystalline focus.

Down in the creek bed, through the smoke and the wreckage of the collapsed bridge, she saw them. Five Sussex raiders were picking their way through the shattered timbers, their rifles held high above the mud, wading through the low creek water.

"She's down in the cut!" one of them shouted, his voice echoing off the sandstone walls. "I see the pack! Drag her out! Thorne wants her breathing when we bring her to the armory!"

Maya looked down at her hands.

Her white ash staff lay five feet away, half-buried under a shower of spruce splinters. She reached out, her fingers closing around the straight-grain wood. The ash had not snapped. The iron ferrule was scuffed, the beeswax finish was scarred by gravel, but the wood was whole, unyielding, and true.

She pulled the staff to her chest, dug the butt into the shale, and forced herself onto her feet.

Pain lanced through her ribs, sharp as an iron needle, but she did not cry out. In her track days at Dalhousie, her coach had drilled a singular physiological truth into her marrow: pain was merely an afferent nerve impulse signaling tissue deformation; it was data, not a command to stop. She conducted an instantaneous clinical self-triage: right eighth and ninth ribs bruised or hairline-fractured, limiting thoracic expansion to roughly sixty percent of vital capacity; left zygomatic arch bruised with superficial cutaneous laceration, bleeding controlled by clotting factor; cervical vertebrae stable; bilateral quadriceps and hamstrings intact.

She was operational.

She planted her feet twenty-two inches apart on the red stone, her weight balanced, her shoulders dropping into the coiled, lethal stance Mac had taught her.

With her left hand, she wiped the blood from her left eye, smearing the crimson across her high cheekbone like war paint. 

Down in the ravine, the five Sussex men had cleared the burning timber of the center bent. One of them—a wiry, dark-bearded tracker wearing a wolf-skin cap and carrying a sharpened six-foot logging pike—scrambled up the shale embankment twenty yards ahead of his companions, his leather boots scrabbling for purchase in the loose red gravel.

"There she is!" the tracker snarled, his yellowed teeth bared. "On the ledge! Drop the stick, girl, or I'll pin you to the sandstone!"

He lunged up the slope with explosive, desperate speed, driving the steel pike point straight toward her diaphragm.

Maya didn't back up. Backing up on a forty-five-degree shale scree was mechanical suicide; the coefficient of restitution on loose gravel was nearly zero, guaranteeing a slip. 

Instead, she stepped *forward* and *down*.

As the logging pike hissed past her ribs, Maya brought the white ash staff down across the pike's hickory haft with a violent downward lever stroke. The iron ferrule struck the wood with three hundred foot-pounds of shearing force, deflecting the iron point into the shale bed.

The 63 HRC clay-tempered edge whispered across the inside of his right elbow joint, severing the biceps tendon and the median nerve with zero resistance.

The logging pike dropped from his lifeless fingers into the scree. The tracker fell to his knees with a choked, bubbling scream, clutching his paralyzed forearm against his ribs.

With a swift, downward kick of her cleated boot, Maya stamped his left ankle sideways into a rock crevice, snapping the lateral malleolus of the fibula with a sickening pop that ensured he would walk no trails for six months.

From the burning wreckage forty yards below, the crack of a .303 British rifle shattered the canyon air.

*SPANG!*

A full-metal-jacketed rifle bullet struck the sandstone face three inches above Maya's right ear, spraying her cheek with stinging rock chips and leaving a bright lead smear on the ancient red stone.

*DOWN! DOWN! MORE FIRE-STICKS!* Scar screamed into her mind.

The squirrel didn't freeze. Leaping from her shoulder to the weathered root of an overhanging red spruce, Scar scampered three feet up the bank, caught a five-pound, frost-rotted granite cobble that was balanced on the edge of the drainage lip, and shoved it with all the leverage of his hind legs.

The heavy stone tumbled down the slope, dislodging a rolling avalanche of loose red shale and football-sized boulders that crashed directly into the creek bed path where the remaining four raiders were advancing.

*HAZARD DECOY DEPLOYED!* Scar chittered triumphantly, scurrying back down the spruce trunk and diving headfirst into Maya's unbuttoned shirt pocket, his cold nose pressed firmly against her sternum. *THE PASSAGE IS CHOKED! RUN, RUNNER! SQUIRREL DEMANDS IMMEDIATE STRATEGIC RETREAT!*

"With pleasure, partner," Maya whispered through clenched teeth.

"I am Maya," she whispered, her voice tightening into cold, tempered steel. "I am the Engineer of the Red Mud."

*WE FIGHT,* the squirrel chittered, his mental voice locking with hers in pure, unyielding resolve. *WE CRACK SKULLS. WE WALK HOME TO THE MOUNTAIN-WALKER.*

Behind her lay seven dead corpses, a shattered bridge, and a crippled raider patrol. Ahead lay fifteen miles of hostile timber, roaming packs of the unburied, and Thorne’s hunting parties sweeping the rail bed.

Her speeder was gone. Her body was bruised and bleeding. Her ribs burned with every breath.

She was bloodied. She was battered.

She was undefeated.

### Part VI: The Beacon on the Headland

Fifteen miles to the south, high atop the red sandstone headland overlooking the bends of the Petitcodiac, Mac stood on the circular iron gallery of the lighthouse watchroom.

The Fundy tide was on the turn. Down on the vast mudflats, forty feet below the cliff lip, sixteen billion tons of chocolate brine had begun its long, thunderous retreat toward Shepody Bay, leaving thirty square miles of glistening silt steaming in the cold autumn air. The red river lay bare, exposed like the open chest of an ancient titan, its mud veins bleeding iron into the sea.

Mac’s hands rested on the cold brass railing of the lantern deck.

His eyes were closed. He was not looking with physical sight. His consciousness—deep, slow, and anchored into the ancient roots of the Mary's Point granite—was listening to the marrow of the earth.

Ten miles beneath the spruce ridges, where the continental crust of Avalonia met the ancient Appalachian bedrock, a faint, sharp percussion vibrated through the stone. It was not the rhythmic, heavy thump of a falling red spruce or the deep, groaning shudder of an ice jam in the river. It was the sharp, unnatural slap of synthetic nitrogen: *CIL Forcite 40% dynamite.*

Mac opened his eyes.

In the quiet, weathered depths of his pupils, the granite gray dissolved. A cold, piercing, incandescent flare of **electric azure blue** ignited in his irises—the subterranean fire of *Mntu* surging from the bedrock through the soles of his boots into his chest.

Beside his left thigh, Nipn stepped to the edge of the iron gallery, his silver-gray ruff bristling against the salt gale. The wolf lifted his black nose to the north wind, his ears pinning back, his amber eyes narrowing into slits.

*The iron bridge broke,* Nipn projected, his mental voice flat, heavy, and lethal. *Fire on the river. Men with guns are yelling in the marsh.*

"I felt it," Mac said, his baritone dropping an octave into that subterranean register that silenced birds in the trees. "Thirty miles of air, and the stone still carries the bruise."

*The runner?* the wolf asked, his muscles bunching along his spine. *Does she breathe?*

Mac reached down with his right hand, pressing his open shovel-blade palm flat against the exterior granite blocks of the lighthouse tower. He felt the cold quartz crystals in the stone, the lime mortar he had mixed with his own hands, and the distant, fragile thread of biological vitality moving through the black spruce forest fifteen miles away.

He felt her heartbeat: one hundred and fifty beats a minute, rhythmic, strained, fighting against thoracic pressure, but unyielding. He felt the sharp, tiny spark of an eight-ounce rodent shivering against her sternum, spitting defiance at the sky.

A slow, terrifyingly calm breath passed through Mac’s nostrils, drawing the salt wind deep into his lungs. The azure fire in his irises stabilized, burning with the steady, blinding brilliance of an arc welder deep underwater.

"She breathes," Mac said softly. "Her ribs are bruised, her skin is torn, and her bridge is gone. But her spine is whole."

He turned from the iron railing, stepping back into the warm, dry air of the upper watchroom.

He walked to the massive iron brazier set into the stone hearth of the beacon deck. Over the dry birch kindling, he laid three split logs of seasoned white cedar and a heavy slab of fat-pine heartwood dripping with amber pitch. He struck no flint. He simply closed his calloused fingers around the pitch-soaked pine, his minor energy manipulation channeling the vital friction of *Mntu* into the wood until the amber rosin hissed, smoked, and burst into a tall, brilliant golden plume of flame.

The light flared through the four-foot Fresnel lens, casting a blinding, thirty-mile spear of clean golden light straight north across the spruce ridges toward Salisbury.

"The gate stays open, Nipn," Mac commanded, his voice ringing like an iron bell through the stone spiral stairwell. "Feed the fire. Lay the salt bandages by the hearth. And tell Ursus to patrol the mud flats at the low-water weir."

*And you, Kwitn?* the wolf transmitted, turning his golden gaze toward the ancient man. *Will you walk the rail to meet her?*

Mac walked to the heavy maple workbench at the center of the watchroom. He picked up an oiled rag and a six-inch leather strop, his hands calm, relaxed, and absolute.

"No," Mac said softly, his azure gaze fixed on the northern horizon. "Maya is not a child running to her father. She is an engineer of the red river, and she is walking home on her own two feet. We hold the fortress. We keep the beacon burning. And when she breaks through the timber..."

His hand closed into a fist, the sound of his knuckles popping like dry pine kindling.

"...whatever is following her into the mud belongs to the tide."
