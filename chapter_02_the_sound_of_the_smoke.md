# Chapter 2: The Geometry of Latches

---

### Part I: The Philosophy of Hardware and the Trunnion Gate

A gate that does not close itself under its own weight is not a defense; it is merely an open invitation to be eaten in your sleep.

Modern suburban people think of a door as a decorative rectangle of pressed sawdust and synthetic glue, hung from a hollow pine casing by two-dollar hinges stamped from soft zinc sheet in a factory outside Shenzhen. You turn a polished brass knob that operates a three-eighths-inch pot-metal latch, you hear a faint, hollow *click*, and you go to bed under the warm, comforting delusion that you have locked the chaotic world outside your living room with three ounces of low-grade metallurgy.

A man who works with real timber in the Maritime provinces knows that metal hardware is a liability that begins to rot the second you drive the screws.

In the tidal basin of the Petkotkweyak, where the cold, dense ocean fog rolls up the Bay of Fundy every evening like a wet wool blanket, commercial steel hardware has a lifespan measured in months. The salt in the air eats the zinc plating; the red clay mud grinds into the hinge barrels like valve-lapping compound; the frost heaves in November twist the frames until the cheap screws shear cleanly off at the shank. 

And far worse than any of that: when a dry steel hinge swings on a calm July morning, it produces a high-pitched, metallic *screeech-clack* that cuts through a quiet spruce forest for three-quarters of a mile, announcing to every living predator and every wandering dead corpse exactly where a human hand has just touched wood.

On the morning of Day Two, while the greasy black smoke from the jackknifed milk tanker on Wheeler Boulevard was still rising in a lazy, sooty smudge across the southern horizon, I went to work on the perimeter boundary.

---

The Creek Camp was blessed with natural terrain that would have made a Roman siege engineer nod in quiet appreciation. 

To the north, the ground rose in an almost vertical, forty-foot wall of fractured gray granite ledge, capped with dense, impenetrable thickets of mature scrub spruce and wild mountain ash. To the east and west, two steep drainage ravines, carved thirty feet deep into the red sandstone bedrock by ancient post-glacial runoff, boxed the hollow in with natural moats of loose shale and wet clay.

The only level approach into the hollow was from the south: a four-rod gap of flat, mossy ground where an abandoned 1940s colonial logging spur met the bank of the freshwater brook. 

If anything was going to walk into my clearing on two legs—or on four—it was going to come through that seventy-foot corridor between the granite ledges.

I spent four hours with the five-foot crosscut saw and the 2.5-pound Hudson Bay felling axe, taking down two straight, twelve-inch red spruces on the western ridge. 

I peeled the rough brown bark with the crooked knife, working the blades until the green sapwood was clean, then used the heavy iron spud bar to dig two post holes four feet deep into the hardpan clay at the narrowest pinch-point of the trail. 

I did not set the posts directly into the dirt. Soil holds stagnant moisture against the end-grain, promoting white rot fungi that will turn an eight-inch timber into wet cardboard in five seasons. 

I hauled six pack baskets of fist-sized, water-worn granite cobblestones up from the creek bed, dumped eight inches of gravel into the bottom of each hole to act as a permanent drainage sump, charred the lower four feet of the spruce posts over a pit fire until the wood was coated in a sterile, rot-proof crust of elemental carbon, and tamped the posts into the ground with crushed shale until they stood as rigid as bridge piers.

Between those two immovable spruce pillars, I hung a seven-foot-tall, eight-foot-wide gate framed entirely from hand-split northern white cedar.

---

The construction was pure L'nu and Acadian pastoral engineering: **the trunnion post.**

I did not use an ounce of metal.

The vertical heel of the gate—the heavy upright timber that carried the entire structural load—was a five-inch white ash sapling that I had seasoned in the shade of my shed for eighteen months. I whittled the bottom ten inches of the ash post into a smooth, hemispherical pintle, four inches in diameter, and fire-hardened the wood over glowing hardwood coals until the outer grain was dense as horn.

For the bottom bearing, I took an eight-inch block of rock maple—the hardest, densest hardwood in the Acadian forest—and used a two-inch gouge chisel to carve a polished, cup-shaped socket into the center of the grain. 

I sank the maple cup into the granite foundation between the gate posts and filled the depression with two tablespoons of rendered black bear fat mixed with powdered graphite.

When the ash pintle dropped into the greased maple socket, the entire seven-foot gate floated on a film of animal lipid and carbon. 

For the upper hinge, I took a heavy, curved yoke of green white oak, mortised it deeply into the main spruce post, and locked the top of the ash trunnion through the yoke with a one-inch dry birch dowel, lubricated with tallow.

When you leaned against the gate with your shoulder, the seven-foot cedar barrier swung open on that greased wooden pivot in **absolute, dead, terrifying silence.**

Not a squeak. Not a groan. Not a scrape. 

Just the faint, oily, whispering sigh of polished white ash rotating in seasoned maple.

---

And then came the geometry.

When I pegged the upper oak yoke into the spruce post, I did not set it plumb. 

I offset the top bearing two inches back toward the camp, tilting the entire vertical axis of the gate inward at a three-degree angle.

Because of that subtle, three-degree lean, gravity was continuously pulling the center of mass of the heavy cedar gate toward the closed position. 

You could shove the gate wide open with both hands while hauling an eighty-pound pack of split firewood, step through the threshold into the yard, and the moment your fingers left the timber, the gate would begin its smooth, silent, accelerating swing back toward the post under the invisible, constant pull of the earth’s core.

The latch was a piece of pure mechanical poetry: **the counter-weighted drop-bar.**

I carved a four-inch-wide, two-inch-thick bar of seasoned sugar maple, five feet long, pivoting on a half-inch birch trunnion pin mortised into the center horizontal rail of the gate. 

To the short, trailing tail of the bar, behind the pivot, I lashed a smooth, five-pound oval river stone using four wraps of wet rawhide soaked in pine pitch. 

The weight of the stone kept the long, beveled nose of the drop-bar permanently forced downward. 

When the gate swung shut under its gravity lean, the angled nose of the maple bar struck the heavy oak strike-plate on the receiving post. The ramp forced the bar up two inches into a vertical guide slot, clearing the stop, and the moment the gate seated against the heavy timber jamb, the five-pound stone dropped the bar down into a two-inch-deep locking mortise with a single, solid, muffled, bone-jarring *clack*.

It locked with the finality of a bank vault.

To open the gate from the inside, you reached down and lifted a small wooden finger lever that raised the counterweight.

From the outside, there was nothing. 

No handle. No iron ring. No latch string trailing through a hole in the wood. 

The exterior face of the gate was a seamless, vertical palisade of split-cedar pickets, seven feet high, their tops sharpened into thirty-degree chisel points, with zero gaps between the boards and zero finger-holds for a climbing hand.

A human being with an axe and an hour of quiet time could eventually chop through the cedar. A man with a knife who knew the secrets of L'nu woodcraft could search the brush, find the concealed release toggle buried in the hemlock bark six feet away, and pull the hidden bank line.

A dead man with no brain, two milky eyes, and two hundred pounds of rotting mass would simply stumble out of the ferns, hit the cedar palisade with his gray forehead, and find himself standing in front of an immovable wooden cliff that did not possess a single moving part his dead fingers could comprehend.

"Physics," I muttered, running my thumb over the polished maple strike-plate. "Gravity doesn't run out of batteries, and it never forgets to drop the latch behind you."

### Part II: The Death of the Municipal Tap and the Cedar Flume

By four o'clock in the morning on Day Four, the modern world gave its final, pathetic gasp in the corner of my cabin.

I had left the quarter-turn brass ball-valve cracked a quarter-inch above the galvanized wash sink before I blew out the candle on the workbench. 

I was lying on my pine bunk, half-awake in the predawn twilight, listening to the first chorus of hermit thrushes singing in the spruce tops, when the pipe began to die.

It was an ugly sound: a dry, hollow, metallic rattle that traveled down the black polyethylene line from the ridge like a marble rattling inside a copper tube. 

The faucet coughed twice—two violent, spitting bursts of brown, rust-heavy water that splashed against the zinc bucket—followed by a long, wheezing, high-pitched whistle of escaping air as the pressure in the municipal main went to absolute zero, and then a faint, wet *shuck* as the check valve seated for the last time.

The gravity water towers on the high ridges above the town were dry.

---

I had read the municipal infrastructure assessments when the city council floated a forty-million-dollar bond to overhaul the Turtle Creek reservoir system six years ago.

A gravity water system is an extraordinary piece of 19th-century engineering: you pump fifty million gallons of water up into massive elevated steel tanks on the highest hills using electric multi-stage turbine pumps, and the weight of the water creates sixty pounds per square inch of hydraulic pressure in every kitchen sink and fire hydrant in the valley without needing a generator at every street corner.

It is brilliant, silent, and completely reliable—for exactly seventy-two hours.

The moment the automated power grid trips at the coal-fired generating station in Belledune or the hydro substations on the Saint John River, the electric pumps stop feeding the reservoir. 

For three days, thirty thousand households in town turn their taps on in pure, panicked frenzy: filling five-gallon plastic buckets, filling bathtubs, filling washing machines, filling plastic garbage cans, while down in the commercial districts, shattered hydrants and ruptured sprinkler systems spew thousands of gallons a minute into the storm sewers.

By the ninety-sixth hour, sixty million gallons has run out into the ocean. The air rushes into the empty mains, the bacterial biofilms on the inner walls of the iron pipes dry and flake into toxic powder, and every chrome faucet in the city becomes nothing more than a metal straw connected to a dry iron tomb.

From this morning forward, anyone remaining down in the streets of Petkotkweyak who wanted a drink of water was going to have to take a bucket down to the toxic, silt-choked river mud, scoop up brown brine, and figure out how to boil it without an electric stove before giardia and cryptosporidium turned their bowels to water.

I did not touch the brass handle. 

I took a ten-inch crescent wrench from my tool chest, unthreaded the modern plastic hose from the back of the sink, carried the coil out behind the woodpile, and tossed it into the scrap heap.

Municipal plumbing was a temporary illusion, a thirty-year parenthesis in the ten-thousand-year history of human hydration.

I picked up my heavy splitting froe, the two-inch gouge chisel, the rock-maple beetle mallet, and walked eighty yards up the northern ridge behind the camp.

---

The mountain spring was located at the base of a sheer, twenty-foot outcrop of fractured gray granite, hidden beneath an ancient canopy of yellow birch and sugar maple.

The water did not come from surface runoff. It came from a deep, subterranean aquifer trapped in the porous sandstone layers five hundred feet below the Albert County plateau, filtering through miles of quartz sand and mineral beds before hydrostatic pressure forced it bubbling out through a horizontal fracture in the rock face.

It emerged into a natural stone basin six feet across, surrounded by wild watercress, sweet coltsfoot, and dense green cushions of sphagnum moss. 

The water was four degrees Celsius—so cold that if you plunged your forearm into the pool to the elbow, your bone ached with an icy throb within ten seconds. It was clear as optical glass, smelling faintly of clean slate, iron, and living moss, completely untouched by whatever bacterial horrors were currently multiplying in the stagnant pipes down in the valley.

I spent six hours felling three straight, eight-inch northern white cedars along the edge of the north bog.

Cedar is the wood of life for watercraft and waterworks. Its heartwood contains natural thujaplicins—powerful, organic anti-fungal compounds that prevent rot, repel insects, and prevent bacterial slime from taking root on the grain.

Using the splitting froe and a heavy wooden wedge, I halved the sixteen-foot logs down their center pith, splitting the timber along the straight radial grain into six twelve-foot half-rounds.

Then came the gouge work.

I straddled the split logs on the forest floor, using the curved two-inch gouge chisel and the maple mallet to hollow out the inner heartwood, carving a smooth, U-shaped flume three inches wide and two inches deep down the entire length of each timber. 

I smoothed the inside of the channel with the crooked knife, then took a small, hand-held torch of burning white birchbark and carefully charred the inner surface of the troughs until the wood was lined with a paper-thin, sterile, glass-like layer of black carbon.

Carbon is nature’s ultimate filtration medium: it absorbs organic odors, neutralizes trace tannins, and prevents the wood from imparting any bitter cedar taste to the drinking water.

I laid the six troughs end-to-end on notched red spruce crotches driven deep into the hillside, maintaining a steady, calculated drop of two inches for every ten feet of horizontal run.

Where the troughs overlapped, I carved matching mortise-and-tenon lap joints, sealing the seams with a flexible, waterproof mastic made of boiled white pine pitch, rendered bear tallow, and finely sifted wood ash, lashed tight with two-inch bands of wet deer rawhide that contracted like iron clamps as they cured in the sun.

By two o'clock in the afternoon, the flume was operational.

A clean, thumb-thick, crystalline stream of four-degree mountain water was pouring off the final cedar spout beside my kitchen porch, arching twelve inches through the clean air before dropping with a steady, rhythmic, musical splash directly into a fifty-five-gallon charred white-oak barrel resting on a flat granite hearthstone beside the door.

When the barrel filled to the fifty-gallon mark, a carved overflow notch near the top rim guided the excess stream into a secondary cedar trough that carried the cold runoff down the slope to irrigate the *Three Sisters* garden beds and keep the live-fish cleaning tank full of cold, running water.

Ten gallons a minute. Six hundred gallons an hour. Fourteen thousand four hundred gallons a day of pure, living, ice-cold water that would never stop flowing as long as the rain fell on the hills of Mi'kma'ki.

I took an enameled tin mug from the porch rail, held it under the cedar spout until the tin was frosted with cold condensation, brought it to my mouth, and drank.

It tasted of cold stone, sweet spruce needles, and living earth.

"Day four," I whispered to the hemlocks, wiping my chin with the back of my hand. "Water secured. Forever."

### Part III: The Architecture of the Four Living Trails

A modern suburban survivalist thinks exclusively in terms of fortifications: he wants concrete bunkers, barbed wire, security cameras, sandbags, and interlocking fields of fire. 

He wants to turn his house into a miniature military fort, lock himself in the center of the room with an assault rifle, and wait for the world to come to him.

An L'nu hunter knows that a fortress is just a stationary target waiting to be surrounded, starved out, or burned to the ground with three bottles of gasoline.

You cannot live inside a wooden box for twenty years. 

You must go down to the river mudflats to harvest the fish weirs; you must climb the high granite bluff to read the weather and watch the horizon from the stone lighthouse; you must walk the northern ridges to fell seasoned firewood; and you must forage the bogs for sweetgrass, sweet flag root, and wild medicinal chaga.

If every time you leave your camp you take the easiest route through the woods, you crush the green moss, you break the hazel twigs at knee height, you leave scuff marks on the exposed roots, and you lay down a continuous, screaming scent trail of human sweat, leather oil, and woodsmoke that an infected corpse—or a desperate man with a hunting rifle—can track straight back to your kitchen porch like a painted highway.

During the first week, while the town six miles away was tearing itself apart, I laid out and engineered the **Four Secure Living Corridors**:

```
                              [ THE OLD STONE LIGHTHOUSE ]
                                           ▲
                                           │ (The Scarp Line: 400 yds)
                                           │
[ THE SPRING & WOODLOT ] ◄───────── [ THE CREEK CAMP ] ─────────► [ THE WEIR CORRIDOR ]
    (Ridge Run: 80 yds)                    │                           (Ravine Bed: 300 yds)
                                           ▼
                                 [ THE DOBSON BYPASS ]
                                  (Covered Ditch: 1 km)
```

---

#### 1. The Scarp Line (Camp to Stone Light — 400 Yards)
* **The Route:** Climbs the eastern sandstone bluff through a narrow, thirty-degree geological fracture in the rock, concealed beneath the low, hanging boughs of stunted black spruce and wild mountain ash.
* **The Engineering:** I did not cut timber steps into the slope, which would expose fresh, bright yellow sapwood to the horizon. I spent two days prying flat slabs of gray granite from the creek bed, carrying them up the bluff in pack baskets, and setting them into the turf along the fracture line, burying their edges beneath thick clumps of living feather moss and polypody ferns.
* **The Tactical Sightline:** A person standing on the river mudflats four hundred yards below looking up at the headland sees nothing but unbroken gray stone, lichen, and evergreen branches. A man walking the scarp line moves completely beneath the rock lip, his silhouette hidden four inches below the skyline.

#### 2. The Weir Corridor (Camp to River Flats — 300 Yards)
* **The Route:** Follows the sunken gravel bed of the freshwater brook where the stream has carved a six-foot-deep trench through the soft red clay of the valley floor.
* **The Discipline:** You never walk along the high grassy bank where your head and shoulders are framed against the sky. You walk directly in the running water of the stream bed, your moose-hide moccasins submerged in three inches of gravel and cold current.
* **The Tactical Benefit:** Running water is the absolute eraser of human presence. It leaves zero footprints, crushes zero vegetation, and washes every molecule of human scent down into the tidal basin in three minutes. If an infected wanderer crosses the creek trail thirty seconds after you pass, there is zero scent of wool, tobacco, or sweat for its dead olfactory nerves to register.

#### 3. The Ridge Run (Camp to North Woodlot — 80 Yards)
* **The Route:** Follows the twenty-foot contour line along the shadowed north slope, staying entirely within the deep understory of the old-growth hemlock stand.
* **The Early Warning Trip-Alarms:** At three natural choke points where the trail squeezes between ancient red spruce blowdowns, I installed **Gravity Drop-Stone Alarms:**
  * A single, hair-thin strand of braided #18 bank line, dyed black with walnut husk juice, stretched four inches above the spruce needles across the game run.
  * The line was tied off to a notched trigger sear carved from dry lilac wood, holding an eight-pound granite cobblestone suspended inside a hollow, standing red spruce stump.
  * If a wandering foot snapped or tripped the line, the sear released, and the eight-pound stone dropped six inches onto a dry slab of sugar maple seated in the bottom of the hollow wood.
  * It produced a deep, muffled, subterranean *thump* that vibrated through the earth for forty yards—audible to a man standing on the cabin porch, but producing zero acoustic echo in the open forest to attract outside attention.

#### 4. The Dobson Bypass (Camp to Wilderness Arteries — 1 km)
* **The Route:** Connects the southern perimeter of the camp to the main 58-kilometer Dobson Trail corridor through an ancient, dry, sunken drainage ditch that had been overgrown with wild hazel, elderberry, and ostrich ferns for fifty years.
* **The Living Canopy:** I took my crooked knife and spent two afternoons bending the living tops of the hazel and alder saplings across the top of the trench, weaving their flexible branches into an overhead arbor five feet above the ground, lashed with split spruce roots.
* **The Tactical Reality:** The living bushes continued to grow, leaf out, and flower. A surveillance drone flying fifty feet above the trees would see nothing but unbroken green canopy. A person walking the ridge trail would pass within six feet of the ditch and see nothing but an impenetrable wall of wild brush, while beneath their feet, a man carrying sixty pounds of gear could walk in full shade from the camp to the southern wilderness without ever stepping into the daylight.

### Part IV: The North Slope Root Cellar

By Day Six, the daytime temperature in the river valley had climbed to twenty-eight degrees Celsius in the shade.

Inside the unfinished cabin, the air under the cedar rafters was hot, dry, and smelling of resin. Down at the weirs, I had sixty pounds of fresh flounder fillets and thirty pounds of wild spring tubers that needed to stay below eight degrees Celsius if they were going to provide calories instead of breeding deadly botulism.

If you do not possess an electrical grid, you do not mourn the absence of a refrigerator; you look at the topography and you dig into the cold north face of the earth.

The northern slope behind the camp was a geological blessing: a steep, forty-foot bank of blue clay and granite shale that sat in the permanent shadow of the ancient hemlocks, never receiving a single ray of direct sunlight from the middle of May until the end of October. The soil two feet below the leaf litter was chilled by subterranean groundwater draining from the bedrock, maintaining a constant temperature between four and six degrees Celsius through the hottest days of summer.

I spent three days of relentless, sweat-soaked labor with the spud bar, the felling axe, and an ash-wood hand shovel, excavating a room six feet wide, eight feet long, and five feet deep directly into the hillside.

I did not leave the walls as raw dirt, which would crumble under autumn rains and harbor burrowing rodents.

I lined the excavation with a dry-stacked masonry wall of flat granite fieldstones, hauling four hundred pounds of rock from the creek bed in pack baskets. I chinked the stone seams with a stiff, waterproof mortar made of sticky blue river clay, dried sphagnum moss, and finely sifted wood ash from the hearth.

For the floor, I laid down six inches of washed river gravel to allow any seepage to drain instantly into the subsoil, capped with a tight, smooth floor of hand-split northern white cedar planks, charred on their undersides to prevent soil mold.

The roof was engineered to support the weight of the mountain:
* Four heavy, six-inch rock-maple joists laid across the granite walls.
* A solid decking of two-inch rough-sawn white pine boards.
* Two overlapping layers of heavy, waterproof paper-birch bark sheeting peeled from standing deadfalls.
* A three-foot cap of damp, living forest soil, planted with wild ferns and moss so the entire roof looked like an undisturbed forest knoll.

The entrance was a horizontal, double-walled hatch made of two-inch white pine planks, counter-weighted with a lead-weighted ash lever arm and insulated with four inches of dried sheep’s wool packed between the inner and outer boards.

When you pulled the wooden lever and stepped down the three granite steps into the cellar, the temperature dropped twenty degrees in two paces. 

The air smelled of cold stone, clean cedar oil, damp earth, and dried sweetgrass.

Along the cold north wall, I constructed three tiers of slatted cedar shelving:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ TOP SHELF (8°C): Dried Chaga, Sweetgrass, Labrador Tea, Heirloom Seeds  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ MIDDLE SHELF (6°C): Wild Groundnuts (Apios), Leek Bulbs, Dried Fiddleheads│
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ BOTTOM SHELF (4°C): Salted Flounder Crocks, Smoked Shad, Rendered Lard │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

In the deepest, coldest corner of the room, elevated six inches off the cedar floor on dry pine blocks, I placed my three sealed, heavy-duty plastic totes containing my physical book collection: two hundred volumes of literature, dictionaries, historical military treatises, and handcraft manuals, protected from the damp maritime air by three pounds of bone-dry charcoal chunks tied in wool socks to absorb any stray humidity.

"Cellar finished," I whispered, pushing the heavy pine hatch down until the wool gasket compressed with a soft, airtight *whump*. "Let the sun bake the valley all it wants."

### Part V: The Great Rot of Petkotkweyak

On the morning of Day Seven, the weather did what the weather always does in southern New Brunswick when a summer maritime high-pressure system stalls over the Bay of Fundy: the wind backed around from the clean northwest to the dead, heavy south.

I was standing forty feet above the ground on the lantern gallery of the Old Stone Light, my elbows resting on the rusted iron parapet, my 25x brass field spyglass pulled to full extension.

The wind hit my face at nine in the morning.

It was not the smell of salt water, and it was not the clean, resinous scent of balsam fir needles.

It was **The Great Rot.**

It was a thick, sweet, heavy, oily stench that coated the roof of your mouth like lard the moment you drew breath through your teeth. It smelled like ten thousand commercial meat lockers filled with chicken parts, pork shoulders, and beef carcasses, left to stew under a sheet of clear plastic in a greenhouse for seven straight days.

Down in the town, every supermarket—the Sobeys on Main Street, the Atlantic Superstore on Regatta, the giant Costco warehouse near the highway—had become a biological weapon.

The power had been dead for six full days. The ambient temperature in the valley had averaged twenty-six degrees Celsius. 

Inside those massive, windowless, flat-roofed steel buildings, millions of pounds of vacuum-sealed meat, dairy, and produce had thawed to room temperature by Day Three. By Day Five, anaerobic clostridial bacteria had colonized the protein, generating massive volumes of methane, cadaverine, and putrescine gas that expanded the plastic packaging until the sealed trays exploded like miniature biological bombs, coating the walls and linoleum floors in six inches of putrid, gray-green fluid.

Through the 25x lens of the spyglass, I swept the commercial strip along Mountain Road.

The air above the flat metal roofs of the shopping centers was vibrating with a dense, shimmering, dark gray haze that looked like industrial smoke rising from a chimney. 

It was not smoke. It was **blowflies.**

Millions upon millions of metallic green bottle flies and black blowflies, breeding in the rotting meat and clouding into swarms so dense that they cast rolling shadows across the empty asphalt parking lots.

And moving through that shimmering, fly-choked haze, drawn from ten miles in every direction by the irresistible olfactory beacon of decaying flesh, were the dead.

They were coming out of the quiet residential suburbs, out of the office parks, out of the stalled vehicles on the bypass—hundreds of them, moving with that slow, heavy, relentless, dragging shamble, their gray heads tilted upward, converging on the broken glass doors of the supermarkets like iron filings being pulled toward an industrial electromagnet.

I watched a cluster of thirty infected squeeze through the smashed entrance of an Atlantic Superstore, tumbling over each other in the dark vestibule, clawing at the empty metal turnstiles in their frantic haste to reach the rotting meat counters in the back of the store.

"Look at that," I murmured, holding the spyglass steady against the granite sill. "The great modern supply chain. Even after their brains rot, they still go to the grocery store."

It was a horrific sight, but from a tactical standpoint, it was the greatest gift the ruined town could have given me.

As long as those hundred thousand pounds of commercial meat were liquefying in the shopping centers, every single infected corpse in the Siknikt valley would be pulled south toward the asphalt, away from the hemlock ridges, away from the ancient trail network, and away from my camp.

The colonial city was consuming itself in its own garbage.

I closed the brass spyglass with a soft, metallic click, slid the tube into its waxed leather field sleeve, and climbed down the iron spiral stairs into the clean, moss-scented air of the hollow.

### Part VI: The Solitary Fortress and the Quiet Night

By seven o'clock on the evening of Day Seven—the end of the first full week—the camp was running like a seasoned timber ship at sea.

The wattle garden fence was complete: a four-foot-high, tightly woven palisade of fresh alder, hazel, and split cedar stakes, surrounding the quarter-acre plot like a fortress of living green wood. Inside the perimeter, the little hills of rich river loam were flourishing: the broad, spade-shaped leaves of the flint corn had pushed three inches through the earth, with the tender green tendrils of the climbing beans just beginning to seek the stalks, while the wide leaves of the squash were expanding to shade the soil and keep the roots cool.

I sat on the three-legged chopping block in the center of the yard, sharpening my crooked knife on the Arkansas oil stone with small, rhythmic, circular strokes.

*Sshhk. Sshhk. Sshhk.*

Beside my right moccasin, the split-cedar gravity flume was splashing clean, four-degree mountain water into the oak wash barrel with a steady, musical cadence that filled the quiet hollow with the sound of living motion. 

The smoke from the cast-iron woodstove was rising straight up through the hemlocks, thin, blue, and completely invisible against the twilight sky, smelling sweet with the scent of burning birchbark and dry maple.

To the south, beyond the granite ridges, the distant valley was silent.

The frantic blaring of car horns on the highway had died two days ago as the last automotive lead-acid batteries drained down to zero. The sporadic handgun fire had sputtered out. The modern world had finished its initial, violent screaming and had settled into the heavy, rotting silence of an unburied graveyard.

I wiped the curved steel blade of the knife on my leather apron, held the razor edge up to the fading light to verify the burr, and slid the tool home into its belt sheath.

"Week one," I said to the empty clearing.

I stood up, picked up my six-foot ash bear-spear, and looked up at the gray headland where the Old Stone Light stood dark and solid against the salt sky.

"The water runs true," I told the quiet trees. "The gate drops under gravity. The cellar is four degrees. The food is swimming in the mud."

I rolled my shoulders, feeling the deep, clean, honest ache of seven days of hard timber framing in my lats, my forearms, and my thighs.

"Now," I said, turning toward the scarp trail, "it is time to climb the big stone chimney, tame the draft, and see what the rest of the world left behind."
